If global shipping stopped for a month, could your community keep
everyone fed? For most places the honest answer is “we donʼt know.”
Thatʼs not a failure. Itʼs the assignment.
What going right would look like
Picture  the  ordinary  version.  A  cook  loses  a  job  and  still  eats.  A
worker finishes a shorter shift and goes home to the same life, not a
smaller one. A caregiver takes help without having to prove thereʼs no
other choice. A grower gets paid fairly for food that goes to people
who canʼt pay for it.
Thatʼs  worth  doing  the  practical  work  for.  It  doesnʼt  need  every
machine to be perfect. It needs a machine breaking, a market crash-
ing, or a boss cutting hours to stop landing straight on somebodyʼs
ability to eat.
And itʼs bigger than nineteen households. The co-op matters because
its  members  learned  to  fix  a  plan  together  and  could  show  their
work. Its limits matter too: a valley full of successful founders would
still leave most of the world outside the fence.
Build the machine and the promise together
There are two jobs, and you have to do both. Get better at making the
food. And build a promise people can count on that the food will
reach  them.  Do  only  the  first  and  you  get  impressive  machines
behind a price somebody canʼt pay. Do only the second and you get a
right on paper that the kitchen canʼt keep.
Start with a food service that already exists. Sit down with the people
who use it and the people who run it, and find where it breaks: the
missed meal, the pickup point nobody can reach, the task someone
repeats forty times a day, the freezer thatʼs been waiting three weeks
for a part. The first fix might be a machine. It might be another paid
shift, a better supplier, or a bus route.
