Rung 1: Count whatʼs wasted and whoʼs hungry
What. Put two measures side by side, in public, for your town: how
much edible food gets thrown away, and how many households canʼt
count on eating. Keep their units and reporting periods visible. You
canʼt subtract households from pounds of food; you can investigate
how much suitable food could reach those households, when, and at
what cost.
Proof itʼs possible. California already makes large food businesses
count and recover. SB 1383 requires large grocers, distributors, and
food services to recover edible food that would otherwise be thrown
away, requires cities and counties to set up food recovery programs,
and sets a statewide target: by 2025, recover 20 percent of the edible
food that would otherwise go to landfill, “to feed people in need.” Cal-
Recycle, SB 1383 Food Recovery Nationally, the Department of Agri-
culture counts food insecurity every year: 13.7 percent of US house-
holds, 18.3 million of them, were food insecure at some point in 2024.
USDA ERS, Key Statistics
Who. Anyone with a library card and a spreadsheet can start with
published  food-bank  reports,  county  recovery  plans,  and  USDA
survey results. Ask local providers which records they can share as
totals. The national survey doesnʼt supply a reliable monthly estimate
for every town, and household records arenʼt public just because an
organization holds them.
How  youʼll  know. One  page,  updated  when  new  records  arrive:
pounds recovered, meals served, households still short. Label dates,
sources, missing groups, and estimated figures. A monthly update
can  report  progress  without  pretending  an  annual  estimate  was
measured again.
