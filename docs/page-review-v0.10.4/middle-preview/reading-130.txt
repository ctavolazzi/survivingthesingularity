John Maynard Keynes saw a version of this coming. In his 1930 essay
“Economic  Possibilities  for  our  Grandchildren,”  he  predicted  that
within a century technical progress could solve what he called the
economic  problem,  and  he  worried  out  loud  about  what  people
would  do  with  themselves  once  the  pressing  cares  of  subsistence
were  lifted,  having  been  trained  for  so  long  to  strive  rather  than
enjoy. The question he asked was how to live “wisely and agreeably
and  well.”  Keynes,  Economic  Possibilities  for  our  Grandchildren
(1930) Itʼs a much better problem than hunger. Itʼs still a problem.
And thereʼs a reason people in the middle of the squeeze canʼt think
about any of this. The economist Sendhil Mullainathan and the psy-
chologist  Eldar  Shafir  describe  how  scarcity  can  capture  attention
and narrow it: tunnel vision. In a 2013 study with Anandi Mani and
Jiaying Zhao, sugarcane farmers in India scored better on cognitive
tests  after  the  harvest,  when  money  was  in  hand,  than  before  it,
when  money  was  tight.  Mani  and  colleagues,  Poverty  Impedes
Cognitive Function Same people. Different financial circumstances.
The finding concerns those tests and conditions, not a fixed loss of
ability in every person short of money. Someone worried about next
weekʼs rent isnʼt failing to imagine a post-scarcity future. Their mind
is fully booked.
