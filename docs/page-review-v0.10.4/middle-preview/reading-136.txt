Someone still has to tend the crop, fix the equipment, cook the meal,
and show up when a delivery fails. The answer to that work is decent
pay  and  conditions,  good  tools,  training  where  itʼs  needed,  and
enough people to share the load. A liberation plan that runs on an
exhausted kitchen crew has lost the plot.
Rosaʼs  meal  makes  the  point  small  enough  to  see.  She  didnʼt  owe
Curtis agreement, labor, or gratitude in exchange for dinner. Curtisʼs
driving still took time. The kitchen still needed funding. One personʼs
unconditional access and another personʼs working conditions have
to be planned together.
A voice before the decision
Nobody should first meet a new system as an instruction to train
their  replacement.  Ask  about  the  change  while  the  plan  can  still
change. What happens to hours, pay, safety, and discretion? Who gets
the time the machine saves? What support is already funded if a job
disappears? A meeting isnʼt enough just because everyone got invited.
The people carrying the cost need a way to change the terms, and a
way to hold someone to them afterward.
People can welcome food security and still object to surveillance or a
dangerous  deployment.  Keep  those  questions  open.  My  argument
doesnʼt need every complaint about AI to be a complaint about eco-
nomics.  It  needs  us  to  hear  the  economic  complaint  when  a
frightened person is making one.
For  the  next  conversation,  ask  Dennyʼs  question:  where  does  the
person  stand  while  the  transition  happens?  Keep  asking  until  the
answer names something they can actually use.
