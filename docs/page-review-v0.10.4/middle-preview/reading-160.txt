And keep a door in every circle. Knowing everyoneʼs name helps you
notice whoʼs missing; it can also make it easy to decide an outsider
doesnʼt count. Nineteen households are a beginning. Humanity is the
ambition.
Start with three
Find two other households. Donʼt try to convert fifty people. Sit
down with two, lay your skills on the table, and map what youʼre
collectively short on.
Share one tool first. Not your living space. A tool: a good ladder, a
tiller, a shared offline library on an old laptop, a chest freezer for
bulk food. Let the benefit prove the idea.
Write down the arrangement. Start with one page in plain
language: how the shared thing gets maintained, what happens
when someone canʼt do their share, how new people join. And one
line that doesnʼt change: nobodyʼs meal depends on their share.
Put a different assumption to work
At the next meeting about a food project, write the promise in one
sentence: a person can get adequate food without money or a work
requirement.  Then  ask  where  the  actual  arrangement  falls  short.
Who canʼt reach the pickup? Who canʼt cook whatʼs offered? Whoʼs
afraid of losing access if they complain? Pick one barrier the group
can remove, name whoʼll do it, and set a date to check. Thatʼs how a
change of mind becomes a change in somebodyʼs afternoon.
