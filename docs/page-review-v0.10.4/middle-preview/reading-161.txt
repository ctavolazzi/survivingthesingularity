Precedent P-11: Torches of Freedom (New
York, 1929)
In 1929 it was still widely taboo for a woman to smoke in public,
which  meant  a  lot  of  potential  customers  for  American  cigarettes
were  out  of  reach.  So  the  American  Tobacco  Company  turned  to
Edward Bernays, Sigmund Freudʼs nephew and a pioneer of public
relations, to pick the lock.
Bernays  didnʼt  buy  ads  arguing  that  women  should  smoke.  He
recruited young women to walk in New Yorkʼs Easter Sunday parade
and light cigarettes in view of the press, and he gave the reporters a
phrase: “torches of freedom.” A real aspiration of the era, womenʼs
emancipation, got retooled into a sales pitch.  Library of Congress,
Easter  Sunday  photographs The  photographs  survive:  Nancy  Hale
Hardin beside her husband, Edith Lee walking a dog on Fifth Avenue,
March 31, 1929, both holding cigarettes.
Bernays had already published his method the year before. In Propa-
ganda he imagined selling pianos without selling pianos: make the
music  room  fashionable,  get  decorators  and  performers  talking
about  it,  persuade  architects  to  design  it  in,  and  the  buyer  would
experience the purchase as completing a life they already wanted.
Bernays, Propaganda, chapter IV
Hereʼs  the  part  the  legend  skips.  The  historian  Vanessa  Murphree
went  through  more  than  250  articles,  including  the  clippings  in
Bernaysʼs own scrapbooks, and found a press that was less gullible
and less uniformly enthusiastic than the story says; some editors saw
the stunt for what it was. Murphree, Torches of Freedom research A
thick scrapbook isnʼt a count of converted minds. The myth of the all-
powerful manipulator asks you to give up the one thing that beats
him: your capacity to notice.
