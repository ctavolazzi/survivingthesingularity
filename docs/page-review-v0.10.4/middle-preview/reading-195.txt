Rule 2: Pivot to the practical. Once theyʼre reading, move quickly
from the story to the steps: the parts list, the wiring diagram, the
schedule, the phone number of the food pantry. The reader came for
the person and stays for the thing they can use.
Rule 3: Point off the platform. Donʼt end on “like and subscribe.” End
with something physical: “Write this on an index card. Put the phone
down. Find two neighbors whoʼll help you try it this weekend.” The
feed is a temporary delivery truck, not the destination.
The analog blind spot
A platform can log every click that crosses its servers. It can predict
your next purchase with unsettling accuracy. But it has a big blind
spot:  the  analog  commons.  A  conversation  without  a  recording
device, a hand-drawn plan that stays on paper, a morning traded for a
box of tomatoes: those donʼt automatically create a platform record.
Photograph them, record them, or put the details in an app, and that
boundary moves. (In the United States, barter generally creates tax-
able income at the fair market value received; the IRS distinguishes
some informal, noncommercial exchanges of similar services.  IRS,
Taxable and Nontaxable Income Outside a feed isnʼt outside the law.)
Thatʼs the real play: use their expensive digital network as a tempo-
rary, free distribution channel to build durable physical ones. Their
silicon, our stone.
Make the handoff small
If the thing you made helps, leave behind something another person
can actually use: a corrected pickup schedule, a short guide, a labeled
record, a contact who agreed to be contacted. If it fails, write down
why and stop spending on it until thereʼs a reason to try again. Donʼt
create a project on someone elseʼs behalf that they now have to main-
tain.
