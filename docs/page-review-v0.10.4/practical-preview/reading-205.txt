A public meal, and an argument about it

On May 28, 1941, the House of Commons discussed a practical obsta-
cle to feeding people during wartime: some local authorities had not
prepared communal feeding centers. The Ministry of Food had
assigned officers to help establish British Restaurants. But encour-
agement was one thing and authority another. When an MP asked
whether the ministry could establish a restaurant where a council
failed to act, its parliamentary secretary could not give a definite
answer.   Even   an   urgent   national   purpose   needed   someone
empowered to carry it out locally. Hansard, May 28, 1941
The restaurant objection was there too. On October 1, Sir Percy Hurd
challenged the treatment of private caterers alongside the public
service. The ministry replied that rationed supplies were allocated on
the same basis, according to meals served, while some scarce foods
received priority for feeding workers. Public kitchens and private
businesses existed together; their terms were already being argued
over. Hansard, October 1, 1941
The next dayʼs debate sharpened both sides. A critic with catering
interests alleged that British Restaurants were taking customers from
struggling businesses. He also described a public restaurant at Fish-
mongersʼ Hall that opened at noon, too late for the Billingsgate
workers he wanted it to serve. These were claims made in debate, not
an independent audit. But the second complaint gives the proposal a
useful test: a kitchen can exist, have supplies, and still miss the
person whose working day it was supposed to help. Hansard, October
2, 1941
