SURVIVING THE SINGULARITY

Grandmaʼs request
Now follow one request all the way to the door.

Picture an eighty-two-year-old woman on a fixed income at the edge
of town. Her joints are stiff with arthritis. Her yard has gone to star
thistle, a fire hazard for her and for everyone downwind, and the
county has sent a code-enforcement notice with a fine she canʼt pay.
A landscaping crew wants $400 cash. The groceries are another calcu-
lation: sheʼs started buying what she can carry instead of what sheʼd
choose.
Whatʼs she supposed to do? Sit there, run out of money, and wait for
fire season?
Absolutely not.
This is exactly where the lone-prepper story falls apart. A bunker
does nothing for her. What she needs is a network of neighbors with
tools, food, and time, organized well enough to show up. Hereʼs what
that looks like, as an illustration, not a report of a real program.
The nodes. Several households in the area each run a shop-house, or
a shouse: half workshop, half home, with the tools, some power, and
often a garden or a greenhouse attached. Chapter 13 builds one. Each
is a node in a small network. Some nodes have a surplus of food in a
given week. Some have a tractor or a chipper. Some have neither, and
thatʼs fine.
The balance. Count each resource in its own unit and over the same
week: usable vegetables, prepared meals, equipment hours, driver
time. For each one:

      Available to share = usable supply − prior commitments −
      reserve for interruptions

hours and call the total abundance. A single garden, even a heavily
