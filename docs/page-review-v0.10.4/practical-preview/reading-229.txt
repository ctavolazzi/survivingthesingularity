Precedent P-15: One Hundred Sixty Acres (United
States, 1862)
The Homestead Act was signed in May 1862, and the timing is half the
lesson: the country was a year into the Civil War. While every head-
line was consumed by the emergency, the federal government
opened millions of acres, offering one hundred sixty acres to eligible
claimants, including women heads of household and immigrants
whoʼd declared their intent to become citizens, who would live on the
land and improve it for five years. National Archives, Homestead Act
of 1862
Filing began in January 1863. The ordinary five-year route therefore
ran beyond the Civil Warʼs end in 1865; Daniel Freemanʼs surviving
certificate of eligibility dates to January 1868.  US Capitol Visitor
Center, Freemanʼs certificate There was also a paid route to earlier
title. The opportunity opened during the crisis. Turning a claim into a
lasting farm still took time, money, and successful proof.
The other half of the lesson is who was already there. That land
wasnʼt empty. It was the home of Indigenous nations, and much of it
had been taken through war, treaties broken, and forced removal.
The National Park Serviceʼs own account of the Homestead story
holds both histories together, and so should we. National Park Ser-
vice, Homestead History and Culture Access for one group canʼt be
told honestly as though it happened on a blank map.
