“Now,” Priya said, sitting on the bed edge like it was a lab bench, “tell
me where your probe lives.”

His probe lived at one centimeter, effectively. It was a surface-
moisture sensor, and July had cooked the top inch of every bed
equally, and his dashboard had done the one thing dashboards do
best, which is extend a reading consistently in all directions. Down in
the pits the beds differed exactly where the roots had gone. His map
had been measuring the weather of the crust and calling it the
climate of the planet.
“Your sensor lives at one centimeter,” Priya said, not unkindly, in the
tone of a woman who had spent twenty years watching agencies fund
the crust. “The root lives at thirty. You instrumented the part of the
system thatʼs easiest to reach and assumed the rest agreed with it.
Everyone does. Whole nations do.” She nodded at the pit. “Roosevelt
wrote every governor in the country in 1937: the nation that destroys
its soil destroys itself. Every extension office I ever worked in had
that framed on the wall, and every budget I ever sat through funded
the crust anyway.”

He sat in the dirt with his ruined conclusion and started laughing,
because he had been here before. The vise, the shovel: the co-op kept
administering the same exam and he kept being surprised by the
questions. Since the month a $211 power bill had rearranged his life,
he had trusted meters over feelings, and the trust had mostly paid.
Here was the lesson under that lesson, the one the plug-in meter had
never needed to teach because a wall socket is shallow: a meter tells
the truth only about the exact place it lives. Instrument the wrong
stratum and you donʼt get noise, you get confident fiction.

The fix cost about eleven dollars a bed: probes at three depths on a
length of PVC, five, fifteen, thirty centimeters, checked against the
shovel, which Priya made him keep doing monthly, pit and squeeze
and smell, “so the instruments stay honest and so do you.”
