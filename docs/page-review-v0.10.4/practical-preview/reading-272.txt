Pool the work
Growing food takes labor, tools, water, and attention across the
season. A household doesnʼt have to provide all of that alone. Pool it:
 • A tool commons. Instead of five neighbors each buying a fragile
   little tiller, pool your money for one heavy-duty walk-behind
   tractor with swappable implements, and a shed to keep it in.
 • Work by the season, not the clock. A coordinated weekend crew
   can share a major planting or harvest. Keep watering, crop
   checks, and the next round of harvesting on a rota too; the garden
   keeps growing between work parties.
 • A community seed library. Collect and share open-pollinated
   varieties adapted to your own climate, whose seed you can save
   and replant. Many hybrids donʼt come back true from saved seed,
   and some patented varieties come with contracts that forbid
   saving it. Your own seed is a living library, shared freely.

From a plot to a plate
Go back to Chapter 9ʼs five-household trial. The garden records the
crop and the usable harvest. A food partner handles the rest. The
record shows what was lost, what reached people, and what still had
to come from elsewhere. A garden can contribute to a food baseline
without being described as self-sufficiency; the baseline has to meet
peopleʼs actual diets across seasons and interruptions.
