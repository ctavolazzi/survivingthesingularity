And thereʼs a part the cheerful posters donʼt carry. Japanese Ameri-
cans grew Victory Gardens inside the camps where their own govern-
ment had imprisoned them. Their skill made life better under condi-
tions they never chose. A national food campaign didnʼt mean equal
standing in the nation.
Then the war ended, the campaign stopped, and the capacity faded,
which is part of why your neighbor thinks food comes from a truck.

The mechanism.  Distributed production scales sideways. Millions of
small plots came online faster than any central capacity could have been
built, because the capacity already existed, dormant, in ordinary soil and
ordinary hands. The bottleneck was the decision to organize it: land,
water, seed, advice, and a way to get the harvest to a kitchen.
The rule. Standby capacity is real, and so is its limit: a vegetable harvest is
a contribution, not a complete diet.
The practice.
1. Activate one plot this season, however small: a raised bed, three
   buckets on a balcony, a community-garden plot. Then measure the
   output honestly: kilograms harvested, meals made. Itʼll be small the
   first year, and thatʼs fine. The Victory Gardens werenʼt built on experts.
   They were built on millions of first-years.
2. Make it a network, not a hobby. One garden is produce; a street of
   gardens is infrastructure. Find the two or three people nearest you
   who grow anything, trade one variety or one skill this season, and
   follow one harvest all the way to someoneʼs table.
3. Keep the standby system warm: save seed from one crop, keep the
   tools maintained and the beds alive over winter, and grow at least one
   thing every year. Capacity that never gets exercised is a rumor, not a
   capability.
