## Corrections to the first pass

Two things I got wrong. Both mattered.

**1. I attributed a causal claim to Allen that Allen does not make.**

The first pass said the Engels' pause reversal "tracks the same countervailing power Acemoglu and
Johnson identify: organizing and legislation." That is not Allen's finding. I imported it from
Acemoglu and Johnson and attached it to Allen's paper. Having now read the paper, Allen's actual
conclusion is the opposite emphasis:

> "productivity growth and capital accumulation were principally responsible for the rise in
> working class living standards after 1850, just as they had been responsible for their
> stagnation in the first half of the 19th century."

Allen's abstract calls the surge in inequality "intrinsic to the growth process." So Allen and
Acemoglu and Johnson are offering **competing explanations** of the same reversal. That tension
is more useful to the book than either one alone, and the book must not present one as settled.

**2. Two C4 figures came from a different survey round than the report I cited.**

The first pass gave 83 percent Hispanic and $12.32 per hour against NAWS Research Report 17.
Those are 2015-2016 and 2017-2018 numbers. Report 17 covers 2021-2022 and says 75 percent and
$14.53. Corrected below. This is exactly why the [summary] tags existed.

**3. I gave a day rate without stating the denominator.**

The first pass converted a monthly wage to "$3.77 a day" using calendar days without saying so.
Over working days the same wage is $4.35 or $5.14, which changes whether CT's claim survives.
See C3. Choosing a denominator silently is how a correct number becomes a wrong conclusion.

**4. I derived a figure and presented it like a quotation.**

"About 70 percent foreign born" was not read out of Report 17. It is 100 minus the reported
US-born share. Sound arithmetic, but it was not labeled as mine. Now labeled.

**5. I made a source claim stronger than the source makes it.**

The first pass said no major brand can show that **any** workers earn enough to escape poverty.
Clean Clothes actually says: "No fashion brand can say **all** its workers are paid a living wage."
Any and all are not the same claim, and mine was the unsupportable one. Corrected in C3.

**6. I gave a derived percentage instead of the source's own framing.**

I wrote "about 55 percent of a living wage." The Industry Wage Gap Metric (2022) states it as
"Garment workers earn on average 45% less than a living wage." Same arithmetic, but quote the
source as written rather than its complement.

**7. My C5 employment figures were garbled from a secondary summary.**

I gave "about 16 percent from late 2022 to mid-2025" and "near 20 percent among young software
developers." Neither matches the study those numbers come from. Corrected in C5 with the
primary figures.

All eight quoted passages were matched as exact substrings against the PDFs on disk: five from

Two deliberately fabricated quotations were run through the same check as negative controls,

something.

### Allen's per-annum table compounds to Allen's stated totals

The paper gives both annual growth rates and period totals. They are independent statements and
they agree:

| | compounded from table rates | stated in text |
|---|---|---|
| Output per worker 1840-1900 | 88% | 90% |
| Real wage 1840-1900 | 125% | 123% |
| Output per worker 1780-1840 | 42% | 46% |

Within two to four points, which is what segment-boundary alignment should cost. This confirms the
table was read correctly.

**One precision note this exposed.** Compounding the wage rates across 1780-1840 gives about
+18 percent, not flat. The pause is genuinely flat in the **1800 to 1830** window, where the
table reads 0.00 percent. Do not describe the whole 1780-1840 span as flat. Use the 30-year window.

### Stanford's two independent figures reconcile exactly

The study reports a relative employment gap of about 19 percent, and separately reports levels:
the two most exposed quintiles fell about 11 percent while the three least exposed grew about
10 percent. Those levels imply a relative gap of **0.89 / 1.10 - 1 = -19.1 percent**. The two
reported numbers are consistent with each other.

### The Bangladesh wage figures looked contradictory and are not

NYU Stern (2019) reports Bangladesh at USD 95 a month. Cornell reports the pre-2023 wage of
BDT 8,000 as USD 75. Same nominal wage, two different dollar figures. Resolved: it is currency.

- Verified 2019 average rate: **1 USD = 84.398 BDT**. BDT 8,000 at that rate is **USD 94.79**,
  which matches NYU Stern's 95.
- Cornell's USD 75 implies about 106.7 BDT/USD, which is 2024 territory. Verified: Bangladesh
  Bank moved the rate from Tk110 in January 2024 to **Tk122 on 31 December 2024**, a 12.72
  percent depreciation in that year alone.

**The mechanism this exposes.** The BDT 8,000 minimum was set in 2018 and not revised until 2023,
because Bangladesh reviews apparel wages once every five years. Over that frozen period it fell
from about USD 95 to about USD 73 **without anyone changing it**, a loss of roughly 21 percent in
dollar terms. The replacement, BDT 12,500, was worth USD 113.64 in January 2024 and **USD 102.46
by December 2024**, losing about 10 percent in its first year. It is not scheduled for review
until 2028.

**Caveat that must travel with this, or it becomes a wrong conclusion.** Garment workers are paid
in taka and spend in taka. Dollar depreciation is not automatically a cut in their purchasing
power. It bites directly on imported goods, and it distorts every cross-country comparison in
this memo, including CT's "$3 a day." The correct measure of what happened to purchasing power is
Bangladeshi CPI over the same period, **and this memo does not have it.** Get it before the book
says anything about workers getting poorer. What is established here is narrower and still worth
saying: a wage fixed in nominal local currency for five years is a mechanism for erosion, and the
dollar figures quoted internationally are not stable enough to compare without a date attached.

## C1. People value safety, food and shelter, not social order as such

**Verdict: overstated as written. Evidence found, and it does not support the strong version.**

- Pew, February 2024: about three-quarters across racial and ethnic groups name strengthening
  the economy a top policy priority. [summary]
  https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2024/02/29/americans-top-policy-priority-for-2024-strengthening-the-economy/
- Gallup: the economy tops voters' list of key election issues. [summary]
  https://news.gallup.com/poll/321617/economy-tops-voters-list-key-election-issues.aspx
- But crime ranks among top concerns too, with a partisan split. Republicans rank economy and
  national security first, **then crime**, education and gun policy. [summary]

**What this means.** "Very few people care about protecting social order" is not survivable as
written. Order and crime are measured priorities for a large bloc, consistently. What the
evidence does support is weaker and still useful: material security ranks at or near the top
across groups and across parties.

**The distinction that rescues the point.** CT is arguing about what people want *protected*,
not what they name in a poll. There is a real difference between "social order" as an abstraction
and "crime" as a felt threat to safety, and the second collapses into CT's own category of
feeling safe. That is an argument CT can make honestly. What he cannot do is assert that few
people care about order, because people say otherwise when asked.

Recommended restatement: order is valued instrumentally, for the safety it delivers, and support
for it tracks whether it is delivering. Offer that as the author's read, and say so.

## C3. Sustained by low-paid labor

**Verdict: supported. Bangladesh now verified at source.**

**Verified, Cornell ILR Global Labor Institute brief, February 2025** (read directly):
> "The 2023 minimum wage was pushed up to BDT 12,500 (USD 113) from the BDT 8,000 (USD 75) rate
> set in 2018."

- Worker organizations called for BDT 23,000, around USD 200.
- Bangladesh reviews the apparel minimum wage **once every five years**, most recently 2023.
- Patagonia endorsed BDT 23,000 per month in October 2024.
  https://www.ilr.cornell.edu/sites/default/files-d8/2025-02/cornell-gli-brief-bangladesh-february-2025_0.pdf

**The denominator is load-bearing and the first pass did not state it.** A monthly wage becomes
a day rate only once you say which days:

| | calendar days (30) | 6-day weeks (26) | 5-day weeks (22) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bangladesh USD 113/mo | $3.77 | $4.35 | $5.14 |
| Ethiopia USD 26/mo | $0.87 | $1.00 | $1.18 |

So CT's "less than $3 a day" **holds comfortably for Ethiopia under every denominator** and
**does not hold for Bangladesh's legal minimum under a working-day denominator**. The fix is to
name the country and state the denominator. Ethiopia carries the sentence; Bangladesh does not.

**Second category problem: a legal minimum is not what anyone was paid.** CT's sentence is about
what Simeon earned. A statutory minimum is a policy floor, and actual earnings sit above it with
overtime and piece rates, or below it where enforcement fails. If the book wants a figure for
what a worker was paid, it needs an earnings source, not a wage-board decision. These are
different objects and the passage currently treats them as one.

**Verified, NYU Stern Center for Business and Human Rights, Barrett and Baumann-Pauly, 2019**
(read at source): Ethiopian garment workers' minimum base wage is **$26 a month**. Comparisons
given: China $340, Kenya $207, Bangladesh $95. The estimated minimum monthly living wage in
Ethiopia is about **$110**, so the base wage is roughly **24 percent** of a living wage.
https://www.business-humanrights.org/en/latest-news/ethiopias-garment-workers-are-worlds-lowest-paid-says-report/

**Caveat that must travel with it: the study is from 2019.** Seven years old at time of writing.
Either find the current figure or date the sentence explicitly. Do not present a 2019 wage as
today's wage.

**Verified, Clean Clothes Campaign** (read at source), and the sharpest fact in this section is
not the one the first pass chose:
- **"less than 2% of garment workers make a living wage"** (attributed to Fashion Revolution,
  no date given on the page).
- "Garment workers earn on average 45% less than a living wage" (Industry Wage Gap Metric, 2022).
- "No fashion brand can say **all** its workers are paid a living wage." Note **all**, not any.
  https://cleanclothes.org/poverty-wages

The "less than 2 percent" figure does the work the first pass wanted from the brand claim, and it
does it without overreaching. Use it, and chase its underlying Fashion Revolution source and date
before print, because the page gives neither.

**The five-year review cycle is the detail worth having.** A wage fixed for five years against
food inflation is a mechanism, not a misfortune. It explains *how* the floor stays down without
anyone deciding each year to keep it there.

## C5. AI alarm is white-collar fear of joining that class

**Verdict: supported on the presence half. Absence half needs a method, specified here.**

**Rhetoric side, still [summary]:** Amodei warning through 2025 of a white-collar bloodbath and
roughly half of entry-level white-collar roles in five years; Suleyman on all white-collar work
being automatable within 18 months; 18 to 24 year olds over twice as likely to fear displacement.

**Measurement side, now traced to the primary study.** Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, "Canaries
in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence,"
Stanford Digital Economy Lab, first released August 2025, updated August 2026. Administrative
payroll data from the largest US payroll provider.
- Original release: a **13 percent relative decline** in employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in
  the most AI-exposed occupations, after controlling for firm-level shocks.
- Updated: the gap has widened to about **19 percent** below where it would be had it kept pace
  with similarly aged workers in less-exposed occupations.
- In levels, November 2022 to June 2026: employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the two most exposed
  quintiles **fell about 11 percent**, while the same age group in the three least-exposed
  quintiles **grew about 10 percent**.
- Mechanism: **reduced hiring of young workers, not increased separations.**
  https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/

**The finding that matters most for CT is the headline on the update: "No Widespread
Displacement."** The best measurement available says the effect is real but narrow, concentrated
in one age band in exposed occupations, working through hiring rather than firing. The alarm in
the rhetoric side above is running well ahead of it. That gap between what is said and what is
measured is C5's argument, and it is now evidenced rather than asserted.

**Note for the book:** Brynjolfsson wrote both `The Second Machine Age`, cited in C6 for the
bounty argument, and this study. The same economist supplies the optimistic case and the
measurement that constrains it. That is worth a sentence.

**Method for the absence half.** Do not assert the absence. Measure it, or drop the word.
Proposed: take a fixed corpus, for example AI-labor coverage in three named outlets over a stated
twelve-month window, count pieces mentioning garment or agricultural labor against total, publish
the window, the outlets and the counts, and report what was found including if it undercuts the
point. A counted claim with a stated method survives review. "Nobody talks about" does not.

## C6. Almost no one discusses floor-raising

**Verdict: UNSUPPORTED. Confirmed a second time, more decisively.**

The first pass found Acemoglu and Johnson. The second found the affirmative floor-raising
argument stated plainly by a prominent figure:

- **Altman, "Moore's Law for Everything"** (read at source, verbatim):
  > "The price of many kinds of labor (which drives the costs of goods and services) will fall
  > toward zero"
  > "Imagine a world where, for decades, everything-housing, education, food, clothing,
  > etc.-became half as expensive every two years."
  > "we can improve the standard of living for people more than we ever have before"

  He also proposes the distribution mechanism, an American Equity Fund taxing companies 2.5
  percent annually and land value 2.5 percent, paying every citizen over 18 an annual
  distribution, under which "Poverty would be greatly reduced."
  https://moores.samaltman.com/

  **This is decisive for C6.** It is the floor-raising argument, stated plainly, by one of the
  most prominent people in the field, with a redistribution proposal attached. The sentence
  "I've seen absolutely NO ONE talking about how the pace of tech promises to increase the floor
  level of quality for ALL HUMANS" cannot survive alongside it.
- **Brynjolfsson and McAfee, The Second Machine Age (2014)**: an enormous bounty is coming, but
  "there is no economic law that says that all workers, or even a majority of workers, will
  benefit from these advances." [summary]
- **Benanav, Automation and the Future of Work (2020)**: argues toward "a world of abundance" by
  social change, while rejecting the automation discourse's premises, on grounds that productivity
  and growth have been *decelerating* for decades. [summary]
  https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii119/articles/aaron-benanav-automation-and-the-future-of-work-1

"Absolutely NO ONE" fails on the first search anyone runs. Altman alone sinks it.

**What is actually rare, and is CT's.** Three positions exist separately and are seldom combined:
the moral accounting of who is exploited *now*, the affirmative floor-raising case, and the
realism that oppression comes first. Altman has the second without the first. Acemoglu and
Johnson have the mechanism without the affirmative case. Benanav has the critique and doubts the
productivity premise entirely. The combination is the contribution. Claim that, not priority.

**Counter-argument to engage.** From the abundance debate: without real supply-side abundance in
the sectors people actually spend on, transfers become a redistribution engine pushing demand
into bottlenecks. That is a direct test of "if we steer this right" and the book should answer it.

## C7. Oppression arrives first

**Verdict: supported, now verified at source. The cause is contested, and that belongs in the book.**

**Verified, Allen, "Engels' pause: Technical change, capital accumulation, and inequality in the
British industrial revolution," Explorations in Economic History (2009), doi:10.1016/j.eeh.2009.04.004**
(read directly):

> "The paper reviews the macroeconomic data describing the British economy from 1760 to 1913 and
> shows that it passed through a two stage evolution of inequality. In the first half of the 19th
> century, the real wage stagnated while output per worker expanded. The profit rate doubled and
> the share of profits in national income expanded at the expense of labour and land. After the
> middle of the 19th century, real wages began to grow in line with productivity, and the profit
> rate and factor shares stabilized."

Exact figures from the paper:
- "output per worker rose by 46% between 1780 and 1840"
- **"Between 1840 and 1900, output per worker increased by 90% and the real wage by 123%."**
- Growth table, per annum: **1800-1830, output per worker 0.63%, real wage 0.00%.**
  1830-1860: 1.12% and 0.86%. 1860-1900: 1.03% and 1.61%.
- "labour's share dropped from 50% in 1801 to 45% in 1841"
- The real wage "grew very little from 1770 to about 1840 and then rose in line with output per worker."

**The strongest single number in the whole argument is 0.00 percent.** Thirty years, 1800 to 1830,
of exactly zero real wage growth while output per worker grew 0.63 percent a year. That is CT's
"oppression will obviously come first" as a measurement, not a mood.

**And then it reverses, harder than it fell.** 1840 to 1900, real wages up 123 percent against
output per worker up 90 percent. Wages grew *faster* than productivity for sixty years. "But what
happens afterwards?" has an answer with a date range.

**The contested part, which the book must present as contested.** Allen attributes both phases to
the same engine: "productivity growth and capital accumulation were principally responsible for
the rise in working class living standards after 1850, just as they had been responsible for their
stagnation." Acemoglu and Johnson attribute the reversal to countervailing power. These are rival
explanations. Presenting either as settled would be the same unsupported-prescription error the
v0.8.0 critique corrected five times. Present both, say which way the evidence leans and why, and
let the reader see that the mechanism of recovery is genuinely open.

**A third explanation, in Allen's own words, that neither side of the above captures:**
> "Consequently, population growth was a necessary condition for stationary real wages: Engels'
> pause looks like Malthus' dismal science come true."

And Allen declines to read the pause as purely extractive:
> "The pause had a progressive side, however, for the bourgeoisie saved from its growing income,
> and the ensuing investment drove the economy forward."

That second quote is uncomfortable for the argument and belongs in the book anyway. Allen's model
has the suppressed wage *funding* the investment that later raised it. A reader who finds that on
their own after trusting the chapter will trust nothing else in it.

**On the duration, do not use "roughly fifty years."** That range, usually given as 1790 to 1840,
comes from secondary summaries. Allen does not bound it that way. He writes "the first half of the
19th century" and "the period of constant wages in the midst of rising output per worker," with
figures plotted 1770 to 1910. What is exactly verifiable from his table is the 1800 to 1830 window
at 0.00 percent real wage growth. Use his framing, or use the thirty-year window with the number
attached. Either is defensible. The fifty-year figure attributed to Allen is not.

## C8. The nine-stage pattern

**Verdict: label wrong, model sound, and stage 8 is stronger than CT wrote it.**

The label finding is unchanged: Gartner's hype cycle is five phases about expectation, Perez is
installation and deployment across decades organized around capital, and CT's stages are about
power, which is a third thing. Cut "This is called the hype cycle." It is the only factually wrong
sentence in the source.

**Gap now closed: Luddite scholarship supports CT and sharpens stage 8.**

- Kevin Binfield, editor, `Writings of the Luddites` (2004): the Luddites "were totally fine with
  machines." They objected "not to the use of a new kind of machine, but to the use of existing
  machines in ways that reduced wages and produced shoddy clothing." [summary]
- They confined attacks to manufacturers using machines in "a fraudulent and deceitful manner" to
  get around standard labor practices. They wanted machines making high-quality goods, run by
  apprenticed workers on decent wages. [summary]
- **Hobsbawm, "The Machine Breakers"** (read at source, verbatim):
  > "He was concerned, not with technical progress in the abstract, but with the practical twin
  > problems of preventing unemployment and maintaining the customary standard of life"
  > "not to the machine as such that he objected, but to any threat to these - above all to the
  > whole change in the social relations of production which threatened him"
  > "Wrecking was simply a technique of trade unionism in the period before, and during the early
  > phases of, the industrial revolution."
  > "collective bargaining by riot was at least as effective as any other means of bringing trade
  > union pressure"
  https://libcom.org/article/machine-breakers-eric-hobsbawm
- Randall (1991) and Binfield (2004): several Luddisms, not one unified movement. [summary]

**Rewrite stage 8.** "The technology is violently resisted" is the popular story and it is wrong.
The historical record says the resistance targeted *deployment that bypassed labor standards*,
by people who operated the machines and wanted them run properly. That is CT's own thesis, found
in the archive, two centuries early. It is the single biggest upgrade available to the piece:
the canonical example of anti-technology panic turns out to be an example of exactly the power
argument CT is making.
