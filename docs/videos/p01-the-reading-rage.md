## Read this first

**1. This might be a better Episode 1 than P-03.** It's the first case in the book,
it's short, it's funny, and it hits the most common AI worry people have ("it's rotting
kids' brains"). P-03 is still the stronger hook for a cold audience, but if you want the
series in book order, this one opens it. Either works; they don't depend on each other.

**2. Don't dismiss parents.** Some worries about kids and screens are backed by real
research, and a parent watching this should not feel mocked. The book's own practice
says "Not to mock them." The script aims at the *panic reflex*, the automatic "this is
rotting their brains," not at every concern. There's a line at 2:15 that says so.

**3. The Sheridan quote, handled honestly.** (The book's text was corrected to match on 2026-09-22.) *The Rivals* is from 1775, not the 1790s,
and the "diabolical knowledge" line belongs to Sir Anthony Absolute, a comic character
the play is laughing at. The script says both. It makes the point better: even in 1775,
the panic was already a joke on stage.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you, holding a paperback novel.]

> In the 1790s, respectable people were sure a new technology was rotting the minds of
> the young. Especially young women.
>
> The technology was this. The novel.

[Title card: THE READING RAGE.]

### 0:15 The panic (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: 18th-century engravings of women reading, circulating library interiors.]

> Here's what happened. Printing got cheap. Circulating libraries made books almost
> free. And suddenly young women were spending hours doing something nobody could
> supervise: reading whatever they wanted.
>
> The adults lost it. A minister named Enos Hitchcock, 1790: "The free access which many
> young people have to romances, novels, and plays has poisoned the mind and corrupted
> the morals of many a promising youth."
>
> People talked about "reading fever." "Reading mania." It was blamed for bad morals,
> ruined eyesight, bad posture.
>
> Reading for fun was the brain rot of 1790.

### 1:15 Even the jokes (about 30 seconds)

[On screen: the Sheridan line as text.]

> It was so common that by 1775 it was already a joke on stage. In Sheridan's comedy
> *The Rivals*, a pompous old guy calls the circulating library "an evergreen tree of
> diabolical knowledge." And the play is laughing at him, not with him.

### 1:45 The flip (about 30 seconds)

> Now think about where we are. The thing we now hand to kids as the cure for screens,
> the thing on the poster in every school library, "READ," got attacked by a whole
> generation of grown-ups in almost exactly the words people now use about the phone in
> your pocket.

### 2:15 The mechanism (about 45 seconds)

[Back on you.]

> Here's what I think was really going on. The panic was never about the books.
>
> A new tool had given power to somebody the adults couldn't supervise anymore. And
> they felt that loss of control as moral decay.
>
> And look, not every worry about a new technology is a panic. Some of them turn out to
> be right. But the reflex, the instant "this is rotting their brains," is usually
> telling you where the power just went.

### 3:00 What to do (about 45 seconds)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> Three things.
>
> One. Next three times you hear "AI is rotting people's brains," write down who said
> it, and what they can't supervise anymore. Not to mock them. Just to train your ear.
>
> Two. Find someone at least fifteen years younger than you who uses AI every day. Ask
> to watch them work for twenty minutes. Say nothing for the first ten.
>
> Three. Next time a tool you don't use annoys you on principle, compare the hour it'd
> take to learn it with how long you've already spent being annoyed. Do the cheaper
> thing.
