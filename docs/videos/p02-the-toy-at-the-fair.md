## Read this first: the fake memo

The most famous line about the telephone is a Western Union memo supposedly calling it
"a toy" with "too many shortcomings to be seriously considered as a means of
communication." **Your own book says that memo "has never been produced by anyone and is
almost certainly a fabrication"** (chapter 7, "The Campaign Has a Letterhead," and
Appendix D on sourcing).

P-02 doesn't quote the memo, but its title ("The Toy at the Fair") and its Western
Union line sit right next to the legend, and commenters will bring it up. So the script
**says the memo is fake, on camera, in one breath.** That's a credibility win: it shows
the channel checks the stories everyone else repeats.

Separately, the book's claim that Western Union was "offered Bell's patents around that
time" and passed has **no Appendix B source.** It's widely reported, but it's marked
VERIFY. "Toy" in this episode's title is the book's own word for how the fair treated
the telephone, not a quote from anyone.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you.]

> At the 1876 World's Fair, the crowd came to see a giant steam engine.
>
> In a side room, a guy was showing off a wooden box that carried a human voice down a
> wire. The judges almost skipped it.

[Title card: THE TOY AT THE FAIR.]

### 0:15 The engine (about 45 seconds)

[B-roll: Centennial Exhibition engravings and photos, the Corliss engine.]

> Philadelphia, 1876. America's hundredth birthday. The Centennial Exhibition.
>
> And the star of the show is the Corliss engine. A massive steam engine, stories tall,
> around fourteen hundred horsepower. President Grant starts it himself. It's the
> certified future of American industry.
>
> People crossed the country to stand next to it.

### 1:00 The box (about 45 seconds)

[On screen: Bell's early telephone, period illustration.]

> Meanwhile, in a side gallery, a teacher from Boston named Alexander Graham Bell is
> showing off a gadget.
>
> It's hot. The judges are wiped out. They nearly tell him to come back tomorrow.
>
> One of them is William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, the most famous physicist alive.
> He listens. And he calls it "the most wonderful thing in America."
>
> Almost nobody else at the fair saw it that way.

### 1:45 The fake memo (about 30 seconds)

> Now you've probably heard that Western Union wrote a memo calling the telephone a
> "toy." That memo has never been found. It's almost certainly made up.
>
> [VERIFY before saying:] What does seem to be true is that Western Union was offered
> Bell's patents and passed. Either way, the steam engine got the applause. The
> telephone got the century.

### 2:15 The mechanism (about 30 seconds)

[Back on you.]

> Every demo has two audiences in the same room.
>
> One is measuring the machine against today. Is it impressive?
>
> The other is measuring today against the machine. What dies when this gets boring?

### 2:45 What to do (about 45 seconds)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> So, three things.
>
> One. Take the last AI demo that impressed you. Assume what it does is free,
> invisible, and everywhere in three years. Which of your income streams survive that?
> That list is your real resume.
>
> Two. Next time you watch a demo, watch the room instead of the stage. Who's
> clapping? Who went quiet and started taking notes? Find out what the quiet ones do for
> a living.
>
> Three. Keep a demo log. One line per demo: the date, what it did, and when you think
> it'll be boring. Check it every six months.
