## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you. No music, no logo.]

> On October 9th, 1903, the New York Times told its readers that a flying machine would
> take one million to ten million years to build.
>
> Sixty-nine days later, two bicycle mechanics flew one.

[Cut to black. Title card: ONE MILLION YEARS, GIVE OR TAKE.]

### 0:15 The editorial (about 1 minute)

[On screen: the headline "FLYING MACHINES WHICH DO NOT FLY." Hold the quote as text
while you read it.]

> The headline was "Flying Machines Which Do Not Fly." And the Times wasn't being
> cute. It ran the numbers. Its conclusion was that a working airplane might come out of,
> and I'm quoting, "the combined and continuous efforts of mathematicians and
> mechanicians in from one million to ten million years."
>
> A million years. Minimum.
>
> Now, the easy version of this story is "newspaper says dumb thing, gets dunked on by
> history." That's not the story. The story is why they said it. Because the people who
> wrote that editorial weren't stupid. They were doing exactly what smart, careful people
> are supposed to do.

### 1:15 Why they were wrong for the right reasons (about 1.5 minutes)

[B-roll: Langley's Aerodrome on the houseboat; the crash into the Potomac.]

> Two days before that editorial ran, Samuel Langley launched his flying machine off a
> houseboat on the Potomac River. Langley was the real deal. Head of the Smithsonian.
> [VERIFY: Fifty thousand dollars from the War Department.] The most credentialed
> aviation effort in the country.
>
> It went straight into the river.
>
> So the Times looked at the best evidence available, which was the best-funded,
> best-credentialed team in America falling into the water, and it extrapolated. Their
> data was accurate. Their reasoning was careful. And their estimate was off by a factor
> of about five million.
>
> Because while they were writing a very reasonable autopsy of Langley, two guys from
> Dayton, Ohio were building the thing. No press. No government money. A bicycle shop.
>
> December 17th, 1903. Kitty Hawk.
>
> [VERIFY: Twelve seconds. About a hundred and twenty feet.] Shorter than the wingspan of
> a modern airliner. It doesn't matter. The door was open.

### 2:45 The mechanism (about 1 minute)

[Back on you. This is the line the whole episode hangs on; slow down.]

> Here's what I want you to take from this.
>
> Expert timelines are autopsies of the most recent failure. They are not forecasts of
> the next success.
>
> The Times measured Langley's crash, because that's what was in front of them. You can't
> measure a bicycle shop in Ohio that nobody's writing about. And progress at a frontier
> isn't a trend line you can extend with a ruler. It's a door. And doors open all at
> once.
>
> One million years to done. Sixty-nine days.

### 3:45 The turn: this is happening again (about 1.5 minutes)

[On screen: simple two-row chart. 2022 survey: 2060. 2023 survey: 2047.]

> So let's talk about now.
>
> There's a big survey of AI researchers, thousands of them, the people actually
> building these systems. One of the questions is: when is there a fifty percent chance
> of machines that can do every task better and cheaper than a human?
>
> In the 2022 round, the answer was 2060.
>
> One year later, same survey, same kind of people: 2047.
>
> They moved their own forecast thirteen years closer in twelve months. And the same
> survey put a ten percent chance on it happening as early as 2027. That's not a
> prediction from some guy on a forum. That's the field.
>
> I'm not telling you the date. Nobody knows the date. That's the point. When somebody
> credentialed hands you a nice, comfortable timeline, remember the unit of error. It
> isn't years. It's orders of magnitude. And history almost always misses in the
> direction nobody was bracing for.

### 5:15 What to actually do (about 1 minute)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> So here's what I do instead of trusting the forecast.
>
> One. Keep a timeline file. Every time an expert publishes an AI timeline, paste it in
> with the date and who said it. Look at it every few months. Within a year you'll know,
> with receipts, exactly how much a confident forecast is worth.
>
> Two. Plan by doors, not dates. Write down three things AI can't do yet that would
> change your job or your household if it could. Check them every month. When one opens,
> and one will, you already decided what you're going to do about it.
>
> Three. Make one bet this month that looks a little early if this takes ten years, and
> looks essential if it takes two. Learn the tools. Plant the garden. Cut one fixed cost.
>
> The Times risked nothing on its million years. You don't get that luxury. You live
> here.

### 6:15 The ask (20 seconds)

[On screen: book cover, survivingthesingularity.com/early-access.]

> This is one of twenty-three cases in my book, Surviving the Singularity. Every one is
> a time the smartest people in the room were sure about a new technology, and what that
> tells us about this one. The draft is free to read on the site. Five bucks preorders
> the finished edition. Link's below.
>
> Next time: the most famous musician in America, and the machine he thought would kill
> music.

[End card. The last line sets up Episode 2, P-10.]
