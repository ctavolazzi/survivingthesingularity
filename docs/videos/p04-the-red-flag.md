## Read this first

**1. What the law was actually aimed at.** Cars didn't exist in 1865. The Locomotives
Act targeted steam road locomotives: heavy traction engines that tore up roads and
frightened horses. Those were real problems. The script says so, because the honest
version is more useful: the safety concerns weren't fake, and the speed limits still
outlived their reason by decades.

**2. The "handed the car industry to Germany" claim.** The book says Britain's red-flag
era decided where the automobile would be born. Historians argue about how much the law
mattered next to other factors. The script says "the argument goes" and "the leash didn't help,"
not that it single-handedly caused it.

**3. Two things from the chapter left out on purpose:**
- **The June 2026 export-directive story.** It's recent, I can't verify it from here, and
  naming a specific lab on camera is a legal and accuracy risk. If you want it in, check
  it against your sources first.
- **The "Pause AI" passage.** The chapter's language there is heated. The script keeps
  the book's own, more defensible line from the mechanism: *regulation aimed at a
  technology's existence, rather than its specific harms,* is what fails. Plenty of
  viewers support some AI rules; this episode shouldn't tell them they're dupes.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you. Hold up a red cloth if you have one.]

> In 1865, Britain passed a law that said every self-propelled road vehicle needed a
> person walking sixty yards ahead of it. Waving a red flag.
>
> The speed limits that came with it lasted thirty-one years.

[Title card: THE RED FLAG.]

### 0:15 The law (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: engravings of steam traction engines on country roads.]

> It was called the Locomotives Act. People remember it as the Red Flag Act.
>
> Four miles an hour in the country. Two in town. A crew of three on every machine. And
> someone walking out front with a flag, so nobody got surprised.
>
> Now, to be fair: these weren't cars. Cars didn't exist yet. These were steam traction
> engines. Huge, heavy things that chewed up roads and scared horses half to death. The
> worries were real.

### 1:15 What it turned into (about 45 seconds)

> But here's what happened. The flag got dropped in 1878. The crushing speed limits
> didn't go away until 1896.
>
> And by then, the machines had changed. Engines got small. Cars showed up. And they
> were still stuck behind a law written for the old monsters, and for the people who
> made money from horses, coaches and railways.
>
> The argument goes that while Britain held the leash, the early car industry grew up
> somewhere else. Germany. France. America. Historians debate how much the law mattered.
> But the leash didn't help.

### 2:00 The mechanism (about 30 seconds)

[Back on you.]

> Here's the pattern. Incumbents rarely attack a technology head-on. They attack its
> speed. Usually in the language of safety.
>
> And a rule aimed at a real, specific harm can do real good. A rule aimed at whether a
> technology gets to exist at all doesn't stop it. It just decides where it grows up.

### 2:30 What to do (about 45 seconds)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> So, three things.
>
> One. Next time there's a big AI ban or pause in the news, ask two questions before
> you form an opinion. Who wrote it, and what do they sell? And while it holds, where
> does the technology go instead?
>
> Two. Check your own moats. If part of your income exists because a license or a rule
> keeps competitors out, assume that protection is temporary. The stagecoach owners had
> thirty-one years, and they didn't use them to change.
>
> Three. Don't build your plans on the assumption that a pause will hold. Plan for the
> technology arriving on schedule. If the pause holds, you've lost nothing.
