## Read this first

**1. The ship sizes are disputed.** The famous figure for the biggest treasure ships
(roughly 400 feet long) comes from later accounts, and many historians think it's
exaggerated. The book already hedges ("even by conservative modern estimates"). The
script avoids a specific length and says historians argue about it.

**2. "The next five hundred years happened *to* China" overclaims.** Ming and then Qing
China stayed one of the largest economies on Earth for centuries after 1433, and the
sea ban itself was relaxed in 1567. The defensible point is narrower: China stepped back
from long-distance ocean exploration, and others took over that frontier. The script
makes that point and no bigger one.

**3. Keep it respectful and unpolitical.** No modern US-versus-China framing. The
lesson is about any institution that walks away from a frontier, and the script applies
it to employers, industries and yourself.

**4. The book's rule is left out.** It's about machine intelligence leaving for space,
which is the chapter's speculative thesis. On camera, the grounded version (someone
keeps sailing, and it compounds for them) carries the episode without asking viewers
to accept the space scenario.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you.]

> In the early 1400s, China had the most powerful fleet on the planet.
>
> Then it stopped sailing. On purpose.

[Title card: THE FLEET THAT SAILED HOME FOREVER.]

### 0:15 The fleet (about 45 seconds)

[B-roll: maps of the voyages, Ming-era illustrations of ships.]

> Between 1405 and 1433, an admiral named Zheng He led seven huge voyages. Southeast
> Asia. India. Arabia. The east coast of Africa.
>
> Hundreds of ships. Tens of thousands of sailors. Historians argue about exactly how big
> the biggest ships were, but even the careful estimates put them well beyond anything
> Europe was sailing at the time.

### 1:00 The stop (about 45 seconds)

> And then it ended. Not because anyone beat them. The politics at court turned. The
> voyages were expensive, and the people who thought they were a waste won the argument.
>
> The voyages stopped. Sea bans restricted private ocean trade for generations.
>
> About sixty years later, much smaller ships from Portugal showed up in the Indian
> Ocean. And the age of ocean exploration belonged to somebody else.

### 1:45 The mechanism (about 30 seconds)

[Back on you.]

> Here's the lesson. A capability you walk away from doesn't wait for you.
>
> It goes to whoever keeps sailing. And it grows in their hands, not yours.
>
> You can't pause a frontier by leaving it. You can only hand it to whoever stays.

### 2:15 What to do (about 45 seconds)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> So, three things.
>
> One. List three skills you used to have and let slide. A language. An instrument. A
> trade. A garden. Pick the one that matters most for what's coming, and give it one hour
> a week.
>
> Two. Keep a frontier hour. One hour a week using the newest AI tools. Not reading about
> them. Using them.
>
> Three. When your company or your industry says it's "stepping back" from AI, ask out
> loud: who keeps sailing while we don't, and what do they own in five years?
