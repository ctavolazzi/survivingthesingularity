# Episode 4: The Horse's Last Ledger

**Source:** Precedent P-07, `src/lib/data/book/07-chapter5.md`, plus "The Meat Engine
vs. The Silicon Machine" and "Step One: Audit Your Energy Budget" from the same chapter.
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 5 to 5.5 minutes (about 760 spoken words at a normal
pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Written to be said out loud; change any line that doesn't sound
like you, and treat bracketed lines as directions, not dialogue.

**Tone note.** "You are the horse" is the most obvious take on this story, and it is a
doom take. The book's own line goes the other way: *the horse never saw its ledger, and
you can.* The script keeps that as the spine so the episode lands as a warning with a
way out, not as "you're getting put out to pasture." The book's tone audits hold it to
zero reader-scolding; keep the delivery on the viewer's side.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you.]

> The word "horsepower" was a sales pitch.
>
> James Watt invented it to sell steam engines to people who owned horses. And from the
> day that word existed, every horse on Earth was walking around with a price tag on
> its back.
>
> The horses just didn't know it yet.

[Title card: THE HORSE'S LAST LEDGER.]

### 0:15 Watt's problem (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: steam engine diagrams, draft horses at work. Public-domain engravings work
well here.]

> So here's Watt's problem. He's built a better steam engine. And the people he needs to
> sell it to, what do they use for power? Horses.
>
> You can't walk up to a guy who's owned horses his whole life and say "my machine
> makes so many foot-pounds per minute." He doesn't care. So Watt does something
> brilliant. He measures the machine in horses. This engine does the work of ten horses.
> Twenty horses.
>
> He priced the animal in its own currency.
>
> And once you can say "this machine equals ten horses," the next question writes
> itself. Which one's cheaper?

### 1:15 A century of looking untouchable (about 1 minute)

[On screen: simple line chart, US horse population rising from 1850 to the 1910s.]

> Here's the wild part. For about a hundred years after that, the horse looked totally
> safe.
>
> Trains come along. You'd think that's the end of the horse. It isn't. The number of
> horses in America keeps going up. Right through the railroad age. It peaks in the
> 1910s at more than twenty-five million.
>
> [Adjust to "horses" or "horses and mules" per the fact sheet.]
>
> And there's a whole economy built on them. Hay fields. Stables. Blacksmiths. Harness
> makers. Teamsters. Entire towns organized around the metabolism of one animal.
>
> If you'd asked anybody in 1910 whether the horse was in trouble, they'd have laughed.

### 2:15 The cost line (about 1 minute)

[On screen: the same chart, now falling off a cliff after the 1910s.]

> And then the gasoline engine crosses the cost line. Work per dollar. That's it.
> That's the whole story.
>
> In one working lifetime, the horse population collapses to a few million, kept mostly
> for fun.
>
> And I want you to notice what didn't happen. The horse didn't get weaker. It didn't
> get lazier. It didn't get less loyal or less beautiful. The horse was exactly as good
> at being a horse in 1950 as it was in 1910.
>
> It just got out-priced. And "but I love my horse" showed up in exactly zero rows of
> the spreadsheet.

### 3:15 The mechanism (about 45 seconds)

[Back on you. Slow.]

> Here's what was going on. There were two conversations happening at the same time.
>
> The horse owners were talking about tradition. Loyalty. Beauty. How it's always been.
>
> The tractor buyers were talking about cost per unit of work.
>
> Only one of those conversations was real.
>
> The math doesn't negotiate. And it doesn't send you a warning before it closes the
> account.

### 4:00 The turn (about 1 minute)

[On screen: "You: ~100 watts. Your brain: ~20 watts." Then the efficiency table from
chapter 5, just two rows: human muscle 18 to 25%, electric motor 90 to 95%.]

> So why am I telling you about horses?
>
> Because you run on about a hundred watts. That's two thousand-ish calories a day. Your
> brain runs on about twenty. And for the first time ever, somebody is selling
> intelligence by the kilowatt-hour.
>
> I'm not going to tell you you're the horse. Because there's one huge difference
> between you and the horse.
>
> The horse never saw its own ledger. It had no idea what it cost, what it produced, or
> who was doing the math on it. It found out its price after the fact.
>
> You can see yours. Right now. Before anybody else runs the numbers.

### 5:00 What to do (about 1 minute)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> So, three things.
>
> One. Actually write your ledger down. On paper. What comes in: food, power, money.
> What goes out: your time, your attention, your rent. And who's capturing the
> difference. Most people have never once looked at themselves the way a buyer would.
>
> Two. Split your work into two lists. The parts where you're competing with a machine
> on raw output. And the parts where you're not. Trust. Being there in person. Your
> hands. Judgment on the ground. Relationships. Spend this year moving toward the second
> list.
>
> Three. Own one piece of your own energy. A solar panel. A woodstove. A bike that
> replaces short car trips. Better insulation. Watt priced the horse in the horse's own
> currency. Every watt you own is a watt nobody can price you in.

## The Short (under 60 seconds)

Post this a few days before the long version, to test the hook.

[Vertical, tight on you.]

> The word "horsepower" was a sales pitch. James Watt made it up to sell steam engines
> to people who owned horses.
>
> For a hundred years after that, the horse did fine. More horses in America every
> decade, right up to the 1910s.
>
> Then the gas engine got cheaper per unit of work. And in one lifetime, the horses were
> gone.
>
> The horse didn't get worse. It got out-priced.
>
> And the horse never saw its own ledger coming.
>
> You can see yours. Look at it before somebody else does.

[Text on screen for the last 3 seconds: "Full story: link in bio."]
