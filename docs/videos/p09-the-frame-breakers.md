# Episode 6: The Frame-Breakers

**Source:** Precedent P-09, `src/lib/data/book/09-chapter7.md`, plus "The Primate
Backlash Is Already Here" from the same chapter.
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 4.5 to 5 minutes (about 660 spoken words at a normal
pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Written to be said out loud; change any line that doesn't sound
like you, and treat bracketed lines as directions, not dialogue.

**Where it sits.** P-09 is the prequel to Episode 2 (P-10, the musicians). In the book
they run back to back: the Luddites had no negotiating table, the musicians had every
table there was. This episode ends by pointing viewers at Episode 2, which by then is
already up.

**Tone note.** "Luddite" is an insult today. The whole point of the episode is that it
shouldn't be: these were skilled workers whose analysis was right. Respect them on
camera. The book's rule for the modern version is "fight for terms, not against
physics," not "the protesters are idiots."

## Fact sheet (check before filming)

Numbers refer to Appendix B. P-09 has the book's strongest sourcing, including a
correction of record, so hold the script to exactly what it says.

| Claim | Source |

| Crews often smashed only the frames of owners who cut wages and quality | Book text; 143 |
| Letters and proclamations went out in the name of "Ned Ludd" | 192 |
| The Frame Breaking Act (1812) made machine-breaking a capital crime | 143 |

| York, January 1813: three men hanged on the 8th, fourteen more on the 16th in two groups of seven, seventeen in all | 190, 191 |
| **The three hanged on January 8 were convicted of murdering a mill owner, William Horsfall.** The script says so. Don't imply all seventeen were hanged only for breaking machines | 190 |
| Modern "coning" of robotaxis in San Francisco; on Feb 10, 2024 a Waymo in Chinatown had its windows smashed and a lit firework thrown inside | Book text (ch7, "The Primate Backlash Is Already Here") |

**Do not say:** "they sent more troops against the Luddites than Wellington took to
fight Napoleon." It's the most repeated line about the Luddites and it's wrong. The book
cut it and printed a correction explaining why (Appendix B, "P-09 Primary Sources"). If
a commenter brings it up, that correction is a great reply.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you.]

> "Luddite" is what we call someone who doesn't understand technology.
>
> The actual Luddites understood it perfectly. They were right about everything. And in
> January 1813, seventeen of them were hanged at York.

[Title card: THE FRAME-BREAKERS.]

### 0:15 Who they actually were (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: engravings of stocking frames and cropping shears; the Midlands.]

> England, 1811. The Midlands. These are croppers and framework knitters. Not random
> angry peasants. They're the elite of their trade. Skilled craftsmen.
>
> And they look at the new wide stocking frames and they see exactly what's coming.
> Cheaper goods, worse goods, made by cheaper workers, flooding the market and dragging
> everybody's wages down.
>
> That's not ignorance. That's a correct economic forecast.

### 1:15 The hammers (about 1 minute)

[On screen: a Ned Ludd proclamation, if you can show the National Archives image with
permission; otherwise text on screen.]

> So they start breaking the machines. They send letters signed in the name of a
> "Ned Ludd." And here's the part nobody tells you.
>
> They were surgical about it. Crews often smashed only the frames of owners who were
> cutting wages and quality, and left alone the machines of masters who were paying
> fair.
>
> Think about what that means. They weren't against the machine. They were against the
> terms. This was a labor negotiation, done with hammers, because there was no other
> negotiating table. No union. No vote. Nothing.

### 2:15 The answer (about 1 minute)

[On screen: "Frame Breaking Act, 1812." Then "~12,000 soldiers." Then "York, January
1813."]

> And the government's answer?
>
> A law that made breaking a machine a crime you could be hanged for. And about twelve
> thousand soldiers sent into the counties where it was happening.
>
> January 1813, at York. Three men hanged on the eighth, for the murder of a mill owner.
> Eight days later, fourteen more. Two groups of seven.
>
> And the machines won anyway. The wages fell anyway. The craft died anyway.
>
> They got the future exactly right, and the strategy exactly wrong, and they paid for
> the difference with their lives.

### 3:15 The mechanism (about 45 seconds)

[Back on you. Slow down.]

> Here's the thing I can't stop thinking about.
>
> Rage against the machine is aimed at the one part of the system that can't hear you.
> The machine doesn't have ears.
>
> The owners heard them perfectly. And they answered with the army.
>
> Because the fight was never really human versus machine. It was, and it still is, a
> fight over who sets the terms.

### 4:00 The turn (about 45 seconds)

[B-roll: a robotaxi with a traffic cone on the hood. Use your own footage or a
properly licensed clip.]

> And it's happening again. In San Francisco people put traffic cones on the hoods of
> self-driving cars, which makes them stall in the street. In February 2024, a crowd
> smashed a Waymo's windows in Chinatown and threw a lit firework inside.
>
> I get the anger. The anger is legitimate. But the hammer is batting zero for two
> hundred years.
>
> Fight for terms. Not against physics.

### 4:45 What to do (about 1 minute)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> Three things.
>
> One. Trade your anger for building. Every hour you spend furious at the machine, buy
> one hour of working on your own position. Keep the ledger honest.
>
> Two. Write down what you know. The croppers' skill was worthless the day the frames
> could run without it. Yours has a clock too. Write down the judgment calls, the
> shortcuts, the things nobody else knows. That's your leverage, or your consulting
> business, or training data for a tool you own. Unwritten, it's nothing.
>
> Three. Before you join a movement, ask it one question: what are we building, and what
> terms are we demanding? If the honest answer is "we're against it," you've found a
> frame-breaking society. You know their record.

## The Short (under 60 seconds)

Post this a few days before the long version, to test the hook.

[Vertical, tight on you.]

> We use "Luddite" to mean someone who doesn't get technology.
>
> The real Luddites got it perfectly. Skilled weavers who saw exactly how the new
> machines would crush their wages.
>
> They were so precise they often smashed only the machines of bosses who cut pay, and
> left the fair bosses alone.
>
> The government answered with twelve thousand soldiers and the gallows.
>
> They were right about the future. And they lost anyway.
>
> Because the machine can't hear you. The people who set the terms can.

[Text on screen for the last 3 seconds: "Full story: link in bio."]

**Cut points if you film the long version first:** 0:00 to 0:15 (cold open) + 1:30 to
2:00 ("They were surgical about it") + 2:15 to 2:35 (twelve thousand soldiers) + 3:25 to
3:50 ("The machine doesn't have ears"). Record "The people who set the terms can"
separately as a closer.

**Short wording check:** "skilled weavers" is a simplification of "croppers and
framework knitters." It's fine for a Short; say the precise trades in the long version.
