# Episode 2: The Robot in the Orchestra Pit

**Source:** Precedent P-10, `src/lib/data/book/09-chapter7.md`, plus "The Campaign Has
a Letterhead" from the same chapter.
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 7 to 7.5 minutes (about 1,100 spoken words at a
normal pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Written to be said out loud; change any line that doesn't sound
like you, and treat bracketed lines as directions, not dialogue.

**Tone note.** This episode is about people with a real grievance who lost the argument
they chose and won the one they almost didn't make. It is not a dunk on musicians, and
by extension it is not a dunk on artists worried about AI today. The book's position is
"fight for terms, not against the machine." Keep that respect in the delivery.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you. Then cut to the 1929 ad, full screen: the robot shouldering the harpist
aside.]

> In 1929, America's musicians took out ads in hundreds of newspapers warning the public
> about a robot that was coming for their jobs.
>
> [Beat. Hold on the ad.]
>
> They were right. It did. And what they did next is the most useful thing I know about
> what to do about AI.

[Title card: THE ROBOT IN THE ORCHESTRA PIT.]

### 0:15 The warning shot (about 1 minute)

[On screen: Sousa portrait, then the quote as text.]

> Start in 1906. John Philip Sousa. The march guy. The most famous bandleader alive.
> He writes an essay called "The Menace of Mechanical Music," and his argument is that
> recorded music is going to kill music. Nobody will play at home anymore. The parlor
> piano goes quiet. And, I'm quoting, "the vocal cord will be eliminated by a process of
> evolution, as was the tail of man when he came from the ape."
>
> Here's the thing. Sousa wasn't some crank yelling at the phonograph. He was one of the
> most recorded musicians on the planet. Hundreds of records. And he kept making them
> for another twenty-five years.
>
> Hold onto that. It comes back.

### 1:15 The machine that actually took the jobs (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: silent-film theater with a pit orchestra; *The Jazz Singer* title card.]

> Sousa was early. The real hit came in 1927, with *The Jazz Singer*, the first big
> talking picture.
>
> You have to understand what movie theaters were back then. Every silent film had live
> music. Pianists, organists, full orchestras in the pit. Tens of thousands of musicians
> made a steady living playing under movies.
>
> Sound film made them unnecessary. Within about two years, the pits started emptying
> out.
>
> This wasn't a prediction. It was a payroll, and it was ending.

### 2:15 The campaign (about 1.5 minutes)

[Full screen: the 1930 ad, the robot holding the CANNED MUSIC IN THEATERS banner. Slow
push-in on the coupon at the bottom.]

> So the American Federation of Musicians fought back. And they did everything right.
>
> Starting in 1929 they bought ads in newspapers across the US and Canada. They put more
> than half a million Depression-era dollars behind it. They formed something called the
> Music Defense League. And they picked, as the face of the enemy, a robot.
>
> A literal drawn mechanical man. Week after week. Shoving a harpist out of the way.
> Feeding instruments into a meat grinder. Failing to soothe a crying baby.
>
> And listen to the copy, because you've heard it recently.
>
> [Read slowly, quote on screen.]
>
> "Dehumanizing the theatre may prove to be an exceedingly dangerous move."
>
> "Tho' the Robot can make no music of himself, he can and does arrest the efforts of
> those who can."
>
> At the bottom of the ad, a coupon. Clip it, mail it to 1440 Broadway, and sign up as
> someone "opposed to the elimination of Living Music from the Theatre."
>
> The union said two million people signed up by that spring. Take that as the union's
> number. Even if it was half that, it's one of the biggest organized campaigns against a
> technology in history.
>
> It did not save a single orchestra pit.

### 3:45 The escalation (about 1.5 minutes)

[On screen: Petrillo photo, then a simple timeline: 1942, 1943, 1944, 1948.]

> So they escalated.
>
> In 1940 a guy named James Petrillo takes over the union. And on August 1st, 1942, he
> tells every union musician in America: you will not make a single commercial
> recording. None.
>
> That ban lasted two years and three months. The longest work stoppage in the history
> of American entertainment. Decca caved first, in 1943. RCA Victor and Columbia held out
> until November of 1944. Petrillo did it again in 1948. Congress got so mad they passed
> a federal law aimed at basically one man. People called it the Anti-Petrillo Act.
>
> Two things came out of all of this. Only one of them was on purpose.
>
> The accident: singers weren't in the instrumentalists' union. So during the ban, the
> singers kept recording and the bands couldn't. A strike meant to protect orchestras
> handed the whole decade to vocalists. That's a big part of why the big-band era gave
> way to the era of the singer. Permanently.

### 5:15 The win (about 1 minute)

[On screen: "Music Performance Trust Fund, 1948." Then footage or a photo of a free
concert in a park.]

> The thing they did on purpose was the only thing that worked in twenty years of
> fighting.
>
> A royalty.
>
> The 1948 settlement created the Music Performance Trust Fund. The record labels pay
> into it per record, and the fund pays union musicians to play free public concerts.
>
> It still exists. It's still running today. It takes a cut of streaming now. Every
> time a major-label record gets played, a fraction of a cent goes into a fund created by
> the guys who bought the robot ads, and it pays a live musician to play in a park.
>
> And here's the sting. Recorded music, the robot, the canned music they spent a
> fortune fighting, is now just how musicians get paid. It's how anybody three states
> away ever hears of them. The thing the campaign existed to stop became the thing its
> own members live on. And the institution still paying them is funded by a tax on it.

### 6:15 The turn (about 1 minute)

[Back on you. Slow down for the last four lines.]

> So here's the lesson, and it's not "resistance is futile." These people were
> organized, funded, national, lawful, and they understood the technology better than the
> public did. They did everything a legitimate movement is supposed to do.
>
> And none of it moved the outcome, because they were arguing about the wrong thing. You
> can't make a technology unpopular by proving it has no soul. The public wasn't voting
> on whether records should exist. The public was listening to records.
>
> The only move that produced anything wasn't a defense. It was an invoice.
>
> Argue about the machine's soul, and you get twenty years and half a million dollars of
> nothing. Argue about the machine's bill, and you can build something that outlives
> you.
>
> If AI is coming for your trade, skip the essay about what's being lost. Go straight to
> the clause. Who pays whom. How much. Per what. Forever.

## The Short (under 60 seconds)

Post this a few days before the long version, to test the hook.

[Vertical. Open on the 1929 robot ad full frame, then tight on you.]

> In 1929, musicians bought ads in hundreds of newspapers warning about a robot coming
> for their jobs.
>
> They were right. Sound movies emptied the orchestra pits.
>
> They spent half a million Depression dollars fighting it. The union said two million
> people signed up.
>
> It didn't save a single orchestra pit.
>
> Then in 1948 they tried something else. They didn't argue the machine had no soul.
> They sent it a bill. A royalty on every record, paid into a fund for live musicians.
>
> That fund still pays musicians today.
>
> Don't argue with the machine. Invoice it.

[Text on screen for the last 3 seconds: "Full story: link in bio."]
