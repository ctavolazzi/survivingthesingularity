# Episode 7: Torches of Freedom

**Source:** Precedent P-11, `src/lib/data/book/10-chapter8.md`, plus "The Great
Cognitive Extraction" from the same chapter.
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 4 minutes (about 590 spoken words at a
normal pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Written to be said out loud; change any line that doesn't sound
like you, and treat bracketed lines as directions, not dialogue.

**Tone note.** The women in the parade are not the joke, and neither is the viewer.
The book says the women "experienced it as liberation," and that's the unsettling
part: the campaign worked *because* the freedom was real to them. Keep it there. The
target is the machinery, not the people it runs through. The turn to phones should feel
like "here's how to see it," not "you're a sheep."

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you.]

> In 1929, a tobacco company wanted women to smoke.
>
> So it didn't sell cigarettes. It sold freedom. And the newspapers ran the ad for free,
> as news.

[Title card: TORCHES OF FREEDOM.]

### 0:15 The problem (about 45 seconds)

[B-roll: 1920s New York street scenes, period cigarette ads featuring men.]

> Here's the setup. In 1929, a woman smoking in public in America is a scandal. Men
> smoke. Respectable women don't. Not on the street.
>
> Which means, if you're the American Tobacco Company, half your possible customers are
> locked behind a taboo.
>
> So they hire a guy to pick the lock.

### 1:00 The guy (about 45 seconds)

[On screen: Bernays photo, if cleared; otherwise his name as text.]

> His name is Edward Bernays. He's Sigmund Freud's nephew. And he's the man often called
> the father of public relations.
>
> And Bernays understands something. You don't change what people want by arguing with
> them. You attach your product to something they already want.
>
> And in 1929, what did young women want? Freedom.

### 1:45 The parade (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: 1920s Easter parade footage or photos; Fifth Avenue.]

> Easter Sunday, 1929. New York. The big parade down Fifth Avenue.
>
> Bernays arranges for young women to march in it. And on a cue, they light cigarettes.
> Right in front of photographers. Photographers Bernays also arranged to be standing
> there.
>
> Then he hands the press a phrase: "torches of freedom."
>
> And the papers run it. Not as an ad. As news. Because it looks like news. Women
> striking a blow for equality, right there on Fifth Avenue.
>
> Here's the part I can't shake. The women in that parade experienced it as liberation.
> For them, it was real.
>
> On the company's books, it was inventory expansion.

### 2:45 The mechanism (about 45 seconds)

[Back on you. Slow down.]

> That's how this kind of persuasion works. It doesn't argue with your values. It wears
> them.
>
> It shows up dressed as the thing you already believe in. So it never trips your
> defenses, because the want feels like yours. You picked it. You're proud of it.
>
> The best delivery system for somebody else's agenda is your own identity.

### 3:30 The turn (about 45 seconds)

[Hold up your phone.]

> Now think about what Bernays had. Newspapers. A parade. Some photographers.
>
> The systems pointed at you right now have your location. Your search history. How long
> you paused on a video. And more examples of what makes people click than anyone has
> ever had.
>
> They don't pick your pocket. They pick your wanting.
>
> So before you act on a want, ask one question. Who profits if I feel this?

### 4:15 What to do (about 45 seconds)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> Three things.
>
> One. For one week, every time you get a sudden strong want, to buy something, to be
> furious about something, to check something, write one line. What I want. When it
> showed up. Who profits if I act on it. You don't have to resist anything. Just write
> it down. By day four you'll start seeing the parade routes.
>
> Two. Pick one opinion you feel strongly about now that you didn't a year ago. Trace
> how it got in. Which feed. Which framing. Which part of who you are it flattered.
> Nobody in that 1929 parade thought she was the campaign.
>
> Three. Do it with your hands, today. Notifications off. Phone in a drawer that
> closes. One real thing before noon. Bernays needed you reachable.

[Vertical, tight on you.]

> 1929. It's taboo for women to smoke in public. A tobacco company wants that changed.
>
> So it hires Edward Bernays, Freud's nephew.
>
> He doesn't buy ads. He arranges for young women to light cigarettes in the Easter
> parade, in front of photographers he also arranged.
>
> He calls them "torches of freedom." The papers run it as news.
>
> The women felt liberated. The company sold cigarettes.
>
> The best persuasion doesn't argue with your values. It wears them.
>
> So next time you really want something, ask: who profits if I feel this?
