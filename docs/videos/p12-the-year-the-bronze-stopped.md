# Episode 8: The Year the Bronze Stopped

**Source:** Precedent P-12, `src/lib/data/book/11-chapter9.md`, plus "Deglobalization,
Not Decentralization" and "A Dense City Cannot Feed Itself, and It Does Not Have To"
from the same chapter.
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 4.5 minutes (about 620 spoken words at a normal
pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Written to be said out loud; change any line that doesn't sound
like you, and treat bracketed lines as directions, not dialogue.

**Tone note.** Civilizational collapse is prepper bait, and the book deliberately moved
away from that in v0.7.1: "a wake-up call and an empowerment, not a prepper / off-grid
survival guide," and "resilience, not secession." The script stays there. No bunkers,
no "when it all comes down." The takeaway is shorter supply lines and knowing your
neighbors, framed as good sense in fair weather, not as bracing for doom.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you.]

> Three thousand years ago, the eastern Mediterranean ran on a global supply chain.
>
> Then, around 1177 BC, it broke. And the richest, most connected places on Earth went
> first.

[Title card: THE YEAR THE BRONZE STOPPED.]

### 0:15 The first global economy (about 1 minute)

[On screen: simple map. Cyprus, Egypt, the Hittite lands in Anatolia, Greece, Ugarit on
the Syrian coast, Babylon, with a thin line running east toward Afghanistan.]

> The Bronze Age. And bronze is the whole story, because bronze doesn't exist in nature.
> You make it. Copper plus tin.
>
> The copper, a lot of it, came from Cyprus. The tin came from far away. Some of it from
> as far as what's now Afghanistan.
>
> So every bronze sword, every bronze tool, depended on a trade network
> running thousands of miles. Egypt. The Hittites. Mycenaean Greece. Ugarit. Babylon.
> All specializing. All trading. All getting rich off depending on each other.
>
> It was efficient. It was sophisticated. For its time, it was the most connected the
> world had ever been.

### 1:15 The cascade (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: ruins of Mycenae, Ugarit, Hattusa. Public-domain photos work.]

> And then, around 1177 BC, it failed the way complicated systems always fail. Not one
> big blow. A cascade.
>
> Drought. Then famine. Then people on the move. Then war. Then the trade routes break.
> And every one of those makes the others worse.
>
> Within a couple of generations, almost every great palace in the eastern Mediterranean
> is rubble. In Greece, writing itself disappears. For centuries.
>
> Historians still argue about exactly what caused it. They don't argue about the
> pattern.

### 2:15 Who made it (about 45 seconds)

[B-roll: a small hillside village, fields, a potter or smith working by hand.]

> Here's the part that matters.
>
> The places that fell hardest and fastest were the most connected, most specialized
> economies. The palaces.
>
> The places that made it through were the small, unglamorous communities that could
> feed, clothe, and equip themselves from what was within walking distance.
>
> The palaces died of their supply chains. The villages lived on their soil. Because
> nothing they needed was on a ship.

### 3:00 The mechanism (about 30 seconds)

[Back on you. Slow down.]

> Depending on each other is efficiency when the weather's good.
>
> When the weather turns, it's contagion.
>
> When the tin stopped moving, everything downstream of tin stopped being possible.

### 3:30 The turn (about 45 seconds)

> Now, I'm not telling you to build a bunker. That's not the lesson. The lesson is about
> the length of the line.
>
> Think about how far the stuff in your house traveled to get to you. Your food. Your
> medicine. The parts that keep your car running. Most of us are running a palace
> economy on faith.
>
> Shorter lines aren't nostalgia. A dense city can't grow all its own food, and it
> doesn't have to. But the towns around it can grow a lot more of it. That's not
> hiding from the world. That's making sure a bad season doesn't become a crisis.

### 4:15 What to do (about 45 seconds)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> Three things.
>
> One. Map your tin. List five things your household can't go a month without. A
> medication. A fuel. A food staple. A spare part. Spend twenty minutes tracing where
> each one actually comes from. Pick the most fragile one and set up one local or stored
> backup this season.
>
> Two. Do a neighbor census. Who within walking distance can fix an engine, stitch a
> wound, grow food, wire a panel, weld? If you can't name five people, that's your
> project.
>
> Three. When two options are close, buy the one that's repairable, local, or standard,
> over the one that's slightly better and only comes from one place.
