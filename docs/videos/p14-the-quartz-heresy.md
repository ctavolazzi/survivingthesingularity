# Episode 10: The Quartz Heresy

**Source:** Precedent P-14, `src/lib/data/book/13-chapter11.md` ("Using the Tech to Your
Advantage").
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 4 minutes (about 560 spoken words at a normal
pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Use it as a map of what to say, not lines to read (see the
note at the top of `p13-the-abbots-confession.md`). Bracketed lines are directions.

**Tone note.** Watch people know this story cold, and the popular version ("the Swiss
ignored quartz") is simplified: Swiss labs built early quartz watches too, and a strong
Swiss franc hurt exports in the same years. The script concedes both in one breath at
1:15 and moves on. As with Kodak, the concession makes the real point stronger: the
industry's identity, not its engineering, was the problem.

**Why this one might matter to you personally.** The rule of this episode is "use the
machine to pay for the craft you want to keep." That is close to the shape of your own
business right now: sponsored videos for small shops funding the book and the long
work. If you want a personal line in the turn, that's where it goes. The script leaves
a bracketed spot for it.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you. If you own a Swatch or any cheap quartz watch, wear it.]

> The Swiss watch industry lost two-thirds of its jobs in fifteen years.
>
> What saved it was the cheapest, plastic-est watch they'd ever made. Built with the
> exact technology that nearly killed them.

[Title card: THE QUARTZ HERESY.]

### 0:15 The threat (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: Swiss mechanical movements, a watchmaker at a bench; then a 1970s digital or
quartz watch.]

> 1969. The Swiss own watchmaking. Centuries of mastery. Gears, springs, tiny hand-built
> movements. It's not just an industry, it's national identity.
>
> And that year, a Japanese company, Seiko, ships the first quartz wristwatch. A battery,
> a crystal, a circuit. More accurate. And eventually, way cheaper.
>
> And the Swiss attitude, broadly, is: that's not a real watch. That's a gadget. For
> people with no taste.
>
> They stay pure.

### 1:15 The fair version (about 30 seconds)

> Now, to be fair, because watch people will be in the comments: Swiss labs actually
> built quartz watches too, early. And a strong Swiss franc was hammering their exports
> at the same time.
>
> But the core of it holds. The industry's whole identity was mechanical. And it bet on
> that identity.

### 1:45 The collapse (about 45 seconds)

[On screen: "~90,000 jobs → ~30,000." Then "~1,600 firms → under 600."]

> Between 1970 and the mid-1980s, Swiss watch employment goes from about ninety thousand
> people to about thirty thousand.
>
> The number of Swiss watch companies goes from about sixteen hundred to under six
> hundred.
>
> Two-thirds of an ancient national craft, gone in fifteen years.
>
> Purity cost them the industry.

### 2:30 The heresy (about 1 minute)

[On screen: a 1980s Swatch, bright plastic. Then a high-end mechanical movement.]

> And then, in 1983, a newly merged Swiss watch group does the unthinkable.
>
> It launches the Swatch. Plastic. Cheap. Fifty-one parts. Assembled by machine. And
> it's quartz. The enemy's technology.
>
> Everything Swiss watchmaking said it stood against, in one bright plastic package.
>
> And it sells like crazy. And here's the twist: the money from that cheap quartz watch
> helps keep the high-end Swiss watchmakers alive. And in the nineties, fancy
> mechanical Swiss watches come roaring back as luxury.
>
> The Swatch was a Trojan horse. It paid for the exact thing it looked like it was
> destroying.

### 3:30 The turn (about 45 seconds)

[Back on you.]

> So here's the lesson for right now. You don't beat the new machine by refusing it. You
> beat it by using it to pay for the thing you actually want to keep.
>
> Use the AI to fund the workshop. The garden. The human craft. That's not selling out.
> That's a strategy with a documented win.
>
> [Optional personal line, CT's call: how your own work does this.]

### 4:15 What to do (about 45 seconds)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> Three things.
>
> One. Write two lines. Line one: the thing you refuse to lose. Your craft. Your
> standard. Line two: the cheap, fast, machine-powered version of your skill that could
> pay for it. If line two feels a little like betrayal, you wrote it right.
>
> Two. Decide what share of the money from line two goes to line one. Write it down like
> a tax you owe yourself. The Swatch only worked because the money actually flowed back
> up to the watchmakers.
>
> Three. Next time you hear "real professionals don't use AI," translate it. "Real
> watchmakers don't do quartz." Then remember what happened to the companies that agreed.

## The Short (under 60 seconds)

Post this a few days before the long version, to test the hook.

[Vertical, tight on you, holding a cheap quartz watch.]

> 1969: Seiko ships the first quartz watch. The Swiss, who own watchmaking, mostly call
> it a gadget.
>
> Fifteen years later, Swiss watch jobs have fallen by two-thirds.
>
> What saved them? This. A cheap plastic quartz watch. The Swatch. Fifty-one parts,
> made by machine.
>
> The enemy's technology. And its profits helped pay for the fancy mechanical watches to
> come back.
>
> Don't refuse the machine. Make it pay for what you want to keep.
