## Read this first: what the book leaves out

The book tells the Homestead Act as a model of acting during a crisis: "one of the
largest transfers of position in the country's history." It does not mention **whose
land it was.** Much of the land opened by the Homestead Acts had been taken from Native
nations by treaty, coercion and war, often in the same years. Appendix B 148, the
book's own source, covers this.

A video that holds up the Homestead Act as a win without saying so will be called out
in the comments, correctly, and it would undercut the book's whole reputation for
checking itself. So the script says it plainly at 2:00, in your voice, and then makes
the narrower point that survives it: **the window opens during the confusion, and who
gets let into the filing office is a political choice.** That reading is also closer to
the chapter's own ending, which is about community land trusts taking ground *off* the
speculative market.

**Consider fixing the book too.** P-15 in `14-chapter12.md` would be stronger with one
sentence acknowledging this. That's your call and a source edit, not something this
script can do.

**Second caution: this is not financial advice.** The book's rule says rural land
near metros "has not yet been repriced." On a public video, say it as your view, not a
prediction, and keep the "not financial advice" line in.

## Fact sheet (check before filming)

Numbers refer to Appendix B.

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| The Homestead Act was signed in May 1862, about a year into the Civil War | 148 |
| It offered 160 acres to people who lived on and improved the land for five years, for a small filing fee. Claims opened January 1, 1863 | 148 |
| Eligibility included women who were heads of household, and immigrants who had declared intent to become citizens | 148. **Check the exact wording**: it was heads of household or adults 21+, and anyone who had borne arms against the US was excluded |
| Much of the land had been taken from Native nations | 148 |
| Roughly 1.6 million homestead claims were eventually proved, covering about 270 million acres | **VERIFY** against 148 before using the numbers |
| Many claims were never proved up, and speculators and railroads got large amounts of land too | **VERIFY** against 148; say it generally if you can't confirm figures |
| Modern programs: rural development grants, agricultural exemptions, county land banks, relocation incentives, conservation leases | Book text (ch12). **Name a specific program only if you've checked it's current** |

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you.]

> In May 1862, the United States was a year into the Civil War. And in the middle of
> it, the government passed a law to give away land. A hundred and sixty acres at a
> time.
>
> The people who filed during the chaos owned it when the chaos ended.

[Title card: ONE HUNDRED SIXTY ACRES.]

### 0:15 The deal (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: 1860s and 1870s homestead photos, sod houses, land office images.]

> The Homestead Act. The deal was simple. Live on the land. Improve it. Stay five
> years. And a hundred and sixty acres is yours.
>
> And it wasn't only for rich men. Women who headed a household could file. Immigrants
> who'd declared they were becoming citizens could file.
>
> And here's the thing. This is 1862. Every newspaper, every conversation, is the war.
> Most people aren't thinking about paperwork at a land office.
>
> Some people were. And they filed.

### 1:15 The mechanism (about 45 seconds)

[Back on you.]

> That's the pattern. When the world changes, land and position get repriced first.
> And that window opens during the confusion. Not after it.
>
> By the time things feel clear again, the deeds are filed.
>
> "Clarity" is what we call the moment when the good positions are gone.

### 2:00 Whose land it was (about 45 seconds)

[Hold on you. No B-roll. Say it straight.]

> Now I have to say the part the textbook version skips.
>
> A lot of that land wasn't empty. It had been taken from Native nations. By treaties,
> by pressure, by war. Some of it in those same years.
>
> So when I say "the filing office was open," I have to be honest about who it was open
> to, and who it was taken from.
>
> And that's actually the real lesson. The window opens during a crisis. But who gets
> let through it is a choice. It's political. It's always been political.

### 2:45 The turn (about 1 minute)

> So why does this matter now?
>
> Because I think we're in a window like that right now. My view, and this is not
> financial advice: a lot of rural land near cities hasn't been priced for the world
> that's coming. And while everybody's watching the front page, the office is quiet.
>
> But the position doesn't have to mean one person buying a big lot. It can mean a
> lease. Joining a co-op's ground. A community land trust that takes land off the
> speculative market for good. Or committing to improve exactly where you are.
>
> The question isn't "should I buy land." It's "am I going to decide while the office is
> quiet, or wait for clarity."

### 3:45 What to do (about 1 minute)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> Three things.
>
> One. Spend one evening doing real research. Three counties. Actual listings. Actual
> price per acre. Water. Zoning. Put it in a spreadsheet. Not to buy. Just to turn
> "someday, land" from a mood into a document with numbers in it.
>
> Two. Put a date on it. The homesteaders had a five-year clock. Pick a date by which
> you'll decide: buy, lease, join a co-op's ground, or commit to where you are.
>
> Three. Look at what your county is offering right now. Rural development grants. Land
> banks. Conservation leases. These programs exist, and they're quiet for the same
> reason the 1862 office was quiet. Everybody's watching the news.

## The Short (under 60 seconds)

Post this a few days before the long version, to test the hook.

[Vertical, tight on you.]

> 1862. America is a year into the Civil War. And the government passes a law to give
> away land. A hundred and sixty acres, if you live on it for five years.
>
> Everybody's watching the war. A few people are watching the land office.
>
> The people who filed during the chaos owned it after.
>
> And yes, a lot of that land was taken from Native nations. Who gets through the door
> is always a choice.
>
> But the pattern holds. The window opens during the confusion, not after it.

[Text on screen for the last 3 seconds: "Full story: link in bio."]
