# Episode 12: The House That Came by Mail

**Source:** Precedent P-16, `src/lib/data/book/15-chapter13.md` ("The Shouse Protocol").
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 3.5 minutes (about 500 spoken words at a normal
pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Use it as a map of what to say, not lines to read (see the
note at the top of `p13-the-abbots-confession.md`). Bracketed lines are directions.

**Safety note, keep it in.** An episode that says "you don't need a professional" can
get someone hurt. The book's own practice draws the line: "Sometimes the answer is real
(structural welds...): know your lane," and "permitted where permits apply." The script
keeps both on camera. Electrical, gas and structural work are where the line is
brightest; say so.

**Business note.** This video is about building for yourself. Don't frame anything as
you building for, or managing builds for, viewers. That keeps it clear of the
contractor-licensing questions Johnny Autoseed has already had to work through.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you. If you can, stand in front of a Sears kit house. Many towns have one.]

> For about thirty years, you could order a house from the Sears catalog.
>
> It showed up on a train. In pieces. Numbered. With instructions.
>
> And thousands of them are still standing.

[Title card: THE HOUSE THAT CAME BY MAIL.]

### 0:15 The kit (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: catalog pages from the Sears Modern Homes catalogs; a house under
construction.]

> Starting in 1908, Sears sold houses. Not plans. The actual house.
>
> You picked a design out of a catalog. Tiny cottage, big family home, whatever. And a
> while later it arrived at your nearest rail depot. Precut lumber, every piece
> numbered. Nails. Paint. Fixtures. And an instruction book.
>
> Pour a foundation, get a few capable friends, and you could put up a real, permanent
> house.
>
> Sears sold tens of thousands of them. Hundreds of designs.

### 1:15 What the industry said (about 45 seconds)

> And you can guess the argument against it. Housing is too complicated. Too important.
> Too dangerous to hand to amateurs.
>
> The houses answered that. Thousands of them are still standing, still lived in, more
> than a hundred years later.
>
> [Optional, if confirmed: "And to be fair, plenty of people hired a local builder to
> put the kit together. But even then, the kit did a lot of the thinking."]

### 2:00 The mechanism (about 45 seconds)

[Back on you.]

> Here's what I think was really going on.
>
> The expertise moved. It used to live in the builder's head. Sears put it in the
> parts and the manual. Precut, numbered, step by step.
>
> A kit plus instructions is how a skill escapes the gatekeepers. The customer who
> everybody said was unqualified turns out to have been qualified all along. Just not
> licensed.

### 2:45 The turn (about 45 seconds)

> And it's happening again. Metal building kits. Solar kits. Prefab panels. Builds you
> can mostly do yourself and fully understand.
>
> So whenever someone tells you a thing requires their permission and their price, go
> look for the version that ships as a kit. It existed in 1908. It exists now.
>
> But, and this matters: know your lane. Some things really do need a pro. Structural
> welds. Gas. A lot of electrical. Get the permits where permits apply. The goal is to
> understand your house, not to get hurt proving a point.

### 3:30 What to do (about 45 seconds)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> Three things.
>
> One. Pick one thing you pay someone for, a shed, a fence, a greenhouse, a small solar
> setup, and do the kit version this year. Permitted, documented as you go.
>
> Two. Next time you hear "you need a professional for that," ask: what exactly does the
> professional know, and where is it written down? Sometimes the answer's real. Often
> it's a manual and some confidence. Learn to tell the difference.
>
> Three. Leave a kit behind. Photograph your steps. List the parts and prices. Write the
> one page you wish you'd had. Give it to the next person.
