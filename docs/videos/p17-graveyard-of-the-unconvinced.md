# Episode 3: The Graveyard of the Unconvinced

**Source:** Precedent P-17, `src/lib/data/book/16-chapter14.md`, with Precedent P-18
("The Mirror Twin," same chapter) as the ending.
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 6.5 to 7 minutes (about 950 spoken words at a
normal pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Written to be said out loud; change any line that doesn't sound
like you, and treat bracketed lines as directions, not dialogue.

**Tone note.** The comment section on this topic is full of people who worked at Kodak,
Sears or Blockbuster, or who know the "Kodak hid the digital camera" story is
oversimplified. Snopes (Appendix B 107) says exactly that. The script deals with it
head-on in 1:30 rather than leaving it for the comments, because conceding the nuance
makes the real point stronger: Kodak didn't miss digital, it couldn't let digital eat
film.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you. Hold up a phone.]

> In 1975, a 24-year-old engineer at Kodak built the world's first digital camera.
>
> He showed his bosses. And according to him, what they said was: "That's cute. But
> don't tell anyone about it."

[Title card: THE GRAVEYARD OF THE UNCONVINCED.]

### 0:15 Kodak (about 1.5 minutes)

[B-roll: photos of the Sasson prototype if rights allow; otherwise a phone camera
shutter and old Kodak film boxes.]

> His name is Steve Sasson. The camera weighed eight pounds. It shot at one hundredth of
> a megapixel. It took twenty-three seconds to save one photo, [VERIFY: to a cassette
> tape].
>
> Ridiculous machine. Also the future.
>
> And the reason it went in a drawer wasn't that Kodak's executives were dumb. It's that
> they were good at math. Kodak didn't really make money on cameras. It made money on
> everything after you pressed the button. Film. Chemicals. Paper. Developing. And
> Sasson's box used none of it.
>
> Now, in fairness, and you'll see this in the comments if I don't say it: the "Kodak hid
> the digital camera" story is more complicated than the legend. Kodak did go on to make
> digital cameras. It held a lot of the patents.
>
> [VERIFY against Appendix B 107 before filming: adjust this paragraph to match what
> Snopes actually says.]
>
> But that's the point. Kodak didn't miss digital. It saw digital more clearly than
> anybody on Earth, and it could not bring itself to let digital eat film. Because film
> was the business.
>
> Kodak filed for bankruptcy in 2012. The same decade that almost every human being
> started carrying a digital camera in their pocket.

### 1:45 Three more headstones (about 2 minutes)

[Quick cuts. Each company gets a title card with its year.]

> And Kodak's not alone. It's got neighbors.
>
> **Sears. 1993.** For decades, Sears basically was Amazon. A catalog of everything,
> mailed to millions of homes, delivered to the middle of nowhere. You could buy a whole
> house from Sears. In 1993 the catalog was still bringing in three point three billion
> dollars, and they killed it, to focus on mall stores.
>
> Amazon was founded the next year.
>
> Jeff Bezos basically pulled the everything-store playbook out of the trash where Sears
> had just thrown it, and added a web browser.
>
> **Blockbuster. 2000.** The founders of Netflix fly to Dallas and offer to sell their
> company to Blockbuster for fifty million dollars. By their account, they got laughed
> out of the room. Blockbuster's CEO later disputed the laughing. Nobody disputes the
> answer was no.
>
> And here's why. Blockbuster made a huge amount of its money on late fees. It was
> literally getting paid by its customers' annoyance. And Netflix's entire pitch was: no
> more late fees.
>
> Blockbuster went bankrupt in 2010.
>
> **Borders. 2001.** The bookstore chain decides online shopping is a distraction, so it
> hands its whole online store to Amazon. Every Borders customer who bought online was
> an Amazon customer wearing a Borders name tag. Borders took its website back in 2008.
> Liquidated in 2011.

### 3:45 The mechanism (about 1 minute)

[Back on you. Slow down.]

> So look at the list again.
>
> Kodak invented the thing that killed it. Sears owned the playbook. Blockbuster had
> the offer on the table. Borders signed the partnership.
>
> Not one of these companies was blind.
>
> An incumbent doesn't die because it can't see the wave. It dies because it owns too
> much beachfront property to admit the tide is real.
>
> And every one of them had the same tell. Somewhere in the business was a line item
> where it charged people for its own inconvenience. Late fees. Film you had to buy and
> develop. Middlemen you had to go through.
>
> That line item is a countdown clock.

### 4:45 The one that lived (about 1 minute)

[On screen: two columns, Kodak and Fujifilm, both starting at 2000.]

> Here's the part that makes all of this a choice instead of fate.
>
> Kodak had a twin. Fujifilm. Same business, same film, same collapse, on the same
> schedule. [VERIFY: Fujifilm's film business peaked around 2000.]
>
> But Fujifilm asked a different question. Not "how do we save film." It asked: what is
> our chemistry actually good at?
>
> [VERIFY the next three lines.] Coatings. Collagen, because film is mostly gelatin.
> Chemicals that stop photos from fading. And it took those exact skills into cosmetics,
> medicine, and the films inside LCD screens.
>
> Kodak thought it was in the film business. Fujifilm figured out it was in the
> chemistry business. The wave destroyed the product. It couldn't touch the skills.

### 5:45 The turn (about 1 minute)

> So here's why I'm telling you this now.
>
> AI is a wave, and most of us are standing on some beachfront. A skill, a job title, a
> business line, a credential.
>
> Three things I'd do this week.
>
> One. Write down everything you'd have to admit is losing value if this wave is real.
> Then write what admitting it would cost you today. That second column is why Kodak said
> "that's cute."
>
> Two. Find your drawer. Everybody has a Sasson camera somewhere. The side project you
> shelved because it didn't fit how you currently get paid. Take it out. Give it one
> honest weekend.
>
> Three. Do the Fujifilm list. One page, no job titles allowed. Not "accountant." Not
> "barista." What are you actually good at? Explaining hard things? Calming angry people?
> Building things precisely? That's what survives the wave. Your product is mortal. Your
> skills travel.
