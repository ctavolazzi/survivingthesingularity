# Episode 5: Twenty Million Gardens

**Source:** Precedent P-19, `src/lib/data/book/17-chapter15.md`, plus the book's
central question from the Introduction (`02-introduction.md`, line 43) and "The Robot in
the Soil: FarmBot and Biological Refineries" (`11-chapter9.md`).
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 4.5 to 5 minutes (about 680 spoken words at a normal
pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Written to be said out loud; change any line that doesn't sound
like you, and treat bracketed lines as directions, not dialogue.

**Tone note.** This is the hopeful episode of the first five, and it should feel like
it. Episodes 1 to 4 are about people who got it wrong. This one is about twenty million
ordinary people who got it right in two growing seasons. The book's own credibility
rule applies: it walked back its "300% yield" overclaims to "a garden will not usually
cover full caloric needs." Don't promise viewers a yard can feed them. The 1944 number is
about fresh vegetables, not total food, and the script says so.

**Johnny Autoseed decision (CT's call).** The book was deliberately scrubbed of Johnny
Autoseed so it stands as your own work. The video is a different surface. The script
includes one optional bracketed line at 4:30 that mentions it. Leave it out and the
episode still works; put it in and it's the first bridge from the book audience to the
company. Nothing else in the script names the company.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you. Standing in a garden if you have one, even a small one.]

> In 1944, backyard gardens grew about forty percent of the fresh vegetables Americans
> ate.
>
> Not farms. Not agribusiness. Yards.
>
> And almost nobody knows it happened.

[Title card: TWENTY MILLION GARDENS.]

### 0:15 The request (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: Victory Garden posters, 1940s photos of city gardens and schoolyard plots.]

> Here's the setup. It's World War Two, and the war is straining the whole industrial
> food system.
>
> So the United States government does something that sounds insane now. It asks
> regular people to grow food. Office workers. Schoolkids. Grandmothers. People who had
> never grown a tomato in their lives.
>
> And this wasn't a morale thing. It wasn't a nice poster to make people feel involved.
> It was logistics. The country actually needed the vegetables.

### 1:15 What happened (about 1 minute)

[On screen: "~20,000,000 gardens." Then "~40% of fresh vegetables, 1944."]

> Roughly twenty million Victory Gardens show up. Backyards. Vacant lots. Schoolyards.
> Rooftops.
>
> And by 1944 they're producing something like forty percent of all the fresh
> vegetables consumed in the country.
>
> Read that again. Forty percent. From people the food industry would have called
> hopeless amateurs the year before.
>
> And it took about two growing seasons. A whole parallel food system, stood up in
> roughly two years, by people who were figuring it out as they went.

### 2:15 And then it vanished (about 45 seconds)

[B-roll: a postwar supermarket aisle; an empty lot.]

> Then the war ends. The posters stop. The marketing stops. And the whole thing just
> evaporates.
>
> Twenty million gardens, gone. Not because they stopped working. Because everybody
> stopped being asked.
>
> Which is why, eighty years later, most people think food comes from a truck.

### 3:00 The mechanism (about 1 minute)

[Back on you. Slow down.]

> Here's what I think is going on, and it's the opposite of every other story in this
> series.
>
> Every other episode, somebody important was wrong about what was coming. This time,
> nobody had to predict anything. The capacity was just sitting there. In ordinary dirt, in ordinary
> hands, dormant, the whole time.
>
> Twenty million small gardens came online faster than anybody could have built one
> giant farm, because nobody had to build anything. It already existed. It was waiting
> for somebody to say "go."
>
> The bottleneck was never ability. It was the decision to start.

### 4:00 The turn (about 1 minute)

[On screen: FarmBot footage or the chapter 15 FarmBot image (CC BY 4.0, credit on
screen).]

> So here's why this matters right now.
>
> In 1943 the hard part was labor. Weeding, watering, planting, every day, by hand.
> That's what twenty million people volunteered for.
>
> That part is changing. There are open-source robots that [VERIFY: plant, water and
> weed] a backyard bed right now. You can build one.
>
> [Optional, CT's call: "That's a big part of why I started Johnny Autoseed."]
>
> And that's the question my whole book is built around. If a robot can grow my food
> and build my house, why am I still renting my survival?
>
> Now, real talk. A backyard garden is not going to cover all your calories. That's not
> the claim. The claim is that the soil near your door is standby capacity, and the
> record shows it can come online at national scale in about two years, if people have
> already made their first-year mistakes.

### 5:00 What to do (about 45 seconds)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> So. Three things, starting this season.
>
> One. Start one node. One raised bed. Three buckets on a balcony. A community garden
> plot. Weigh what you harvest. It'll be small. That's fine. The Victory Gardens weren't
> built by experts. They were built by twenty million beginners.
>
> Two. Make it a network. Find the two or three people nearest you who grow anything.
> Trade one plant or one skill with them. Learn their names.
>
> Three. Keep it warm. Save seed from one crop. Grow at least one thing every single
> year. A capability you never use isn't a capability. It's a rumor.
