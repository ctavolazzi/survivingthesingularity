# Episode 14: Seventy-Nine Pages

**Source:** Precedent P-20, `src/lib/data/book/18-chapter16.md` ("Digital Leverage and
Media Autonomy").
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 3.5 minutes (about 500 spoken words at a
normal pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Use it as a map of what to say, not lines to read (see the
note at the top of `p13-the-abbots-confession.md`). Bracketed lines are directions.

**Do not say any of these.** They are the most repeated facts about *Common Sense*, and
the book printed a correction of record because none of them survive checking (Appendix
B, the note above entry 193):

- "It sold 120,000 copies" / "150,000" / "half a million." These trace to no source.
  Trish Loughran puts the far upper limit at 75,000, and believes the real number was
  well below it.
- "It was 47 pages." The page count depends on the edition. Robert Bell's first
  printing is 79 pages.
- "Proportionally the most widely read thing ever printed in America." It rests on the
  unsourced sales numbers.

Say instead: **"about twenty-five printings in its first year,"** which is the hardest
number available and comes from the skeptic's own count. If a commenter posts "500,000
copies," the correction is a great reply.

**Why this one is personal.** The chapter's practice is "own your printing press": an
email list and a site you control, not just a platform account. That's what this channel
is for. The ask at the end leans into that honestly.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you. If you can, hold a printed copy of *Common Sense*.]

> In January 1776, a man who'd been in America for about a year published a
> seventy-nine-page pamphlet.
>
> He had no office, no money, no army, and no famous name.
>
> What he had was a printing press.

[Title card: SEVENTY-NINE PAGES.]

### 0:15 The nobody (about 45 seconds)

[B-roll: the Robert Bell title page (Library of Congress), colonial Philadelphia prints.]

> His name was Thomas Paine. And on paper, he was nobody. A corset-maker's son. A failed
> tax officer back in England. No title. No connections worth mentioning.
>
> And he wrote *Common Sense*.
>
> Seventy-nine pages, in the first printing. And he wrote it plain. Not for scholars.
> For regular people. In language you could read out loud to your neighbors.

### 1:00 What happened (about 45 seconds)

[On screen: "~25 printings in the first year."]

> It ran through about twenty-five printings in its first year.
>
> Now, you'll see huge sales numbers for this pamphlet online. Half a million copies,
> that kind of thing. Those numbers don't hold up. Historians have checked. Twenty-five
> printings is the real, solid number, and it's plenty.
>
> Because what it did was bigger than sales. It took a complaint about taxes and turned
> it into an argument for independence. It changed what ordinary people believed was
> possible.
>
> The most powerful empire on Earth, with the biggest navy in the world, couldn't answer
> seventy-nine pages in time.

### 1:45 The mechanism (about 45 seconds)

[Back on you.]

> Here's why it worked. Paine didn't need anybody's permission. One printer. That's it.
> No institution signing off. No gatekeeper.
>
> Leverage is reach divided by permission.
>
> And every time a new medium makes reach cheap, the first people to notice get influence
> way out of proportion to their credentials. And the institutions that still think
> credentials are the bottleneck don't see it coming.

### 2:30 The turn (about 30 seconds)

> You've got a printing press Paine couldn't have imagined. It's in your pocket. So does
> everybody else.
>
> Which is exactly why most people use it to consume instead of publish.

### 3:00 What to do (about 45 seconds)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> Three things.
>
> One. Write your seventy-nine pages. Or your four. The one thing you know from real
> experience that would help somebody two years behind you. Plain words. What happened,
> what you'd do differently. Publish it this month.
>
> Two. Own your press. A social account is a stall in somebody else's market. An email
> list and a site you control are a printing press. Set them up now, even if nine people
> are on the list.
>
> Three. Read your writing out loud. Every sentence you trip on, every word you'd never
> say to a neighbor over the fence, rewrite it.
