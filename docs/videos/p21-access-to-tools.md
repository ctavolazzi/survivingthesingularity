# Episode 15: Access to Tools

**Source:** Precedent P-21, `src/lib/data/book/19-chapter17.md` ("Tools of the Trade").
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 3.5 minutes (about 480 spoken words at a normal
pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Use it as a map of what to say, not lines to read (see the
note at the top of `p13-the-abbots-confession.md`). Bracketed lines are directions.

**Tone note.** This is a hopeful episode, like P-19. Nobody failed. It's a story about
one person curating tools for strangers, and it ends by asking the viewer to do the same
for one person. It's also the closest precedent in the book to what Johnny Autoseed
says it does ("access paths" to autonomous tools). The book deliberately doesn't name
the company, so any mention is your call; there's an optional line in the practice
section.

**One overclaim to avoid.** The book says the commune and the personal computer "both
came out of" the catalog's pages. Its influence on early personal-computing culture is
well documented; "came out of" is stronger than that. The script says "influenced."

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you.]

> In 1968, a guy put a photo of the whole Earth on a black paperback cover, and under it
> he printed three words.
>
> Access to Tools.
>
> Steve Jobs later called it Google, thirty-five years before Google.

[Title card: ACCESS TO TOOLS.]

### 0:15 The catalog (about 1 minute)

[B-roll: Whole Earth Catalog covers and interior pages, if cleared; otherwise tools on
a workbench.]

> His name was Stewart Brand, and he made the *Whole Earth Catalog*.
>
> And it wasn't really a store. It was a list. Welding gear. How to do the math for a
> geodesic dome. Seed suppliers. Calculators. Books on midwifery right next to books on
> computers.
>
> The idea, in his words, was to help a person "conduct his own education, find his own
> inspiration, shape his own environment."
>
> And it didn't separate the farm from the computer. The same readers were building
> homesteads and tinkering with the first personal computers. The catalog influenced
> both.

### 1:15 Jobs (about 30 seconds)

> Years later, in 2005, Steve Jobs gave a commencement speech at Stanford. And he ended
> it talking about this catalog. He called it "sort of like Google in paperback form,
> thirty-five years before Google came along."
>
> [If verified: "His last words to those graduates, stay hungry, stay foolish, came off
> the back of its final issue."]

### 1:45 The mechanism (about 30 seconds)

[Back on you.]

> Here's what Brand understood.
>
> People don't become capable by getting credentialed. They become capable by getting
> access, and then getting started.
>
> The tools already existed. They were just scattered and invisible. The catalog made
> them findable. Curation was the whole product.
>
> A catalog of tools is secretly a map of who you could be.

### 2:15 What to do (about 1 minute)

[Numbers on screen as you say them.]

> So here's how to make your own. Three things.
>
> One. Tonight, write a one-page list of every tool you own that you actually
> understand, can fix yourself, and can't be locked out of. Be strict. If you can only
> use it, it doesn't count. The page will be shorter than you think. That's fine. The
> gaps are your curriculum.
>
> Two. Close one gap a month. Learn one tool down to the repair level, or swap one thing
> you rent for one thing you own. Do that for a year and you're a different person.
>
> Three. Be somebody's access. Brand's genius wasn't owning tools. It was listing them
> for people who didn't know where to start. Publish your list. Lend a tool. Run one
> workshop for your street.
>
> [Optional, CT's call: "That's a big part of what I'm trying to do with Johnny
> Autoseed."]
>
> The catalog made builders out of readers. Your one page can do that for one person.
> And one is how it always starts.
