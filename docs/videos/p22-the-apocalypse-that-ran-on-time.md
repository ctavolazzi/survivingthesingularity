# Episode 16: The Apocalypse That Ran On Time

**Source:** Precedent P-22, `src/lib/data/book/20-chapter18.md` ("The Roadmap and the
Premortem Pivot").
**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).
See `src/lib/data/book/VOICE-GUIDE.md`.
**Target length:** long version about 3.5 minutes (about 470 spoken words at a normal
pace). Short under 60 seconds.
**Status:** draft for CT. Use it as a map of what to say, not lines to read (see the
note at the top of `p13-the-abbots-confession.md`). Bracketed lines are directions.

**Keep the caveat.** The book is careful here: "some countries that spent little also
passed quietly, so the true size of the averted disaster is genuinely contested." The
script says that out loud at 1:30. It's what makes the episode trustworthy, and it
doesn't weaken the point: the lesson is about judging decisions by the reasoning before
the deadline, not about proving exactly how bad Y2K would have been.

**Tone note.** "Prepare for the transition" can slide into prepper territory. The
book's practice keeps it grounded: skills, soil, energy, savings, community, and a
date-stamped notebook. The script stays there.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you.]

> The world spent roughly three hundred billion dollars to make sure nothing happened
> on January 1st, 2000.
>
> And nothing happened.
>
> And a lot of people decided that meant it was all a waste.

[Title card: THE APOCALYPSE THAT RAN ON TIME.]

### 0:15 The bug (about 45 seconds)

[B-roll: late-90s news graphics, old computer terminals, a "2000" countdown.]

> The Y2K bug was real. For decades, to save memory, a lot of software stored the year
> as two digits. Ninety-eight. Ninety-nine.
>
> So what happens at midnight when it rolls over to "00"? A lot of those machines might
> read it as 1900. And those machines ran banks. Power systems. Air traffic. Payroll.

### 1:00 The fix (about 30 seconds)

[On screen: "~$300 billion worldwide. ~$100 billion in the US."]

> So instead of waiting to see what broke, the world assumed it would break, and worked
> backward. Audit the code. Patch it. Test it. Before the deadline.
>
> Roughly three hundred billion dollars worldwide. About a hundred billion in the US.
>
> Midnight came. Planes flew. Banks opened. The disaster turned into trivia.

### 1:30 The punchline (about 45 seconds)

> And then came the strangest part. Because nothing happened, a lot of people concluded
> nothing would have happened. It was hype. Hysteria.
>
> Now, to be fair: some countries that spent very little also got through fine. So how
> big the disaster would have been is honestly debated.
>
> But sit with this. One of the biggest acts of prevention in history is remembered by a
> lot of people as an overreaction.

### 2:15 The mechanism (about 30 seconds)

[Back on you.]

> That's the thing about prevention. When it works, it's invisible.
>
> From the outside, a plan that worked looks exactly like paranoia that wasn't needed.
> Because the disaster you prevented never shows up to testify.

### 2:45 The turn and what to do (about 1 minute)

> So here's the warning for anybody preparing for what's coming with AI. If you prepare
> well, and your life doesn't get wrecked, people will say you overreacted.
>
> Let them. Judge your preparation by how good your reasoning was before the deadline.
> Not by the silence after it. The silence is the product.
>
> Three things.
>
> One. Run a household Y2K. One hour. Everyone affected in the room. Ask: "It's a
> couple of years from now and our income got cut in half. What broke first?" List the
> top three. Fix one this month. Just one.
>
> Two. Pick a preparation budget and write it down. Some percentage of your income and
> hours going to skills, soil, energy, savings, and your community.
>
> Three. Date your reasoning. Every big decision gets one paragraph in a notebook. What
> you knew. What you assumed. Why you acted. With the date. So when someone says you
> overreacted, you can judge yourself by what you knew then.
