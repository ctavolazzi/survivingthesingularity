**Register:** The Argument (first person, contractions, short hammers after long runs).

**Target length:** long version about 4 minutes (about 600 spoken words at a normal

**Status:** draft for CT. Use it as a map of what to say, not lines to read (see the

## Read this first

**1. This is the finale, so it closes the loop.** The Precedent Ledger ends here in the
book, and this episode ends the series by calling back to Episode 1 (the 1903 New York
Times editorial). It works best posted after at least a handful of the others.

**2. The 2027 line, a decision for you.** The book's rule for P-23 says flatly: "The
tipping point is 2027." But Episode 1, built on the book's own P-03, says "I'm not
telling you the date. Nobody knows the date," and P-03's rule is that forecasts miss by
orders of magnitude. Saying "2027" as fact in the finale would contradict the first
episode on camera. **The script leaves the date out.** If you want it in, say it as
your view ("my bet is 2027") rather than a fact. It's also worth deciding which way the
book itself should read, since P-03 and P-23 pull against each other.

**3. Keep it generous to the three critics.** Stoll and Krugman both publicly owned
their mistakes later, and the book quotes them doing it. That's the classy version of
this story and it's more persuasive than a dunk. The book's conclusion also has some
heated language ("cowards," "capitalist goblins"); it isn't used here.

## Long version

### 0:00 Cold open (15 seconds)

[Tight on you.]

> In the year 2000, a British newspaper printed this headline: "Internet may be just a
> passing fad as millions give up on it."
>
> That's the last case in my book. And I think it's the most important one.

[Title card: THE PASSING FAD.]

### 0:15 Three receipts (about 1.5 minutes)

[On screen: each headline as its own card, with the date.]

> The book ends with three receipts.
>
> One. February 1995, *Newsweek*. An astronomer named Clifford Stoll, a guy who'd been
> online for twenty years, writes an essay called "The Internet? Bah!" Online shopping?
> Digital news? Working from home? His word: "baloney." The internet, he says, is "one
> big ocean of unedited data."
>
> Two. June 1998. Paul Krugman, who'd later win the Nobel Prize in economics, writes
> that by about 2005 it'll be clear the internet's impact on the economy was "no greater
> than the fax machine's."
>
> Three. December 2000. The *Daily Mail*. "Internet may be just a passing fad as
> millions give up on it." People were bored, the story said. It was slow. It cost too
> much.

### 1:45 They were right (about 1 minute)

[Back on you. Slow down here.]

> And here's what I want you to notice. These weren't idiots. They were smart,
> credentialed people.
>
> And every specific complaint they made was true when they made it. The modems were
> slow. Search was bad. Getting online was expensive.
>
> They described the technology's annoyances perfectly, at the exact moment those
> annoyances were being engineered away. They mistook the worst version of the thing for
> the final version.
>
> And to their credit, two of them owned it later. Stoll called it his most public
> "howler." Krugman said he was "trying to be provocative, and got it wrong."

### 2:45 The mechanism (about 30 seconds)

> A technology that's compounding is always the worst it will ever be. Today.
>
> Critics review the version in front of them. Builders build for the next one. Both are
> describing something real. Only one of them is describing the future.

### 3:15 The turn and what to do (about 1 minute)

> You're reading this decade's version of those three articles every week, about AI.
> Smart people. Accurate complaints.
>
> So here's how to read them. Three things.
>
> One. Start a receipts file. Every time someone credentialed calls AI a bubble or a
> fad, save it. Headline, author, date. Set a reminder to reread it in a few years. Not
> to gloat. To build your own immunity to confident dismissal.
>
> Two. Learn one distinction. Is this complaint an annoyance, something engineers are
> clearly grinding away at? Or is it a limit, something physics or math actually
> forbids? Slow modems were an annoyance. The speed of light is a limit. Only limits
> should change your plan.
>
> Three. If you've watched any of this series, go back and pick the one practice that
> made you most uncomfortable. That discomfort is pointing at your beachfront. Do that
> one first.

### 4:15 The close (30 seconds)

[Tight on you. This is the end of the series; let it land.]

> Remember the first episode? The New York Times said a flying machine would take a
> million years. Sixty-nine days later, it flew.
>
> So many of these stories end the same way. The essayists kept writing. The builders
> kept building. And the decade sorted them.
>
> It's sorting us now.
>
> Stop arguing. Stop waiting. Start building.
