# Beats like the choice, 2026-11-19

You asked for more beats like his mother's stroke. What made that one work:

1. It took things already on the page (the 9:40 call, the pot, the back door) and turned them.
2. It made a character **do** the thing the book is about, which is deciding for someone else.
3. It tied two people together who were not tied before.

Every beat below passes those three tests. Each one says what it is built from, who it changes,
where it goes, and what it costs. The lines are drafts in the book's voice, ready to drop in or
rewrite. Nothing here is in the manuscript.

Seventeen beats, about a thousand words of new text in all. Five of them change the book.

---

# The five that change the book

## 1. At the fire, Grace uses his mother against him

**Built from:** her story at the first fire ("My house, my choice." REFUSED). Your dictated
quarrel. The choice he made in chapter 13.

**What it does:** right now the last fight is two positions. This makes it an argument he can
lose. He is the one person at that fire who has decided for somebody who could not say no, and
she knows it. His answer is the book's answer: he asked first, and his mother could not reply.
The people Grace wants to save can.

**Who it changes:** Grace gets the strongest case anyone makes against him. Elijah has to say
out loud where the line is.

**Where:** two places. He tells her in chapter 24. She uses it in chapter 36.

**Cost:** about 190 words. It is an insertion into your dictated scene. None of your lines move.

**Chapter 24, after her fire story and his bar story:**

> He told her the other one too. He had not meant to. The two pages, the room with the window,
> the man with the folder.
>
> "Did you ask her?" Grace said.
>
> "She said 'thin.'"
>
> Grace looked at the stove for a while.
>
> "I'd have signed," she said. "I'd have signed in the parking lot."
>
> It was the kindest thing anybody had said to him about it. He would think of it again at
> another fire.

**Chapter 36, after "it's not our responsibility to choose for them" and "Yes, it is":**

> She wiped her face with the heel of her hand.
>
> "You chose for your mother."
>
> He didn't say anything.
>
> "She was sitting in a chair and she couldn't say one word, and you sold her house out from
> under her. And you were right. You were right, Elijah. That's all I'm asking you to do."
>
> "I asked her."
>
> "She couldn't answer."
>
> "No," he said. "They can."
>
> "Oh, don't," she said. "Don't you dare. You can't live with what you did, so now nobody gets
> to choose for anybody. And now you're taking out your mother trauma on me!"
>
> She growled, low in her throat, and shook both hands up by her chin.
>
> Elijah was speechless. His heart sank and fell out the bottom of his gut.
>
> Here was the woman he trusted most. In one instant all of that trust was utterly shattered.

Her line and the three beats after it are yours (19 November), put into the book's past tense.
The line lands here because it is partly true, and because she is doing the same thing to him
with the man in the fire. A few lines later she says "I can't believe I did this again."

Then your scene goes on as you dictated it: she breaks the silence. "Look, I thought you were
one of us."

## 2. David has been sitting with his mother every Sunday

**Built from:** David "who answered with a thumb." "I watch that guy. Every week." "He had never
said it was every week." "You missed my dad." The new 9:40 call.

**What it does:** Elijah goes north for three years. Somebody has to be in that room, and nobody
is. David goes, every Sunday before the bar opens, and tells no one. It is the most David thing
in the book. It also answers a gap from the audit: nothing says why Elijah goes to the Bear Flag
on that night of all nights. Now he has found out.

**Who it changes:** David. His flaw was that he will not leave the bar. He has been leaving it
every week.

**Where:** one line on a Sunday call in Act Three. He finds out in chapter 37. Three lines in
chapter 39.

**Cost:** about 80 words.

**On any 9:40 call, from the aide:**

> "She had a good day. Your friend was by."

He takes it for one of the church ladies.

**Chapter 37, in her room:**

> "He comes Sundays. Before he opens. He does the batteries in her radio."
>
> "Who does?"
>
> "The bartender." She looked at him. "I figured you knew."

**Chapter 39, after "You missed my dad":**

> "You didn't miss my mom," Elijah said.
>
> David wiped the same piece of bar twice.
>
> "We don't open till four on Sundays," he said.

## 3. Wes bought the guitar back

**Built from:** the pawn ticket ("Then it's in the window"). Wes's text from chapter 2, "is my
stuff in that thing," and "He never did." Main Street, where Wes looked away.

**What it does:** Wes and Nate vanish after chapter 14. This gives Wes an ending without a
scene of his own. He was the one person Elijah really wronged, he looked away on the street,
and then he saw a Telecaster in a window in Santa Rosa and knew whose it was. Elijah finally
sends the answer he deleted five years ago.

**Who it changes:** Wes, from the question to the look to the guitar. And Elijah pays the one
debt in the book that was real.

**Where:** chapter 39, at the bar.

**Cost:** about 130 words.

> "He never said what he paid. He said don't make a thing of it."

## 4. He has seen that folder before

**Built from:** the man from Sacramento in chapter 13. Chapter 23, where the same outfit comes
for Irene's forty acres. Dale on the speakerphone: "Their number is higher, Mom. That's just
math." Reuben: "It's her porch, Dale. It's her choice."

**What it does:** chapter 23 is the co-op's first win, and Elijah's part in it is a dashboard
that "had not closed the deal." Now he is the only person at that table who has sat across from
that folder and signed. He knows the pitch from the inside. And Dale is Elijah, one year
earlier. Irene can be asked. His mother could not.

**Who it changes:** Elijah brings something no model could. Dale gets a moment of being
understood. Lou, who lost his own ground, should be at that table to hear it.

**Where:** chapter 23, at Irene's kitchen table.

**Cost:** about 130 words.

> "Their number is higher, Mom. That's just math."
>
> "It is," Elijah said.
>
> Everybody looked at him. He had not meant to speak.
>
> "Dale. It's Elijah. I do the computers." He kept his eyes on the oilcloth. "I took their
> number last year. My mother's place. He'll tell you what it brings at auction, and what's
> left after. Then he'll tell you how many months a court takes. That last one he lets sit.
> That's the one that sells it."
>
> The phone was quiet.
>
> "What'd you get?" Dale said.
>
> "A room with a window. For her." Elijah looked at Irene. "I'm not saying it was wrong. I'm
> saying your mother can still answer. Mine couldn't."

Then what is already there: what Irene finally asks is about Walt's tractor.

## 5. There were two other ways to pay for that room

**Built from:** the two numbers in his phone that the book never uses. Ruby: triple his salary
"for doing nothing." Gus: "Gus would come. That was the thing." Gus's line: "The ones for
nothing are the expensive ones."

**What it does:** it makes the choice a real one. He could keep the farm with one phone call.
He sells it. That is who he is: a man who tells and cannot ask. In chapter 34 he finally offers
to ask Gus for something, for the co-op, and they say no. That is an arc the book already has
and has never shown.

**Who it changes:** Elijah. It also gives Ruby and Gus a weight they keep to the last chapter,
where he "does not call either number."

**Where:** chapter 13, before he signs.

**Cost:** about 120 words.

**Risk:** some readers will not forgive him for it. I think that is the point, but it is your
call.

> There were two other ways to pay for that room. Both of them were in his phone.
>
> *ruby*, lower case. Triple what he used to make, for doing nothing. One call and the farm
> stayed and the room was paid, and Elijah went on a shelf in a white apartment next to a lamp
> that faced the wall.
>
> And Gus. Gus would have come. He would have written the check in the cab of a U-Haul and
> asked if Elijah wanted the rest of the sunflower seeds. That was the trouble. He had told that
> man no. You did not get to call a man like that back when you needed him.
>
> He looked at the two names for a long time. Then he put the phone in his pocket and picked up
> the pen.

**One line for chapter 34, when he offers the arms:**

> He had not called that number for his mother. He was offering to call it for squash.

# Six that tie people together

## 6. The realtor's card

**Built from:** chapter 0. The silver-haired realtor "took the napkin, read it, folded it into
his breast pocket." "Good people, don't get me wrong. But the numbers just don't…"

**What it does:** Elijah has to sell a farm in three weeks and knows exactly three realtors. The
men he handed the future to in chapter 0 hand him to Bidwell's buyer. Nobody is a villain. The
napkin went into that pocket and a card comes out of it.

**Where:** chapter 13, before the man from Sacramento. **Cost:** about 90 words.

> He knew three realtors. He knew where they would be.
>
> The silver-haired one was sorry to hear it, and meant it. He asked the acreage and the sale
> date and looked at the ceiling.
>
> "I'd love to help you, chief. Three weeks, with paper on the door." He turned his class ring.
> "The numbers just don't."
>
> He took a card out of his breast pocket. It was the pocket the napkin had gone into.
>
> "These folks close fast. Tell them I sent you."

**What it does:** the book jumps three weeks after he arrives. This is one small thing for that

thing she knows about him that he did not tell her.

**Where:** his first Sunday at the co-op, chapter 16 or 17. **Cost:** about 100 words.

> The pump house had one bar of signal if he stood on the step. At 9:40 he called, and somebody
> held the phone for her.
>
> "It's good, Ma. They gave me a room. I'm working. It's all good."

> Grace was coming up the path with a jar. She stopped where the light stopped. She had read his
> email, so she knew who he was saying it to, and that nobody on the other end could check.
>

## 8. Somebody checks on Grace

**Built from:** the orange tape. "Somebody checked." Marta on Grace: "she will carry you till her
arms give out." Marta "knocked once and let herself in."

**What it does:** Grace checks on everyone and nobody checks on her. The morning Elijah leaves
there is new tape on the gatepost, which is hers. Put a strip on her own door, in someone else's
knot. Marta has been by. It is the only moment in the book where Grace is looked after, and
Elijah is not the one who does it.

**Where:** chapter 37, as he drives out. **Cost:** about 50 words.

> There was a strip on the door of the kitchen trailer too. It was a clumsy knot, the kind you
> tie with gloves on.
>
> Somebody had been by to see about her.

# What these replace

- **Wes getting his answer** is now beat 3.
- **Lou in chapter 23** is now inside beat 4.
- **The three weeks after he arrives** has beat 7. It could still take a page.
- **Grace's missing steps:** beat 1 does the work of her turn before the fire. Three are still
  open: how they become a couple, "You're the lamp" coming back, and her asking him out loud for
  the truck.

# What I would not add

More about Bidwell. Your plan keeps him out of sight, and beats 4, 6 and 10 already let the
reader feel him without anyone learning his name.
