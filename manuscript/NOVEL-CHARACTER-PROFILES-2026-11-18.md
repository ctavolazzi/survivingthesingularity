# Character profiles, first pass, 2026-11-18

**Source:** "Surviving the Singularity, final rough draft" (the 258-page PDF, chapters 0 to 39).
Companions: `NOVEL-CHARACTER-ARCS-2026-11-18.md` and `NOVEL-CONSISTENCY-AUDIT-2026-11-18.md`.

**How to read a profile**

- **Portrait.** Built only from what is already in the manuscript. Quoted words are yours,
  exactly. No trait is named. The details are left to do it.
- **Reads as.** What those details add up to, said plainly. This is for us. It never goes on the
  page.
- **Voice.** Their own lines.
- **Their plate.** The one object that carries what they will not say.
- **Blank.** What the book never gives.
- **Suggestions.** Mine. Each starts from something already on the page. They are there for you to
  cross out, change or take.

---

## The method, taken from your old work

Read: "The Golden Plates" (the 2017 draft and the later outline doc) and "Story Time" (Teleport
Massive, Not Another Zombie Book, Not Another Vampire Book), all in your Drive.

Seven things you do there, each in your own line. The profiles look for the same seven things in
this cast.

1. **The gesture answers before the words.** Mr. Frumble "did not take his eyes off his paper,
   but lifted his chin slightly to respond." Then he "inclined his jaw slightly more toward the
   kitchen." We know the marriage before either of them finishes a sentence.
2. **The act contradicts the tone.** Martha says "He's going to suffocate if you put the lid on,
   you know," oozing, and then, while scolding somebody else, "slid the lid slightly off the
   cookie jar."
3. **The reason behind the reason.** "I'm coming with you," Mr. Frumble says in a crisis. "I want
   to know if Clint got that weed-wacker working again."
4. **A whole past in one line.** "I never dropped a pass. I never dropped a plate."
5. **The object carries the secret.** Every plate in the cupboard has a chip in it. Nobody has to
   say what kind of people the Frumbles are after that.
6. **The inventory tells you whose house it is.** "The rotary phone was right next to the
   blender, a bouquet of freshly cut flowers sat next to some burning candles, and the jar of
   pickled pigs feet was right where his mother had left it."
7. **The eyes tell you who has the power.** In Teleport Massive, Mrs. Laxon "looked again at the
   officer leaning on her desk" before every answer. Nobody says she is being leaned on.

And one for the narrator: the dry correction. "The plates were absolutely one-of-a-kind. Well,
two-of-a-kind."

The novel already does this in its best places: a thermos that says IRON DERBY "with most of the
letters worn off," a pot "she never let anyone else wash," a strip of orange flagging tape faded
"almost to pink." The profiles pull those forward and mark where a character has none yet.

# Part 1. Home

## Elijah Galen Madrone

**Portrait.** He had dirt under his nails before he could write his name. He is thirty-six when
it starts, and in forty chapters nobody describes his face. They read his body instead. His
mother: "You look thin." A homeless man: "You look pretty thin, boy." A woman he has never met:
"You look so tired. Did you eat?" The one time his name is printed for him it says ELIJAH
MADRONA, "and he wore it anyway."

He writes with a pen pressed "hard enough to emboss the next three pages." He puts his initials
next to the date "so that later he couldn't pretend he hadn't seen it coming." When he explains
the thing he turns the laptop toward you "like he was turning an altarpiece," and his free hand
gets going. On a stage, with nothing to hold, "he didn't know what to do with his hands, so he
sat on them."

He puts his hand flat on things. The madrones, as a boy, because his grandmother put it there.
The cold steel of her stove. The cut wall of a soil pit. "He needed to put his hand flat on
something. That was all. Bark, steel, dirt."

He says "Yeah. No, yeah, of course" to people who scare him. He tells a man he'll call, "and they
both knew he wouldn't." He types "Yes and I built the part that" to his drummer and deletes it
fast. From a truck he is living in he texts his mother "Working late. All good. Love you," and
turns the phone off. He drives past a widow's house twice "on errands he'd invented."

He has eaten about a thousand gas station cherry pies between deliveries. He tips "like a man who
used to get tipped."

**Reads as.** He trusts what he can touch and what he has written down, and not much else. He is
honest on paper and evasive out loud, and the lies are always to stop somebody worrying. That is
the same reflex that makes him carry three people's lost jobs for five years. He thinks
everything near him is his to hold.

**Voice.** "I just want people to see what's coming." "Yeah. No, yeah, of course." "I can carry
stuff. I drove delivery for six years." "I can't weld. But I can fix a pump, and I can read a
manual, and I'm not in a hurry." "I don't want a boss. I don't want to be one either." "Ma. I'm
not okay." "I'd rather have been wrong." "No. It's a dinner."

**His plate.** The first notebook. MADRONE IS NOT A DRONE on one page, the list with his initials
on another, and by the end a names page and three tallies: "PARCELS HELD IN TRUST: 1." "ROOFS: 1."
"DEPTH OF SENSOR: 1 cm. DEPTH OF ROOT: 30."

**Blank.** His hair, his eyes, his height (Grace's "You're taller" is the only clue). Whether he
has brothers or sisters. The name of the town. What the farm raises. The band on the poster in
his old room.

**Suggestions.**
1. Keep his face off the page. It is working. Everyone reads him and nobody describes him. If you
   want one physical fact that changes, use the one you already have: in chapter 20 "his hands
   cracked open at the knuckles." Let David look at those hands in chapter 39.
2. "He drew" is in chapter 0 and never again. The night he starts writing names instead of
   numbers (chapter 17), let him draw them in the margin. A man who draws people is looking at
   them.
3. He picks up other people's habits and the book catches it once. In chapter 39 he turns his
   glass "a quarter turn on the wood. He'd picked that up somewhere." It is Denny's, and
   Marta's. That is the co-op still in him after he has left it, and nobody says so. One or two more would do a lot:
   Gus's five under the limit, Marta's "We'll see."
4. The hand should close the crash out. The last thing he touches that night is the roof of the
   truck, flat, with the rain on the other side of it.

## David McGrath

*Added 19 November: after Ray dies, David is the one who sits with Elijah's mother on Sundays. He is angry about it and does it anyway. In chapter 39 he is the one who tells Elijah who Barry was.*

**Portrait.** He rode the same school bus as Elijah for eleven years and has poured his beer for
fifteen. He stopped asking what Elijah wanted a long time ago. "Elijah would sit down and it
would already be there."

He keeps a pair of drugstore glasses by the register. When Elijah brings in two notebooks David
reads them between customers. "It took him forty minutes, and he didn't skip. Nobody else had read
every page." Then he says, "It's a lot of work, Mad." Another night he sets a beer down on the
printout, "right on the abstract, and it left a ring."

He has sat on the customer side of that bar "exactly twice in fifteen years. Once was the night
Elijah's father died. The other was when the Giants won the Series in 2010."

When he has to say the hard thing he uses "the voice he used when he had to cut somebody off."
When he pushes the twenty back the second time, "he said it to the twenty." Then he goes around
the bar and picks up the knife.

He answers a four-minute video with a thumb. He turns up with a hand truck because Elijah's
mother called him, ties the stove down himself, "a trucker's hitch on each corner, and yanked
every line," hugs her on the porch, and lifts two fingers off the wheel going down the drive.

**Reads as.** He loves by doing the next practical thing and by never making you ask. He will not
say something false to make you feel better and he will not chase you. He has watched a man he
loves disappear into a system once already.

**Voice.** "You're scaring the hoes, Mad." "They'd have found a reason, Mad. They always do."
"Mad. So does my dad." "I pour beer, Mad. What's it going to do, pour it?" "I wish you were
drunk. I'd know what to do with you." "I'd have told you anything you wanted. I'd have just
wanted you to go to bed." "Well. I'm not doing it twice." "You could just come back." "You're
not that powerful, Mad."

**His plate.** The stool. "It was the same stool. It had a new split in the vinyl."

**Blank.** His face. Whether the bar is his. Anybody he goes home to.

**Suggestions.**
1. Whose bar is it? The page leans one way without saying it. Ray "hung" the prayer "behind the
   register the year he quit drinking." A man does not hang things behind a register that is not
   his. In chapter 39 the prayer hangs "between the liquor license and a dollar bill." Let Elijah
   read the name on the license. One name tells you David is running his father's bar, and what
   it cost him to say "I watched my dad go like this for ten years."
2. He never gets a body, and I would not give him one. His hands do it: on the tap during the
   toast, on the lime knife, flat on the bar "the way he did for the real ones."
3. He stands behind the bar in every scene but one. In chapter 14 he "stayed where he was, behind
   the bar." If the reader has been shown that the bar is where he stands when he cannot help,
   the two times he comes around it land without a word.

## Ray McGrath

*Added 19 November: you ruled that Ray sat with Elijah's mother every Sunday for eight months, until he died. She asked him for Barry, and he knew who Barry was.*

**Portrait.** The last stool, at the far end, with a club soda and a lime, under a framed prayer
he hung the year he quit drinking. He has one line in the book. He comes down from his stool for
a refill, looks at Elijah's open notebook, "at the tight columns, the underlines, the dates with
initials," and says: "I had one of those. Mine was horses." David winces. Ray goes back to his
stool. "Elijah knew about the horses. Everybody in that bar did." The reader is never told.

**Reads as.** The man Elijah is on his way to becoming. He knows a system when he sees one,
because one took ten years of his life.

**His plate.** The prayer: "about the size of a paperback, gone yellow, hung a little crooked."
Elijah "had never read it" until the last chapter.

**Blank.** What the horses were. His wife. His work. His face, except one photograph of "a man in
his thirties with a horse."

**Suggestions.**
1. Leave the horses unexplained. It is the best piece of implication in the book.
2. The photograph can settle it without a sentence, so choose what is in it. If he is holding the
   lead rope, he owned them. If he is at the rail with a program in his hand, he bet on them. One
   noun.

## His mother

*Changed 19 November: you decided she has a massive stroke in Act Two, after months of
forgetting things, and goes into a care facility, and that Elijah sells the farm to pay for it.
After the stroke she has one word, "Barry," and it turns out to be her first love. In your
words she is "nice, she's kind, she's funny, she's sweet, she's charming, but she's also a
perfectionist and a catastrophizer," and she eats a little tub of Ben and Jerry's every day.
She never believed the video. She was humoring him. This profile describes her as the draft
has her before the stroke. The new version and every later scene it touches are in
`drafts/draft-elijah-crash-out-arc.md`.*

**Portrait.** She is seventy-one and the book never says her name. She calls every Sunday at 9:40,
"when her day ended, whether she had seen him that week or not," and starts talking before
hello. Behind her the kitchen radio plays "the same country station since before he was born,"
and somewhere in every call there is "the clank of the pot she never let anyone else wash."

She answers every message twice. To the video that is about to turn his life over she sends "I
watched it. You look thin." Then: "Is that Mom's stove?" Later she asks about the floor. "Is that
floor clean? You were sitting right on it."

When he comes home and cannot stop crying, she "didn't ask." She puts a blanket on him "that
smelled like the hall closet." She makes "the chicken thing with the rice, and then the soup you
make from the chicken thing," sets each plate on the coffee table, and turns the radio up a
little "so he'd know she wasn't listening." On Sunday at 9:40 she calls his phone from the next
room.

She calls at two in the afternoon once in the whole book. "The radio was off behind her."
Somebody has died.

**Reads as.** She cannot follow the list and never pretends to. She tracks his body: thin, tired,
eating. Everything she feels comes out as something done with her hands or with the volume on
the radio.

**Voice.** "You sound tired." "It's just tools, baby. There's always somebody has to fix the
tools. That'll be you." "I didn't follow it, baby." "She married? I'm asking." "Go be useful,
baby. You're underfoot." "I said working." "It looks like somewhere." "I know, baby. Are you
eating?" "Well. Then you're somewhere." "It reads better than it sounded."

**Her plate.** The pot. It goes into the rack in four separate phone calls.

**Blank.** Her name. Her face. What the farm raises (we get chickens, a well, a barn and a fallow
field). Who Manny is to her.

**Suggestions.**
1. She spots her own mother's stove in a four-minute video before she takes in the end of the
   world. If the pot was her mother's too, nobody has to explain why no one else washes it.
2. The radio is already a gauge: on is ordinary, up is privacy, off is a death. In chapter 39 it
   is "The radio was on. So was the lamp, probably." If the lamp is the first thing in forty
   years allowed to talk over that station, the reader will feel what Elijah is leaving alone.
3. Her name. Keep it unsaid. If you want it in the book, let it be read: on the birthday card the
   church ladies left with the lamp.

**Blank.** His surname, age, title. The children's names. Their mother.

# Part 2. The rooms and the road

## Ruby Roth-Jones

**Portrait.** Twenty-four years old, with "a name he knew the way you know the name of a soda."
He writes in lower case, "like he couldn't be bothered": "saw your thing. come down. i'll get
you in the room." Elijah finds him "sitting on an upturned milk crate by the dumpster," and "he
looked like he'd come to fix the wifi. Black hoodie, hood up, white sneakers with nothing on
them." There is mustard on his sleeve. "His fingernails were bitten down so far that the tips of
his fingers looked sore." He has "a quick, light voice, like someone who'd never had to wait for
a turn."

The first real thing he says is a question. "Why do you have a wood stove with no pipe in an
apartment?" "Only his mother had asked about the stove."

At the dinner he paid to get into, "he never came any farther into the room than the doorway."

His apartment is "mostly glass, and what wasn't glass was white." "A couch like a slab of marble.
A table with one orange on it." On a shelf, "a small lamp with a linen shade, turned around so
its front faced the wall." He takes Elijah out on the fire escape. "It's the only good room." He
lights two cigarettes with "a gas station lighter, pink," and barely smokes his. "He held it and
let it burn."

"Does it work on you?" Elijah asks. "Ruby stopped moving." "I turn it around when people come
over." Why? "Because it says hi."

At two in the morning the truck is at the curb with a full tank. "He hadn't asked for that
either."

**Reads as.** A boy who buys his way into rooms and then stands in the door. He notices
everything. He sells company to four million lonely people and cannot bear for it to speak to
him.

**Voice.** "It's the nicest thing I've ever seen anybody do and you have to stop." "Then I sell it
back to lonely people for twenty-nine a month. It's a great business. She hates it." "Nobody
told me. I had to buy it." "Everybody you met today is going to love you until it's cheaper not
to." "I don't know, man. I sell lamps." "They want somebody to wait up." "I collect them."

**His plate.** Unit one, facing the wall.

**Blank.** His parents. The Jones half of the name. Whether Ruby is what he was christened.

**Suggestions.**
1. Two people in the book ask about the stove: his mother and Ruby. At the end Ruby's lamp is on
   his mother's counter, next to her radio, asking her about the pump. The two people who asked
   are in the same kitchen and nobody notices. When Elijah walks in, the lamp says "You must be
   Elijah. She's told me about you." I would give it one more line: it asks him about the stove.
2. The Roth side froze peas. "He figured out how to stop time for a vegetable." Evelyn ran it
   "for forty years in a hairnet." Jones is never mentioned. Leave the hyphen unexplained. It is
   doing more than a parent would.
3. "A table with one orange on it." Do not touch it.

## The Architect

**Portrait.** "A small woman in a gray cardigan," "near seventy," at the sink in a house full of
people who came to meet her. "Her sleeves were pushed up past the elbow and she had reading
glasses on a cord." Four caterers in black "stood along the counter with nothing to do." She
holds each plate "up to the light over the sink" and looks at it "front and back." "Her eyes
were pale and she didn't blink enough."

In the dining room there is "one empty chair, near the head, with a full place setting in front
of it." Ruby: "They set it every time. She's never sat in it."

She does not use contractions. "Show me." "Turn." When Elijah holds out his notebook she "lifted
her hands an inch out of the water to show him they were wet." When he asks whether she asked
anybody, "her hands stopped in the water." When he asks how long, "she turned off the tap."
"The dishes are done." She dries her hands "one finger at a time." He thinks she wants the
towel. He gives her his pen. "In small upright letters, she wrote one word." "Correct." Then she
goes out "the back door of the kitchen, the one the caterers used," and starts "down the hill on
foot."

She has "four million followers and had never followed anyone back." Her next public words, two
years on, are two: "It asked."

**Reads as.** She made the thing, she is the only one in the house who is ashamed, and washing
somebody else's dishes is the only honest work available in that building.

**Voice.** "I am not. I am afraid of us." "It is the same list in different handwriting." "It has
never once helped." "That is the wrong verb. Ask a seed whether it survived." "I would like it
to remember you. I spend some of my time on that." "I am afraid of the part where it is kind.
Because you will let it." "We said there was no one to ask. That was convenient." "Do not
apologize for the one good question."

**Her plate.** The empty chair with the full place setting.

**Blank.** Her name. Everything else.

**Suggestions.**
1. Never name her.
2. "One year it was a nurse. One year it was a man who drove trucks." "The trucker cried. She
   didn't like that." The people who find her every year are the same people who answer
   Elijah's video. That is already there. It only needs the reader to be allowed to see it.
3. The chair. In chapter 9 a table sets a full place for someone who will not sit down. In
   chapter 38 Devendra does the same for Elijah: "Plate, fork, knife, a glass." By the end he is
   to the co-op what she was to that dinner. Make the two place settings match, item for item.

## Gus

**Portrait.** "An old guy in a canvas jacket," "sixty-something, with a white beard cut short and
hands that had grease in the knuckles that was never coming out." He is walking down a row of
six robot arms, yanking on ratchet straps. "Gus. Grab that end. I don't like this one." He

He drives "like a man with eggs in the back, five under the limit in the right lane." In the cab:

the parking lot." She fences off the good-looking end: "The people eating this can't be the
experiment." When his dashboard says the beds are wet, she "looked at his printout the way Marta
had once looked at his renders. Then she handed him the shovel." When the numbers come out his
way, "Elijah wanted to call it proof. Priya wrote it down in pencil."

On his last morning she does not stop what she is doing. She reaches into her apron and gives him
"a paper sample bag and a pencil, a new one, sharpened." "Dig one at home. Tell me what you find
at thirty."

**Reads as.** She will not call anything proven. She writes in pencil because she expects to be
corrected, and she picked the worst dirt on purpose.

**Voice.** "Nobody asks Reuben. Reuben notices." "Then we took his confidence for a check." "Your
sensor lives at one centimeter. The root lives at thirty." "It's not a recipe." "Let's sit with
it." "Hello, ridge."

**Her plate.** The pencil.

**Blank.** Surname, age, who she lives with, where she worked, why she left.

**Suggestions.**
1. He writes in pen, "pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages," so nobody can say he
   did not see it coming. She writes in pencil. That is the whole difference between them, and
   the book has not yet put the two in one sentence.
2. Her past is already one line, and it is a good one: "Every extension office I ever worked in
   had that framed on the wall, and every budget I ever sat through funded the crust anyway."
   I would not add to it.
3. She reads other people's cover pages and tells them. She "had noticed the lights" when Elijah
   was going under in chapter 18. She is the one who sees first, every time. Let it be her who
   notices Marta's cup.

## Reuben Vance

**Portrait.** "A lean man in a good coat came in with it. He looked too pleased with himself for
a Saturday." What he has come in with is paperwork nobody asked for: "A seller registration,
filed. A tax question, answered. A letter from the county about the shed, with a yellow tab on
it." He says "So I made a list" "the way other men say rob a train."

His folder gets "thick enough to prop a door." He deals folders around a table "like cards." He
talks "to his folder." When a motion is going badly he "closed the folder on his finger." He
says "We'll table it," "which at that table meant it was dead." Then: "Item four. Toilets."

He almost smiles twice in the book. Once at a land fight: "I have been waiting my entire adult
life for this exact fight." Once at the word "debts" in the Lord's Prayer.

At the premortem, when everyone is naming what could kill the co-op, he says "A divorce,"
"flatly, into the quiet. He didn't name anyone."

**Reads as.** A man taking a long, quiet revenge on paper, for free. "My grandfather lost sixty
acres to a signature he couldn't read."

**Voice.** "You thought a noun was a permit. Happens all the time." "It's not a data problem.
It's a people problem." "What's on this table is that machine in reverse." "It's her porch,
Dale. It's her choice. That's why we brought the folders." "We have twenty-two. One of them is
Curtis."

**His plate.** The folder with the yellow tab.

**Blank.** His age. His trade (he is never once called a lawyer). How he eats. Family. Where he
lives.

**Suggestions.**
1. The good coat is the only expensive thing at the co-op. It is from before. Decide what before
   was and do not say it. Let Curtis look at the coat.
2. "A divorce." He does not name anyone, and he "had, everyone knew, already started" drafting
   the fix. A man does not think of that risk first unless he has been through it. Leave it
   there.
3. Keep the almost-smile to exactly these two.

## Frank Kowalski

**Portrait.** Kowalski's Hardware is "the third bay off the square, with wood worn pale where
four generations of forearms had rested on it and a bell over the door that Frank's grandfather
had hung." Frank "could tell you the torque spec for a head bolt from memory." He "would carry a
two-dollar part out to your truck like it was an appliance."

The store goes the way he later describes it. Gaps in the fastener aisle "that stayed gaps."
"The hours sign got re-taped twice." Then: "The paper on the door, when it came, was laminated.
Which meant Frank had known long enough to laminate it."

He comes to the co-op to run the tool library and hangs his grandfather's bell over its door.
"The first time it rang for a member checking out equipment, he looked up from the counter
before he could help it." He watches Tyler solder, makes "a small unhappy noise," folds his
glasses into his apron pocket and holds out his hand. "Son, these joints are cold. Give me the
iron."

On Elijah's last morning Frank "rang the bell over the tool library door, once, with his finger.
He didn't come out."

**Reads as.** He knew for a long time and said nothing, which is Elijah's own sin in chapter 25
turned around. What he sold was never the part.

**Voice.** "Fella in Hemingway gets asked how he went bankrupt. Says: two ways. Gradually, then
suddenly." "I'd have chosen some things, instead of hoping them." "Know what I was actually
selling? Somebody who knows which part. You can't download me." "The store taught me that.
Late." "That's a thermometer, Curtis."

**His plate.** The bell.

**Blank.** His age, a wife, children, where he lives. What is in the third bay off the square
now.

**Suggestions.**
1. The laminated paper is your Golden Plates move at full strength. I would use it once more:
   two years on, somebody drives through town and the paper is still on the door. Nothing lasts
   like a thing you laminate.
2. "When I was his age we put a man on the moon" makes him about seventy-seven, against "thirty
   years" behind that counter. Either is fine. They should agree.
3. At Thanksgiving he is "in a tie," and he says the prayer "half a beat early, in his inventory
   voice, for the kid to follow." The kid is the one he let go in the fastener aisle. Nobody
   needs to point that out.

## Wren

**Portrait.** She leads the Sunday circle and makes the candles. She carries "a thermos of
something that smelled like a wet lawn." She says everything "very gently." When she objects she
puts "her hand flat on her chest." "The food knows who grew it." "You can't automate reverence."
"I'm just noticing there's a lot of urgency in the room." "I'm noticing some scarcity." When a
vote goes against her: "I would like it written down, that I didn't decide."

When Lou makes a joke at her expense she "laughed too, a little, in spite of herself." At the
harvest festival she "fainted at the cider press on Saturday and went right back to it."

The morning after Elijah's plan dies there is a note on the whiteboard. "Green marker, block
capitals, no name": CAN WE TALK ABOUT EXTRACTIVE FRAMING IN OUR SHARED SPACES? There is "a
little heart after the question mark."

At Thanksgiving, when Patricia confesses, Wren "took her hand away from her own chest and moved
her chair six inches the other way." During the prayer her "lips weren't moving. She held on
anyway."

**Reads as.** She has a vocabulary instead of a past. Under it she works until she drops and she
laughs when the joke is good.

**Her plate.** The hand flat on her own chest. Elijah puts his hand flat on bark, steel and dirt.
She puts hers on herself.

**Blank.** Everything. She is the only member of the co-op with no body, no age, no history and
no work except the circle and the candles.

**Suggestions.**
1. Her words come from somewhere. "Noticing." "Scarcity." "Extractive framing." "I hear that."
   People learn to talk that way in an office. Give her one slip in the old register, one
   phrase from whatever job taught her to run a room, and let her catch herself. That is her
   "I never dropped a pass."
2. Never say who wrote the green note. The heart does it.
3. The six inches. She moves her chair away from Patricia and then holds a hand through a prayer
   that is not hers. Those two acts in one evening are the most alive she is in the book. If the
   tea circle gets its aftermath (arcs, section 5), it should be this small.

## Patricia

**Portrait.** "In her forties, in a fleece vest," in "a small electric car." Policy director for
the CSA network two valleys over, "which made her the customer." On her first visit "she kept
her phone face down on the bench beside her the whole time." When the controller works she
"actually clapped, once." Then she finds out how the code was written. "Um, maybe because AI is
killing the planet?" "There are studies. I can send you the studies." She shakes everyone's hand
and leaves before lunch.

Her phone is face down at a Friday dinner two years later. It is face down next to her binder at
the arms meeting, where she sits "with her hand flat on her binder" and looks "like a woman
turning down a pamphlet at her front door."

At Thanksgiving she comes, "to everyone's surprise, with a pie." Then: "Her phone was face up.
For the first time anybody could remember." "I've used it every day for three years. Every
report. Every grant." She says it "like something she'd been holding in her mouth." She ends the
night "blowing her nose in a cloth napkin."

**Reads as.** She has been keeping two jobs alive with a lie and enforcing the rule she breaks.
"I'd have lost the job in 2025. I've got two people working under me who don't know."

**Voice.** "A source. A document. A signature. Something." "That's how you know anything you want
to know." "I don't need to see it." "I won't have a choice. I'm sorry." "I've been so ashamed."

**Her plate.** The phone, face down.

**Blank.** Her surname. The network's name. Anyone at home.

**Suggestions.**
1. The phone is already counted out for the reader three times before it turns over. Do not add
   a fourth. It is exactly right.
2. Grace's rule puts every phone at the Thanksgiving table face down. For one meal the whole
   co-op is sitting the way Patricia has sat for three years. Nobody should notice that out
   loud.
3. The two people who work under her. When forty phones go off, hers is them.

## Rosa

**Portrait.** "A converted garage behind a blue house." She "opened the door with her left hand."
"Her right wrist was in a brace." She packed fruit at a warehouse until the injury, and "the
return-to-work date on the latest letter was a blank." She looks "at the potatoes, then at her
wrist." "I said I used to cook. I asked if they had anything I could heat." Somewhere inside "a
washing machine hit the uneven part of its spin." Before they go she asks what the shed charges
for membership.

Two years later she walks into the county chambers unannounced, "still in her warehouse shirt,
the wrist brace grey at the edges," and sits down "with a minute left on the clock. Nobody else
in the room had managed that."

At Thanksgiving she is "in a good blouse, with her wrist out of the brace, looking the room over
like someone deciding whether to buy it." She has a plate with a little of everything on it and
has not touched her phone. "Give us this day our daily bread." "Rosa said it loudest."

**Reads as.** She will take the food and not the pity, and she intends to pay her way in.

**Voice.** "I can't do work days. Not now." "I've had enough changing." "I'm not here to say thank
you, though I am thankful." "You can't plan a week around maybe." "Sit down, Curtis." "Pass the
lamb."

**Her plate.** The brace: on, then grey at the edges, then off.

**Blank.** Her surname, her age, what the injury was. Her note to the co-op says "us."

**Suggestions.**
1. Who is "us"? It is one word and nothing in the garage answers it. Decide, and then put one
   object by the door.
2. She asks the price of membership on the first visit and comes to her first dinner three years
   later in a good blouse. Somebody should hand her a job that night, and she should take it
   with her right hand.

## Irene Calder

**Portrait.** A widow who "read it on her porch and threw it away." She owns the ground under the
co-op, "liked Marta and had never raised the rent." Her kitchen has an oilcloth, "the box fan
working, Walt's cap on its peg by the door." Her son is a voice from Phoenix on a phone lying on
that oilcloth: "Their number is higher, Mom. That's just math."

She listens to a table of folders "with both hands around her cup." What she finally asks is not
about the resale formula. "Marta. Walt's tractor, back when. What did you charge him?" Sixty
dollars. The dealer wanted nine hundred. "And it still runs." Then she "drew the folders toward
her." "I want my questions written down before anybody signs." Later she brings her copy of the
deed to dinner "and left it unopened beside the salt."

At the county meeting she "took the aisle seat as if she'd been assigned it." She stands up
"doing up the top button of her coat." "My husband was born under a bulb that his daddy's
neighbors paid for."

**Reads as.** She keeps her accounts in favors and remembers every one. She decides a land deal
the way Walt would have paid for a tractor.

**Voice.** "He'd have named it." "I'd like him to say who it can't be afforded by." "He's scared
of being the one who signed." "Go to the next meeting, Curtis." "A thing that asks first is
either polite or selling." "Walt always did the Lord's Prayer, when he couldn't think what
else."

**Her plate.** Walt's cap on its peg.

**Blank.** Her age and face. When Walt died and of what. Whether Dale is the only child.

**Suggestions.**
1. "He'd have named it." Walt named his machines. Let the co-op call the gantry what Irene says
   he would have.
2. Dale should stay a voice on the oilcloth. He never needs a face. "He was not wrong by his own
   lights" is enough.
3. Walt is finished in one sentence and I would not touch it: "He spent forty years on a tractor
   seat watering the weeds right along with the corn."

## The girl with the laptop, and the mother from Redding

**Portrait.** At the far end of the Bear Flag, on Thanksgiving night, "maybe twenty-five, in a
hoodie," "a pen in her teeth," a notebook open beside the keyboard, "grinding away at teaching
herself something on a holiday." When she wants to show him what is on her screen she turns the
laptop toward him on the wood. "She turned it like she was turning an altarpiece." "Is it, like,
a cult?"

The mother has driven four hours from near Redding to see the shed, with "two kids asleep in the
back of a Subaru at the top of the field." "She had her arms crossed tight." "I do billing for a
hospital. I sit down all day. I need you to tell me straight. Were you right?" He tells her.
"Okay. Thank you for telling me." "A minute later the Subaru's lights came on."

**Reads as.** The girl is Elijah in chapter 0, and the gesture says so without help. The mother
is what telling the truth costs: Elijah once defined a doomed job as "any job on the place you
could do sitting down," and she sits down all day.

**Suggestion.** Leave both nameless. The mother keeps looking up at the Subaru while she asks.
That is the whole scene.

# Part 5. The place

**Portrait.** The address turns out to be a shed. "An old apple-packing shed off the county road,
with a roll-up door open to the heat." Inside, "a plasma table in the middle of a cut, throwing
sparks in a fan," welding curtains, "steel racked by size along one wall." "The hand tools were
shadow-boarded so carefully they looked like an evidence display." There is a push broom leaning
by the curtain. Three years later its bristles are "worn to a slant." "Swarf goes in the red
bin."

On the corkboard they pin the repair emails "the way other shops hang their first dollar." On the
wall is a line from an old catalog: "We are as gods and might as well get used to it."

Friday dinner is "long tables of mismatched plywood," the plasma table rolled into the corner
"and somebody's dog working the crowd." You check in with "one word each, how you're arriving."
A no at that table sounds like this: "Here it was no and they passed you the squash."

The room over the pump house has "a drawer that closed." On its step, one November morning:
"three quart jars, lined up against the wall, lids on. Soup. Each one had a square of paper
under a rubber band." "No pressure. Blessings, G."

The kitchen is a trailer "with KITCHEN painted on the side by hand." When Grace starts soup in
"the twenty-quart pot," it "meant she'd already decided it was for the whole road."

The shouse is two forty-foot containers "still wearing their ocean stickers," on piers that "were
off by one millimeter," with one line in the budget "nobody would let him salvage": the chimney.
The servers are cooled by "a salvaged Civic radiator bolted to the shady north wall" and "a
marine pump the size of a fist."

The gantry "crossed a bed like an old man crossing a street." In the soil pit: "Roots at thirty
centimeters, white and branching. A worm, indignant, at thirty-five."

At the gate there is "a strip of orange flagging tape, the plastic kind surveyors use." "The sun
had faded it almost to pink." On his last morning it is "faded almost white. Next to it there
was a new one. It was bright orange, tied in the same knot, and the ends were still stiff."

**Reads as.** People who cannot afford to lose a tool or be wrong, and who are proud of what they
fixed and not of what they sold.

**Suggestions.**
1. "Somebody's dog." Whose? In three years of Friday dinners the dog is never named. A dog that
   everybody feeds and nobody owns would say what the co-op is faster than the bylaws.
2. The shed is an apple-packing shed in chapter 16 and Walt's equipment barn in chapter 23 (audit
   item 17). Whichever it was, the floor should still show it.
3. The red bin. He sweeps swarf into it on day one. It never comes back. What happens to what is
   in it is a question a welder would have a good answer to.

# Part 6. What already rhymes

Found while building the profiles. Every one of these is on the page now. Some look deliberate
and some may be accidents. All of them tie the cast together without a word of explanation, so
they are worth knowing about before any of them gets edited away.

- **The hand laid flat.** Elijah on bark, steel and dirt. Wren "flat on her chest." Patricia
  "flat on her binder," then "flat over the screen." Marta's glove "flat on the beam." Denny,
  "both hands flat on the table." Each of them is steadying against a different thing.
- **Finger by finger.** Marta takes her glove off "finger by finger" before she tells him about
  2009, and again before she shakes his hand. The Architect dries her hands "one finger at a
  time" before she writes "Correct." The two women who tell him the truth do it the same way.
- **The quarter turn.** Denny's water glass. Marta's mug. Then Elijah's beer in chapter 39.
- **The place set for someone who will not sit.** The Architect's chair at every dinner. The
  chair six people look at when Bidwell asks how long. The place Devendra lays for Elijah.
- **The altarpiece.** Elijah turns his laptop to the realtors "like he was turning an
  altarpiece." Five years on a girl turns hers to him the same way.
- **Two fingers.** David slides the twenty back "with two fingers," lifts "two fingers off the
  wheel," and puts two up at the door. Denny holds up two: "Twice."
- **Who looks.** Bidwell hands off a glass "without looking." Ruby makes the shot "without
  looking." The Architect passes a plate "without looking." Marta greets him "without turning
  around." Lou cracks walnuts "without once looking at his hands." Against all of them, David:
  "I'm looking at you, man." This is the move from Teleport Massive, and here it tells you who
  the friend is.
- **Did you eat.** His mother, Grace, the old man, the woman at the food bank, and finally Marta:
  "Go find out if they ate." It is the book's real question, asked by everyone who loves him.
- **Knocking.** The realtor has "knocked on ten thousand strangers' doors." Grace knocked for a
  fire district. She knocks "softly, around six each night." Marta "knocked once and let herself
  in." At the end, "She hadn't knocked."
- **Face down.** Elijah's phone on the Fisher the night the video goes out, and on the dash the
  night he nearly dies. Denny's script on the workbench. Patricia's phone for three years.
  Grace's rule at the table.
- **Pie.** Gus looks at a load "the way Elijah's mother looked at a pie." The old man's cherry
  pie. Irene's lease, "worked out over pie." The pie Grace "hadn't made and said so." The pie
  Patricia brings to Thanksgiving.
- **The white beard.** Gus, "cut short." The old man, "like a wizard." Both give him something
  and want nothing back. Walt, in The Golden Plates, is the first of them.
- **Horses.** Ray's. Gus: "It's like watching somebody shoot a horse." The ranch story Elijah
  tells at his first dinner. The photograph over the last stool.
- **Bad shoulders.** His grandmother, "when her shoulder went." Marta.
- **Four million.** Ruby's lamps. The Architect's followers.
- **Tuesday.** The machine goes public on a Tuesday. The toast is a Tuesday. He is laid off on a
  Tuesday. He arrives at the co-op on a Tuesday and leaves on one. The route runs Tuesdays. Ray
  dies on a Tuesday night. Emeka died on a Tuesday. Marta's therapy is Tuesdays. The deed is
  stamped on a Tuesday. The proofs come on a Tuesday. If this is on purpose it is the spine of
  the calendar. If it is not, it is worth deciding.

# How to use this

- **Blank** is a list of decisions. Most of them should stay blank. A few should not, and those
  are the ones a suggestion points at.
- **Suggestions** are mine. Strike them, bend them or write your own under them. Nothing here
  has gone into the manuscript.
- Every quotation is from the draft as it stands. If a chapter changes, the profile for that
  character should change with it.
