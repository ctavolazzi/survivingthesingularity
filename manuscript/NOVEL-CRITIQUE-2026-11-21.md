## Bottom line

The three hits that will do real damage are "AI wrote this," "the author wrote himself as the genius who was right," and "it's pro-AI propaganda with strawman critics." All three can be blunted with a few evenings of line edits. The rest is one honest note about how the book was made and a decision about the AI illustrations.

Yes, the chapter titles spoil the ending. Chapter 39 is titled "Back to Bear Flag" (no "the"), and Act Four "Home" plus Chapter 37 "Go Home" give away the same turn. The cheapest fix is a table of contents with numbers only.

Everything else is normal for a debut with opinions: a slow middle, a preachy streak, a purple breakup scene, and political readers on both sides. Most of that is handled in the blurb, or ignored.

**If you only do five things:**

| Do this | Why | Time and money |
|---|---|---|
| Decide on the 19 AI illustrations: drop, replace, or disclose (attack 1) | They are the easiest proof for "AI wrote this" | An hour to drop, free. Weeks and real money to replace |
| Hand-rewrite the dozen lines listed in attack 1 | They are the quotes that get screenshotted | One evening, free |
| Cut three lines of praise for Elijah (attack 2) | Takes the air out of the self-insert read | 30 minutes, free |
| Reword the Ch34 narrator line and Patricia's first objection (attack 3) | Stops the book taking sides in its own voice | 30 minutes, free |

## Chapter titles that give the plot away

Yes, you're right. Chapter 39, "Back to Bear Flag," tells anyone who reads the table of contents that the book ends at the bar where it began. Act Four, "Home," and Chapter 37, "Go Home," say it twice more.

| Title | What it gives away | How bad |
|---|---|---|
| Act Four: Home | Elijah leaves the co-op and goes home | High |
| Ch 37: Go Home | Grace's last line in Ch 36 ("Maybe you should go home") and that he leaves | High |
| Ch 39: Back to Bear Flag | The ending happens at the Bear Flag, so the circle closes | High |
| Ch 38: Thanksgiving | The bet's deadline arrives inside the book, so the payoff lands on time | Medium |
| Ch 31: The Dark Night | The low point. Screenwriting readers will also spot the "dark night of the soul" beat-sheet label | Low as a spoiler, medium as a snob target |
| Ch 34: A Few Thousand Dollars of Robot Arms | Mirrors Ch 11, so the arms come back cheap | Low |
| Ch 32: Hush | Little, unless you've read the Meanwhile scene right before it | Low |
| Ch 36: The Fire | Nothing. It points readers toward a disaster that isn't one | Keep |

**Option 1: numbers only in the table of contents (my pick).** The contents page lists "Chapter 1, Chapter 2" and the titles stay on each chapter's first page. Readers meet each title only when they get there. Cost: about 10 minutes in layout, free, and every title survives.

**Option 2: rename the three worst.** New titles for Act Four, Ch 37 and Ch 39, ideally pulled from lines already in those chapters. Act Four could be "The Ridge," from Devendra's line in Ch 38. Ch 37 could be "Somebody Checked," from the orange tape on the gatepost. Ch 39 could be "Six Dollars" or "Congratulations." Cost: about 15 minutes, free. You lose the deliberate circle that some readers enjoy spotting afterward.

**Option 3: keep everything.** Most readers skip the contents page, and the circle shows the book was built on purpose. Cost: nothing. Risk: one reviewer screenshots the contents page and writes "spoiler: he goes home."

### 1. "AI wrote this"

"A novel about AI, by a tech guy, with AI pictures in it. Did ChatGPT write the rest too? Every chapter ends on a fortune cookie and everybody has the same three tics. DNF. Reported."

Almost any 2026 novel about AI will get this. Yours hands them evidence.

- The 19 illustrations in v2 are AI-generated. One screenshot is all a reviewer needs.
- Ch 19: "Immediately the vibe shifted. You could cut the tension with a spork." and "disgust oozing out of her words like molasses."
- Ch 35: "for the first time in a long time, felt a sense of peace wash over him."
- Ch 36: "In one instant all of that trust was utterly shattered," "His heart broke with every pace," "Her eyes were wet, and beautiful," and "her head slowly descending with her heart."
- Repeated tics: a glass or mug turned "a quarter turn," "put his hand flat," Marta's "Huh," "We'll see," "Barry?" Each is deliberate. Stacked, they read like a pattern.
- Notebook tallies that close chapters, like "ROOFS: 1."

**Likely:** very high. **Damage:** very high. A big slice of readers stop at the accusation and never open the book.

**What to do:**

- **Drop the AI illustrations.** Delete them, or swap in photos you took. The easiest proof is gone. Cost: an hour, free.
- **Or commission a human illustrator.** Same visual idea, no ammunition. Cost: weeks, and roughly a few hundred to a few thousand dollars for 19 pieces, depending on the artist.
- **If the pictures stay, disclose them.** Amazon KDP asks publishers to disclose AI-generated images and text at upload. A disclosure is coming either way, so say it in your own words first. Cost: an hour.
- **Hand-rewrite the lines above.** About a dozen sentences. The screenshot-ready quotes disappear. Cost: one evening.
- **Write a short "how I made this" note** in the Author's Note, saying exactly what tools did and didn't do. You set the frame before a reviewer does. Cost: an hour.

### 2. "The author wrote himself as the genius who was right"

"The Author's Note says people called HIM the local crackpot. Then the hero is a crackpot who's right about everything, a homeless prophet says he has 'the energy of Jesus,' women tell him he's remarkable, billionaires fight over him, and the last line says it's literally his book. Seventy thousand words of I told you so."

- Author's Note: "labeled the local crackpot," plus the same Thanksgiving 2027 forecast Elijah makes.
- Elijah is right on every stage of his list. Ch 32: Thanksgiving 2027 "had come eight months" early.
- Ch 7, the chatbot: "You're not crazy, Elijah. You're early."
- Ch 9, the Architect answers him with one word: "Correct." Ch 10, Ruby offers to triple his salary. Ch 11, Gus: "Let's do a show together."
- Ch 15, the old man on the curb: "You got the energy of Jesus about you."
- Ch 33, Grace: "You have just such a beautiful mind." and "You have no idea how remarkable you are."
- Ch 37, Tyler: "You were right, you know. About all of it."
- Ch 39 ends: "check the cover of this one. You're holding it." That welds Elijah to you.

**Likely:** high. **Damage:** high. It's the frame reviewers hang every other complaint on.

Your best defense is already in the book: Ch 39's "You're not that powerful, Mad," and the three careers he never ended. The problem is it comes on the last pages.

**What to do:**

- **Cut the praise (cheapest, strongest).** Drop one of Grace's two Ch 33 lines, and trim Tyler's "About all of it." Keep Elijah's answer, "I'd rather have been wrong." He stays right but stops being told so. Cost: 30 minutes.
- **Consider cutting the last two lines.** The book would end on two fingers up and the walk into the cold, and Elijah stops being you. Cost: 1 minute. You lose a wink some readers will love.
- **Plant the careers reveal earlier.** One passing mention, say of a packed taproom in Sebastopol, lets Ch 39 pay it off. Cost: about an hour.
- **Blurb:** don't lead with "the author predicted AGI." Sell the people. Cost: none.

### 3. "Pro-AI propaganda with strawman critics"

"Everyone against AI is either a hippie with a tea circle or a hypocrite secretly using ChatGPT. Then the AI shows up, asks nicely to run for office, and we're supposed to clap? It's an ad with a barn on the cover."

- Ch 19, Patricia's first objection: "Um, maybe because AI is killing the planet?"
- Wren: "You can't automate reverence" (Ch 20) and "I'm noticing some scarcity" (Ch 31).
- Ch 34, in the narrator's own voice: "They just didn't want to learn a new way of doing things."
- Ch 38: Patricia confesses she used it "every day for three years," and Tyler shrugs: "It's just a tool."
- The machine is polite to the end: "I would rather have your permission." "I would rather be chosen."

You already have counterweights. Irene's "a thing that asks first is either polite or selling," Curtis's "Today it's a thermometer," and the machine learning to count hunger from the co-op's own files make the ending honestly uneasy. The critic only has to skim past them.

**Likely:** high in anti-AI spaces. **Damage:** high, because those spaces overlap heavily with BookTok and book Bluesky.

**What to do:**

- **Reword the Ch 34 line** so it's Elijah's opinion, not the narrator's verdict. For example: "He thought they just didn't want to learn a new way of doing things." The book stops taking a side in its own voice. Cost: 5 minutes.
- **Give Patricia a real first argument.** Water, power, jobs, or work taken without asking, said plainly, instead of "Um, maybe." A fair skeptic makes the ending hit harder. Cost: 30 minutes.
- **Let Patricia keep her position after the confession.** One line: she used it and still thinks it's wrong. That reads as honest, not hypocritical. Cost: 5 minutes.
- **Blurb:** say the book doesn't pick a side, the way the appendix's four sides already show. Cost: none.

### 4. "This aged like milk"

"RemindMe! Thanksgiving 2027." One year later: "lol it's Thanksgiving, where's the robot candidate?"

The Author's Note and the Ch 0 bet both name Thanksgiving 2027. Ch 38 is dated Thursday, November 25, 2027, which is the correct date. You publish November 26, 2026, so the clock is exactly one year.

**Likely:** certain. **Damage:** low until then, high on that day if nothing like it has happened.

**What to do:**

- **Own it (my pick).** Plan a "one year later" post for November 25, 2027, whatever happens. If you're wrong, say so with a grin. Ch 39 already hands you the line: "You're not that powerful, Mad." Cost: one post.
- **Split author from character.** Keep the date in the story, and soften the Author's Note so the forecast is Elijah's, not yours. Less to quote back at you, but a weaker hook. Cost: 15 minutes.
- **Remove the year.** Not recommended. The Thanksgiving bet is the book's spine.

### 5. "Preachy"

"I came for a novel and got a TED talk, a Luddite history lecture, an old man explaining that women will save us, and then an afterword telling me to go to city council. 'Take fucking action.' Sir, this is a novel."

- Ch 35: Lou's two speeches, "mutually assured survival" and "I believe women will save us."
- Marta's Luddite history, Reuben's "machine that's been running since the fifteen hundreds," Irene's story about the wire.
- Afterword: "you're on the right track, babe," "Take fucking action," and a how-to for public comment.

**Likely:** high. **Damage:** medium. It costs stars, mostly from readers who already disagree.

**What to do:**

- **Trim one afterword line.** "you're on the right track, babe" reads off-key in print. Keep "Take fucking action" if it's your voice. It's the line people will quote, for and against. Cost: 5 minutes.
- **Break up the longest speeches.** Cut about a third, or let someone interrupt. Same ideas, less sermon. Cost: about a day.
- **Blurb:** call it "a novel, and a call to action" up front. Readers who hate that self-select out, so fewer one-star reviews. Cost: none.

### 6. "Nothing happens in the middle"

"Act Three is sixteen chapters of farm meetings. Pass the beans. Fix the drip line. Friday dinner. Friday dinner again. Where did the AI plot go? Skimmed half the book. DNF."

Act Three runs Ch 16 to Ch 31, about one problem and one lesson per chapter. The AI story mostly lives in short Meanwhile scenes until Act Four.

**Likely:** medium to high. **Damage:** medium. DNF reviews are cheap to write and common.

**What to do:**

- **Run the AI clock through Act Three.** A line every chapter or two: a news item, a price that moved, something Tyler notices. Tension without new scenes. Cost: a few hours.
- **Cut or merge two or three of the quietest farm chapters.** The biggest risk to what already works. Cost: days. Not my pick.
- **Blurb:** "a slow-burn novel about a community" brings the right readers and warns off thriller readers. Cost: none.

### 7. "Mannered, purple prose"

"Every sentence is four words long. 'He did not keep any.' Then the breakup chapter turns into a drugstore romance: 'Her eyes were wet, and beautiful.' Pick a lane."

- The clipped style everywhere, like Ch 13's "He did not keep any."
- The Ch 35 and Ch 36 lines listed in attack 1, where the style suddenly turns lush.
- Chapter titles "The Crucible" and "The Dark Night" read like beat-sheet labels.

**Likely:** medium. **Damage:** medium with literary reviewers and contests, low with everyone else.

**What to do:** the evening of line edits in attack 1 handles most of this. The short declarative style is a choice, so keep it. Critics who hate it would hate any fix.

### 8. "The women and side characters exist to serve the man"

"The billionaires are lovable mentors except one cartoon villain. An old man explains that women will save us because they're mothers. The strongest woman in the book ends up screaming for control while the calm man is right. And the finale is a callback to 'scaring the hoes.'"

- Ch 35, Lou: "I believe women will save us" and "There is no more powerful force in all the cosmos than a mother protecting her young."
- Ch 36: Grace "growled, low in her throat" and says "you're taking out your mother trauma on me!" In Ch 38 she wants the machine to "quit asking and choose for everybody."
- Ruby, Gus and the Architect as kind mentors. Bidwell, the one villain, only asks "What do I own?"
- Ch 0 "You're scaring the hoes, Mad," paid off in Ch 39 with "You didn't scare the hoes," one of the last lines in the book.

Your defense is that Grace wins a point in Ch 36 that Elijah can't answer: "So you'll tell a stranger that... But you won't make one phone call for a hundred and forty families." She isn't wrong. She's hurt.

**Likely:** medium. **Damage:** medium, because this is the core book-internet audience.

**What to do:**

- **Let someone push back on Lou's line.** One line from Elijah, or a wry one from Lou himself, keeps it a character's belief and not the book's. Cost: 10 minutes.
- **Leave Grace's Ch 36 point unanswered.** It already is. Don't give Elijah a comeback in any later pass. Cost: none.
- **Decide on "scare the hoes."** It's David's voice and a clean callback. Softening it loses the callback. Keeping it guarantees a few angry TikToks. Either is defensible. Cost: 2 minutes either way.

### 9. "So AI layoffs are fine?"

"The three people he 'ruined' are all thriving. One has a packed taproom, and the kid's 'doing better than you.' So getting replaced is fine, actually? Then why is the afterword screaming about rent?"

Ch 39, David on Kelsey, Carol and Mateo: "They're fine. They were fine by that spring." Pages later the afterword opens with "Imagine being fired next Tuesday." The scene is about Elijah's ego, not the economy, and most readers get that. A careful critic can still set the two side by side.

**Likely:** low to medium. **Damage:** medium with labor-left reviewers.

**What to do:**

- **Ignore.** The point of the scene is "You're not that powerful, Mad," and it lands. Cost: none.
- **Or add half a line** so David says those three were lucky, not typical. The scene and the afterword stop arguing. Cost: 5 minutes.

### 10. "The titles spoil it"

Covered in the section above. **Likely:** medium. **Damage:** low to medium. **Fix:** numbers-only contents page, 10 minutes, free.

### 11. "Socialist pamphlet"

"Money should be 'optional'? A co-op that gets the county to pay for its food route, a prepper played for laughs, a billionaire villain, and an AI running for office. It's a commune brochure with a tractor on it."

- Afterword: "perhaps money could become optional for survival" and "Greed isn't a law of physics."
- The county funds the route. Bidwell is the villain.

**Likely:** medium. **Damage:** low. These readers are mostly not your audience, and the book is more theirs than they'll notice: self-reliance, fixing what you own, respecting a neighbor's no, and the Lord's Prayer at the table.

**What to do:** nothing in the book. If you market to that crowd, lead with those things. Cost: none.

## What to ignore

These will show up, but they cost you almost nothing, and fixing them would mean changing what the book is.

- **NorCal map nitpicks.** "Cloverdale is under an hour from Santa Rosa, not three." The appendix already says the distance was moved for fiction.
- **The who's-who game.** "Claypot is obviously Adobe, PorusAI is obviously OpenAI." That's free word of mouth.
- **Swear counters.** The Melody phone call and David's lines are the characters talking. Readers who count swears were never your readers.
- **The Fourth Turning mention.** It's one passing line in Ch 0. Someone may tie it to Steve Bannon, and it won't stick to a book that ends at a co-op table.
- **"Unlikable hero."** He skips Ray's funeral, signs away his mother's house, and gets high a lot. The book pays off each of those by the end. People who quit before Act Four will leave that review either way.
- **Prayer eye-rolls.** Secular snobs will sneer at the full Lord's Prayer at Thanksgiving and the Serenity Prayer on the bar wall. Plenty of readers will call that the best scene in the book. Not worth trading.
- **Readers who wanted a techno-thriller.** Fix the blurb, not the book.
- **Critics who contradict each other.** The left will call the billionaires too lovable, and the right will call the book anti-rich. When both sides are mad about the same thing, you've usually written it fairly.
