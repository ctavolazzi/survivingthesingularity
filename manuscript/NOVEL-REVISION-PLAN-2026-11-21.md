# Surviving the Singularity: Revision Plan

2026-11-21 · Christopher Tavolazzi

Status: filed for reference on Nov 21, 2026. The working copy lives in Google Drive, in the folder "Surviving the Singularity, Revision Notes". The revisions are being applied in the Google Doc "Surviving the Singularity, Revised Manuscript v3". This file holds notes only, not the manuscript itself.


A plan to make the book less of a vanity project without changing its big call. Elijah stays right about the timeline. What changes is that other people get to be right too: about cost, consent, power, water, jobs, and about Elijah himself. And his haunting shows in what he does, not in what the narrator says about him.

Nothing in the manuscript has been changed. Every sample line below is a placeholder written in the book's clipped register so you can see the shape of the change. Rewrite each one in your own words.

## How to use this plan

- **Effort.** S means under an hour, usually one to three lines. M means a few hours, usually reworking one scene. L means a day or more.
- **Timing tags.** [Launch] means do it before Nov 26. [If time] means small and safe before launch, or first thing after. [Later] means it belongs in a revised edition.
- **Pairs.** Many changes are a plant and a payoff in different chapters. If you make one end, make the other. The section "Threads that move together" lists them.
- **What stays.** The Friday table's politeness, the farm's slow pace, Lou's Mutually Assured Survival scene, "We'll see," and the ending all stay. The plan adds pushback inside them, it does not replace them.
- **The honest math.** Almost every change is small on its own, but there are about 150 of them. All of them together is roughly a week of work, which is why the launch list below is about one day.

## What the research says, applied to this book

Twelve principles from 21 craft sources (listed at the end). Each one names the problem it fixes here.

1. **Steelman every opposing view until a believer would sign it.** A strawman is an argument built to be knocked down; readers can tell, and they side with it out of spite (S1, S2, S3). Here: Patricia, Wren and Curtis get the strongest real versions of their case: water and power, consent of the people the models learned from, one-company dependency, wages, and who gets a vote.
2. **Separate "wrong about the tech" from "wrong about what matters."** A character can misunderstand how something works and still be right about who gets hurt (S2, S3, S4). Here: Patricia thinks the vent boxes call the model and they don't; she still wins on water and consent. That is your brief in one exchange.
3. **No easy conversions.** Skeptics who fold the moment the hero explains are the clearest sign of a strawman (S1, S8). Here: Curtis keeps his fence, Patricia keeps her position after confessing, Lou still doesn't want the car.
4. **Opponents compete for the same goal.** Truby's rule: a real opponent wants the same thing as the hero and disagrees about how (S4). Here: everyone at the Friday table wants the farm and the valley to survive. Fights are over means and trust, never good versus bad.
5. **Every side character has a want, a wound and a life off the page.** Side characters go flat when they exist to react to the lead (S5, S6, S7). Here: Devendra's daughter, Gus's divorce, Melody's share of her father's place, Patricia's two staff, Grace's fire district, Lou's late wife.
6. **"You were right" has to cost something.** Unearned agreement is the textbook Mary Sue tell (S8). Here: every "you called it" now comes with a barb, a loss or a disagreement (Tyler would rather it was here; David says those three got lucky).
7. **Give Elijah a Lie about himself while his truth about the world holds.** K.M. Weiland's model: the hero's inner false belief drives the arc, not the plot facts (S9, S10, S14). Here: see "Elijah" below.
8. **Let Elijah lose arguments on the page.** A hero who wins every exchange reads as the author's mouthpiece (S10). Dostoevsky let Ivan's case against God stand unanswered and replied only through what happens next (S19, S20). Here: Grace's 140 families stay unanswered for the rest of the book.
9. **Show the haunting as behavior.** Wounds show through defense mechanisms: lecturing to stay in control, avoiding, snapping, projecting (S14, S15, S16). Here: he lectures when cornered, refuses to look up the three, skips Ray's funeral, and thinks the farm "just doesn't want to learn."
10. **Release the wound in pieces and let healing be uneven.** Short triggered flashes, small wins, relapses, and help from other people rather than one epiphany (S15, S17). Here: the toast, the gavel hand and the ticking come back as body reactions across Acts Three and Four.
11. **Every conversation turns, and each speaker wants something different.** McKee: a scene that ends where it started is not a scene (S13). Dialogue works through subtext and confrontation, not agreement (S11, S12). Here: interruptions, half concessions, a third party changing the subject, and arguments left on the table.
12. **Dramatize the thesis instead of preaching it.** Preachy themes come from speeches; earned themes come from consequences (S18). Bakhtin's polyphony: let other voices stand as equal minds the author does not overrule (S21). Here: Reuben's enclosure speech gets interrupted, Lou's sermon gets talked back to, and the Afterword grants the skeptics' points.

## Elijah

### The Lie and the Truth

- **The Lie he believes:** If I see it first and say it loud enough, nobody I love gets hurt. And if they do, it's because they didn't listen.
- **The Truth he learns:** Being right protects nobody. Showing up does, and so does letting people choose.
- **Why this one:** it is already in the book. Ma's "So what do you want me to do about it?" (new, Ch 3), the barn of buckets (Ch 13), the missed funeral (Ch 22), "Ma. I'm not okay" (Ch 31), the visitor book (Ch 37), and David's "You're not that powerful, Mad" (Ch 39) all point at it. The plan just makes each one land on the Lie.

### Haunting as behavior

What he's been through is harrowing: the toast and the three jobs, the pipeline with his name in it, Ma's strokes, selling her house to the people he warned about, five weeks in the truck, the dark night. The plan shows it through things he does:

- **He avoids.** Cuts David off (Ch 13), "I don't want to know" (Ch 24), skips the funeral (Ch 22), hides his deed (Ch 23), types "I'm sorry I missed" to David and deletes it (Ch 27), lies to Wren's screening (Ch 31).
- **He lectures when cornered.** The history lesson to Devendra (Ch 2) now costs him; the "they just don't want to learn" verdict (Ch 34) becomes his spite, and he notices it felt good.
- **His body remembers.** Three raised glasses (Ch 17, 35, 39), one sound turning (Ch 28, 31, 32), a hand on his shoulder like a gavel (Ch 31, 35).
- **He says "Working. All good."** to his mother every Sunday and to Devendra (Ch 29) until Ch 31 breaks it.
- **He hides guilt about his own hands.** He built the part that took Wes's posters (Ch 2, 6, 18, 36). His insistence on choice is a scar, not a slogan.

### Growth already on the page (keep, these are strong)

- Ch 18 Marta revising her own moral, which shows him how: "Same severance. Different room to move."

## Chapter titles that spoil

The problem: Act Four "Home," Ch 37 "Go Home" and Ch 39 "Back to Bear Flag" tell the reader where the book ends before they get there.

1. **Numbers-only contents page.** What: the table of contents lists "Chapter 1, Chapter 2" and every title stays on its chapter's first page. What changes: readers meet each title only when they arrive. Effort: about 10 minutes in layout, free. Limit: the Act Four title page still says "Home" eight chapters before the turn.
2. **Rename the three.** What: Act Four becomes "The Ridge" (Ch 17 and Ch 38 both use the image), Ch 37 becomes "Somebody Checked" (the orange tape), Ch 39 becomes "Six Dollars" (David's "It's six now") or "Congratulations" (the word that bookends Ch 0). What changes: no title spoils anything. Effort: about 15 minutes, free. Cost: you lose the deliberate circle back to the Bear Flag in the titles.
3. **Both, lightly (my pick).** What: numbers-only contents page, and rename only Act Four to "The Ridge." What changes: the act page stops spoiling eight chapters early, and "Go Home" and "Back to Bear Flag" stay as titles readers only see on arrival, where the circle feels earned. Effort: about 15 minutes, free.

Two more titles read like screenwriting-manual labels: Ch 31 "The Dark Night" and Ch 22 "The Crucible." "The Crucible" is also the name of the co-op's machine, so it can stay. For Ch 31, an option is "Working. All Good.", the line he gives his mother every Sunday that this chapter breaks. Optional, 2 minutes.

## Bigger changes for a revised edition

These are worth doing, just not in five days.

- **A full turn pass on the Friday dinners** (Ch 17, 25, 27, 31, 33, 34, 38). For each scene, note what's at stake at the start and end, give each speaker a separate agenda, and add interruptions, half concessions and changes of subject. L.
- **The AI clock, one line per chapter.** A cost that lands on a named person each chapter: Rosa's sorting cameras (Ch 17), the dealer's laptop (Ch 20), the voice copies of Denny (Ch 21), the water letter (Ch 30), and so on. Each is S; all of them together is M.
- **Ch 31's silences, Ch 23's Dale, Ch 27's water exchange, Ch 35's cowboy speech.** The M items above. Each reworks a scene rather than a line.
- **The "how I made this" note and the illustrations.** The illustrations are AI-made Canva images, and the first attack harsh readers will make is "AI wrote this." A plain paragraph in the Author's Note about what you used and why defuses most of it. The decision on the images themselves is yours.
