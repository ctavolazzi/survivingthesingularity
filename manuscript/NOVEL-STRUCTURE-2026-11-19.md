# The shape of the story, 2026-11-19

You asked about rising and falling action, obstacles, paths that open and close, the choices
people make, and what drives them. This is the book looked at that way, with the stroke and the
sale of the farm counted in. Nothing here is in the manuscript.

The chart and every number in it are my read of the draft, not a measurement.

---

## 1. Rising and falling action

Each chapter gets one number for how things stand for Elijah when it ends: plus five is as good
as his life gets, minus five is the floor.

**What the line shows**

- **Three holes.** Chapter 14 (the crash out), chapter 31 (the dark night) and chapter 36 (the
  fire).
- **The deepest one now comes early.** Before the stroke, the truck was a dip. Now chapter 14 is
  the lowest point in the book, a third of the way in. That is unusual and it can work, on one
  condition: the later two falls must each take something the first one could not.
  - In chapter 14 he loses what he **had**: the house, her voice, the guitar.
  - In chapter 31 he loses what he hoped to **become**: "it would never let him drive."
  - In chapter 36 he loses a **person**.

  A reader who has watched him survive the truck will not fear for his survival again. They
  have to fear for those other two things. The last line of each chapter should name its own
  loss.
- **A long sawtooth through Act Three.** Up at 21, down at 22, up to the first fire at 24, and
  then six chapters (25 to 30) that sit between zero and plus two with no real turn. That is
  where a reader can drift.
- **The best he ever has it is chapter 33.** He is believed for "about one minute," and Grace
  says "I love you." Three chapters later it is gone. That drop is the right size.
- **The ending rises only a little.** That is your design: "a man who can sit at a bar."

## 2. Obstacles

| Act | What he wants | What is in the way | What he does | Where it leaves him |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One, The Bar | Somebody to tell him he is wrong, or believe him | Nobody can hear it: Devendra, David, his mother | Measures, writes it down, dates and initials it | Alone, and then out of a job |
| Two, The City | To be believed | The people who believe him do not care about him. His own yes. | Says yes to every room. Says no to the two real offers. | Dropped. Then his mother. He sells the farm. |
| Three, The Farm | To be useful. Then to steer. | Marta's standard. Then the table's no. His own speed. | Sweeps, builds, leaks, rings the bell, confesses, brings plans | A roof and a lane. Then "it would never let him drive." |
| Four, Home | To tell the world. To keep the route alive. | The table, and then Grace | Promises to stay quiet. Offers the arms. Lets them go. | Loses Grace. Leaves. |

**What this shows.** In every act the obstacle is a decent person saying no for a good reason.
The book has almost no walls that are not people. The exceptions are heat (chapter 1), the
stroke (13) and the storm (28). That is the book's nature and I would not change it. It does
mean the no's can start to sound alike, so each one should cost the person saying it. The best
ones already do: Marta breaks his weld and then welds his house herself. Grace says "I have to
live with them."

**What this shows.**

- **Until the stroke, nothing in this book was ever lost for good.** Two phone numbers he could
  still call. A stool being saved. "We'll see." New tape on the gate. Every door was left ajar.
  That is why the old chapter 13 felt soft. Now two things are gone for good, and he shut one
  of them himself. I would keep it at those two and Grace.
- **He shuts more doors than are shut on him.** Wes, Ruby, Gus, David's shifts, the farm, the
  arms, the co-op. That is a man who cannot ask and will not be owned. It is consistent, and
  beat 5 (the two phone numbers at the kitchen table) is the place the reader should see it.
- **One door opens that nobody walks through.** The machine asks. The book ends before anyone
  answers.

**Four places where something happens to a person and it should be a choice, or have a cause.**

1. **The layoff (chapter 6).** "They called it a restructuring." That is all the book says.
   *Fix, one line:* the pipeline he built runs itself now. His own work lets him go, the way it
   let three strangers go.
2. **The video spreading (chapter 6).** A band page with two hundred followers, and then his
   phone is buzzing. *Fix, one line:* Linda's granddaughter, who "sent it to" his mother, posts
   it somewhere bigger.
3. **Grace never acts on what she believes.** She argues it. At the fire she says "I kept looking
   up the driveway for a truck." She wanted it to just arrive. The strongest version of her
   would make the call herself, the truck would come, and Elijah would have to send it back.
   **I do not recommend this now.** It is a new plot event three days from your writing
   deadline, and it would change your dictated scene. I am naming it so you know it is there.
4. **He is absent from the biggest event in the book (chapter 38).** That is your design. It
   means his choices that night have to be easy to see: he refuses the sound on the television,
   he tells the girl the truth when she asks him straight, he answers Wes, he leaves the book.
   With beats 2 and 3 in, that chapter has enough.

## 7. What I would do with this

Five small things. None is a new scene.

1. **Give the layoff its line** (chapter 6).
2. **Put a clock on the wall in Act Three.** Reuben already keeps numbers on a whiteboard. Add
   one that says how long the route is funded, and change it in chapters 27, 29, 30 and 34. The
   reader then watches December come through the flat stretch.
3. **Make chapter 24 turn.** One line where he first thinks he could run this better. One line
   for Grace that is more than a chair.
4. **Check the last line of chapters 14, 31 and 36.** Each should name what that fall took:
   what he had, what he hoped to be, who he loved.
5. **Leave Grace's phone call alone** this week.
