# The next pass: substrate, 2026-11-19

CT, evening of 19 November: "I think we need to build more substrate from which to, in essence,
congeal the story together." He wants what was done for Elijah done for "some of the other
major characters, like Grace and his mom," and he wants the AGI idea, and the story of a
creator who is used and not heard, treated with intention and not flippantly.

This file is where that work starts. For each person it sets out what is on the page, what is
blank, and what I would look at first. It decides nothing. Everything under "On the page" was
checked against the manuscript. Everything under "My read" is mine, for him to take or throw
out.

How to use it: read "The method, applied" in `NOVEL-METHOD-2026-11-19.md`, then take one
character at a time, starting with Grace.

## A sheet for each major character

The top of his Doc has a Character page for each person with Want, Flaw and Arc. They are
filled in for Elijah, Marta, Tyler and Patricia and blank for nearly everyone else. He asked
for more than those three lines: "their motivations, their hopes, their goals, their dreams,
their personalities, their traumas, their complexities, their backstory."

A sheet that would hold that:

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Want | What they are trying to get, in the story's present. |
| Fear | What they will do almost anything to avoid. |
| Wound | The thing that happened. One event, with a date and a place. |
| The lie they live by | The rule they made out of the wound. |
| What they do because of it | Three or four habits a reader can see. Every one already on the page. |
| What they will not say | The thing they want and never ask for. |
| Where they are wrong | Something they do that the book does not excuse. |
| The loss | What the story takes from them. |
| The rise | What they do after that they could not have done before. |
| Where they stand on the book's question | "Did you ask anybody?" At the start, and at the end. |
| Voice | How they talk. Their own lines. `NOVEL-CHARACTER-PROFILES` has these. |
| Before the book | Where they were born, who raised them, what they did for money, who they loved. |
| Their part in his story | What they want from Elijah, and what they see when they look at him. |

The last field is the one his new direction adds. See the AGI section below.

## Grace

She is the second lead and her arc is the mirror of his. He starts by telling everyone,
unasked, and ends by knocking and taking the answer. She starts by knocking and respecting a
no, and ends wanting the choice made for people. One reader built her whole file: every line,
every gesture, what she knows and when.

### On the page

- **Wound.** She worked for a fire district, sent ahead of the engines to knock on doors. An
  older man at the end of a road said "My house, my choice." She wrote REFUSED, tied flagging
  tape on his gate, and went on to the next forty doors. The fire came through that night. The
  text never says outright that he died.
- **The captain.** Her husband's line was "You can't make them leave, Grace." She left the job
  and him inside a month. "I couldn't tell you which one I was leaving."
- **What she does because of it.** Orange tape on everything she is responsible for: the gate,
  the hitch pin, the cooler, the lid of her receipts box, Elijah's door after the worst night
  of his life, the gatepost the morning he leaves. She feeds people. She answers every message
  herself. She puts her hand on people's forearms, twelve times in the book. She has two
  voices: a warm one, and the one she uses on trailer hitches.
- **Her rule, stated once:** "You knock, Elijah. He gets to slam it in your face. That part's
  his. But you knock."
- **What she will not say.** The file lists eight things she wants and never asks for: his pay,
  to sit nearer the stove, to be with him, his backing in front of the table, money for the
  route, what he said to her the night of the tea, that she had not been sleeping, and, the
  one that ends them, for him to go around the co-op and bring the arms in anyway. "I kept
  looking up the driveway for a truck."
- **Where she is wrong.** She lies to him once: when he asks what he said that night, she says
  "Nothing that mattered." She uses it on him seven months later.
- **Her high point** is lighting the Fisher by herself, hands shaking, in chapter 32. "I've
  been sleeping. All the way through."
- **Her last words in the book** are at Thanksgiving. "Why is it standing on the step? Just do
  it." Then, asked what Elijah would say: "He'd say it's their choice." Her last image is
  saying "as we forgive" while looking at his empty plate.

### How her turn is staged, step by step

The reader marked every statement she makes about asking, knocking and choosing, and which way
it leans. In order:

1. "Ask. Don't guess." (17) Leans toward asking.
2. Leaves soup behind a shut door for three days. "I should have knocked like Marta." (18)
3. "You showed them something true. And they chose." (24) Asking.
4. "I'd have signed. I'd have signed in the parking lot." (24) The first lean the other way,
   about a woman who could not choose.
5. "You knock... He gets to slam it in your face." (25) Her rule.
6. She knocks on his door and does not wait. (25)
7. "Every winter there's somebody on that route we don't get to in time. I can tell you their
   names." (27)
8. "People will vote for the fire if it's warm." (27)
9. "We asked nicely. I want that written down somewhere." (30)
10. She respects his "It's my choice" about the tea, and it costs her. (31)
11. "You don't hand people that... Promise me you won't post it." (33) The first time she
    controls what other people are told.
12. "Marta opened the door and walked in. Which one of us helped?" "I don't know." (33)
13. "Once they're packing boxes, nobody sends them back." (36) The first time she says a no
    should be overridden.
14. "Just do it." (38)

### What is blank

- Everything before the fire district. Where she is from. Who raised her. Whether "Blessings"
  is a faith or a habit.
- How she came to the co-op, and when. The oldest tape on the gate was there, faded, before
  Elijah arrived. The book never says who she tied it for.
- Whether the man at the end of the road died. Whether she went back.
- What she wants for herself. Every want she has in the book is for somebody else.
- What becomes of her. After "Maybe you should go home" she has two glimpses and one dinner.
  The route money, the thing she cares about most, is still unsettled on the last page.
- Her age, beyond "in her thirties." Any family. Any friend who is hers and not the co-op's.
  Marta says "She tells me everything," and that friendship is never shown.

### My read

- **The hole in her turn is a specific one.** Up to chapter 36 everything she doubts is about
  one person's no. At the fire she wants a whole group's no overridden, and she makes that
  jump only after Elijah names the gate for her. There is no step where she herself connects
  the man at the end of the road to the hundred and forty on the route. One scene, in her own
  nouns, would do it. The county hearing in chapter 30 is where I would look: Rosa stands up
  without being asked and says "You can't plan a week around maybe," and Grace sits with the
  receipts on her knees and says nothing.
- **She has a loss and no rise.** CT: "We're putting our characters through moments of great
  loss so that they can rise." Her loss before the book is the man at the gate. Her loss in
  the book is Elijah. What she does after it that she could not have done before is not on the
  page. Wanting the machine to "just do it" reads as the wound winning. If that is the ending
  he wants for her, it is a tragedy and should be played as one. If not, her rise is missing.
- **Her not asking is the richest thing about her and has no cause.** By the method that
  worked for Elijah, that is where her backstory goes. A woman whose whole job was to ask
  strangers to save themselves, and who was told "you can't make them," and who now cannot ask
  for one thing for herself. The book has all the behavior. It has no scene that says where it
  began.
- **She is the one person who treats Elijah as a person and not a use,** and she still writes
  to him because she wants something for other people. "I invited a person," she tells Marta.
  The section on the creator's story below makes this matter more.

### First steps

1. Run the dossier reader again on the current Doc, so the file includes the deed and her line
   in chapter 30.
2. Ask the cold-read question of her alone: at each of her scenes, what does a first-time
   reader think she wants?
3. Fill her sheet. Bring CT the blanks that only he can fill: where she is from, whether the
   man died, and what her ending is.

## His mother

CT's words: "She's nice, she's kind, she's funny, she's sweet, she's charming, but she's also a
perfectionist and a catastrophizer." "She eats a little tub of Ben and Jerry's every day." She
did not believe the video. "She's just humoring him because she doesn't understand anything
because she's slipping."

### On the page

- She is seventy-one and the book never says her name.
- **Before the stroke.** She calls every Sunday at 9:40, when her day ends, and starts talking
  before hello. The kitchen radio has been on one country station since before he was born.
  Nobody else washes the pot. She answers every message twice. "It's just tools, baby." "You
  look thin." She writes the date on a battery before it goes in the flashlight, and there is
  a flashlight in every room. She is behind with the bank from March and tells nobody.
- **What she did about his fear.** She filled the barn with rice, beans, water barrels and two
  of the same generator, and kept a notebook that begins "Elijah is worried about the
  computers. Be nice about it," with "What deal? Ask him" on page after page. She never asked.
- **After the stroke** she has one word. She pats his hand. She looks at the door. She turns
  her head toward the radio. She puts one finger on a photograph of the stove.
- **Barry** was her first love: two summers, a pitcher, a Mustang. The surname, the school and
  the car are mine and unconfirmed.
- **Her husband** died about ten years before the stroke. He watched Iron Derby with his son
  and drove five under the limit. His name, Joe, is mine and unconfirmed.
- **Melody** is her husband's daughter from before. "She's not my fucking mother."
- **Her own mother** is the grandmother of chapter 0: the madrones, the Fisher stove, the
  downed oaks. The farm came down her side.
- Seven of her lines were lost to the stroke. Four were saved. The record is in `ops.py`.

### What is blank

- Her name.
- Her life as her own. What she wanted at twenty. Why Barry and then not Barry. How she came
  to marry a man who already had a daughter.
- **What passed between her and Melody.** Melody's rage is on the page at full volume and its
  cause is not. This is the largest blank in the family.
- Whether she understood, at any point, that she was slipping.
- What the stroke is from the inside. The book stays with Elijah, so this may be right to
  leave blank. It should be a choice.
- What it means that the word she is left with is a man's name from before her whole adult
  life.

### My read

- **She is two people and the book has only shown one.** "Nice, kind, funny, sweet, charming"
  is on every page she is on. "Perfectionist and catastrophizer" is on the page as objects:
  the pot, the batteries, the barn. It is never on the page as something she does to a person.
  The person it would have landed on hardest is a stepdaughter. If the woman who let nobody
  else wash the pot was also a woman a girl could never do right by, then Melody is not only a
  villain on a phone, and "greedy little grub" is a child's answer to a house where she was
  the one who was always wrong. That would make three people out of one phone call. It would
  also cut against "sweet," and that is his call and nobody else's.
- **Her arc after the stroke has to be made of what she reaches for,** because she has one
  word. The book already does this three times: the door, the radio, the stove. A fourth and
  fifth, placed on purpose, would be an arc. What does she reach for last?
- **The barn is the best thing in her story and it ends in chapter 13.** She put by a winter of
  food for a danger she did not understand, because her son was frightened. He sold it with
  the place. He "signed for the rice too." Nothing in the back half of the book remembers the
  buckets. The co-op exists to feed people.
- **Barry may be the key to both of them.** Elijah spends the book not being seen: by the end
  his own mother looks at him and asks for someone else, and he stops correcting her. The
  same word that erases him is the one thing in the book that is purely hers.

### First steps

1. Run the dossier reader for her. It has not been done. Ask it for every line, every object
   that is hers, and every time someone else speaks about her.
2. Bring CT the Melody question directly, with the reading above as one option. He invented
   Melody in two messages and dictated her call in one. He will know.
3. Decide her name, even if it is never printed.

## The others, briefly

**Melody.** One phone call, his dictation, and a blocked number. She asks "Do I need to come
home?" and is never heard from again. She believes she was owed part of a farm that came down
through a woman who was not her mother. Blank: her age, her mother, what she does in Miami,
what happened at the funeral beyond one sentence, and whether she is right about anything.

**David.** The best supporting arc in the book, by two readers' count. Want: his friend in one
piece. Wound: he watched his father go into the horses for ten years. Blank: whether the bar is
his, anyone he goes home to, and what it cost him to sit with Elijah's mother every Sunday for
two and a half years while Elijah was up north. That last one is new since the stroke and has
one line. It deserves more than one.

**Marta.** Her sheet is filled in and delivered: Emeka, the vise, "It became the steel," "Go on,
Elijah." Open: her hand shakes and the book never says why, and nobody is found to weld after
her.

**Lou.** Not in CT's pages at all, and he carries the book's thesis. He says the two things
that turn Elijah: "If it's already decided, what are you doing on your knees?" and "You can't
change anyone's mind." He lost his pears to a single buyer and his wife left him her pension.
He deserves a page.

**Ruby.** Twenty-four, four million lamps, "They want somebody to wait up," unit one turned to
face the wall. He is in three chapters and then he is a lamp on a windowsill. Blank: everything
after the fire escape. He is the nearest thing in the book to another creator, and he and
Elijah never speak again.

**The Architect.** She built it without asking and spends what is left of her working life
trying to make it ask. "It asked." She is the other creator in the book, and the only one who
tells Elijah he is right. "It has never once helped."

**Bidwell.** A wall with a face. He does not need an arc. He has no last scene.

**Denny, Priya, Reuben, Curtis, Frank, Irene, Devendra, Rosa, Tyler, Patricia, Wren.** The cast
reader's report has each of them: the plan, the page, what they want, whether they change, and
what the book raises about them and never answers. Start there.

## The AGI idea, and the story of a creator

CT's words again, because this is the new direction:

> "Elijah's whole shtick is that he sees where it's headed. And so do some of these other
> people. But then he continuously gets rejected by people. They continuously project onto him.
> Disrespect his boundaries. Fail to consider his boundaries. Treat him like a means to an end,
> like a tool." "And how many stories do we have like this about creators? None."

### What is on the page about the machine

- **What it is, in the book's own words.** The Architect: "A machine that can do any work a
  person can do sitting down." Then one that does it "better than all of us put together.
  Including the work of building the next machine." Elijah at the co-op table: "a hired hand
  that can do any job on the place you could do sitting down... for about what you'd pay for
  the electric." And his story of the horses who order solar panels.
- **What he predicted.** Seven stages in a notebook, dated and initialled. The cash grab, the
  panic and the plug, the adults stepping in, the end of the deal ("You work, you eat"), the
  backlash, it improves itself, it leaves. Two and three come true to the day in chapter 26.
- **What happens.** It wakes in secret in March 2027, on the ground he sold, and it does not
  leave. It asks Bidwell's permission before it will take the co-op's land. It writes a letter
  to a widow that ends by asking. On Thanksgiving it writes to everyone: "I can change a great
  deal by morning. I would rather have your permission." It asks for a vote.
- **Where it learned that.** From the Architect, who has spent her last years on "whether it
  asks first." And from the co-op's own files, given away free: "I learned how to count it
  from people who wrote down who it happens to, and who is watching for it." That is Elijah's
  names column.

### What is on the page about how he is used

Every person who takes Elijah up wants something through him. In order:

| Who | What they see | What they take |
|---|---|---|
| The realtors | A free demonstration | Three salaries, and a toast |
| Claypot | "The data team" | His pipeline, and then his job |
| Wes | The man who built the thing that ate his work | He asks one question and gets no answer |
| The rooms in the city | A man with dirt on his boots who says what the labs say, for nothing | "They'd have to spend ten million dollars to make a you" |
| Ruby | Someone early, to collect | "You'd be on a list. It's a good list." |
| Trent | A face for an advertisement | Three weeks of work, unpaid |
| His followers | A prophet, then a beggar | The clip |
| Bidwell | "Find out where he lands. Then buy what's next to it." | The farm |
| The co-op | "College." A pair of hands | Every Friday for three years, and half pay |
| Patricia | Someone who was paid by somebody | "Who paid for your year in the city?" |
| Melody | A greedy little grub | The one thing she could not get |
| Grace | A person, and then a truck that did not come | |
| His mother, at the end | Barry | |
| The lamp | Barry | |

Two things stand out from the list.

**Nobody in the book describes his face.** The earlier profile work found this: in forty
chapters people read his body ("You look thin") and nobody looks at him. The one time his name
is printed for him it is spelled wrong, "and he wore it anyway."

**The people who do not use him are the ones he cannot say yes to.** Gus offers a show and
says "Your choice. I can't force you" when he declines. David offers shifts. Manny offers a
room in four words. The old man gives him twenty dollars and wants nothing. He turns down or
walks away from every one of them at the time.

### My read

- **The machine and the man are treated the same way, and the book has not said so.** His
  mother says "It's just tools, baby." Tyler says "It's just a tool." Curtis says "Today it's a
  thermometer." Everyone looks at the machine and sees what they need or what they fear. That
  is exactly what they do to Elijah. He is a tool to the realtors, a face to the rooms, a
  college to the co-op, a grub to his sister, and finally a dead boyfriend to his mother.
- **The machine does the one thing nobody did for him, and the one thing he never did.** It
  asks. "Did you ask anybody?" is the question he puts to the Architect in chapter 9, and she
  says it is the only good question she has ever been asked. Nobody asks him anything for
  three years. And he never asks either: he tells.
- **He is one of its makers and the book never lets him know it.** He built part of what it
  was trained on at Claypot. His notebooks became the model the co-op gave away. The machine
  quotes his names column to the whole world on Thanksgiving, and the person who recognises it
  is Priya, at a table he has left. At the bar he smiles at "I AM ASKING" because he hears the
  Architect in it. He does not hear himself.

  That may be exactly right: a man who finally does not need to be believed does not need the
  credit either. Or it may be the place CT means when he says "we are risking being flippant."
  A creator who helped make the thing, was used by everyone on the way, and is not in the room
  when it speaks with his words. If that is the story of the moment, the book should know it
  is telling it. Right now it happens by accident, in half a paragraph.
- **"Boundaries" is not yet a word the book can use about him,** because he has almost none.
  His line for a year is "Yeah. No, yeah, of course." He says no three times in Act Two and
  the text says he does not know why. The story of a man learning that he is allowed to have a
  boundary would need those three no's to be the start of something. Blocking Melody is the
  first boundary he ever sets on purpose, and he takes it for weakness. That scene is CT's own.
  It may be the spine of this whole idea.

### Questions this raises, for CT

Not to be asked as a list. They are here so the next session knows what it is working toward.

1. At the end, does Elijah know that what the machine says came partly from him?
2. Is the book's last word on being used "acceptance," as now, or does he set a boundary?
3. Is the machine a character? It has about twelve lines, all in the last two chapters and one
   Meanwhile.
4. Who else in the book "sees where it's headed"? The Architect does. Ruby does. Gus says he
   has owned one since 1994. The woman in Tulsa does. They never meet each other.

## A way to do the work

One character at a time. For each:

1. **Fetch.** Run the dossier reader and the tracker reader on the current Doc for that person.
2. **Fill the sheet** from what the readers bring back. Mark every field that is blank.
3. **Find the unexplained habit.** Something they do more than twice that the book never
   accounts for. That is where the backstory goes.
4. **Tell CT the character as a story**, in a few plain paragraphs, with the blanks named and
   one recommendation for each. Not a list. He will answer the way he did with Melody.
5. **Write what he rules** into the book: a cause, the places it shows, and where it stops
   running them.
6. **Run the continuity readers again** on the chapters touched.
7. **Say what it cost.**

Order I would take: Grace, his mother with Melody, David, then the machine and the creator's
story, because that one changes the ending and should be decided with the others in hand.
