## What CT asked for

A crash out in the vehicle after the rooms, the attention and the cold welcome back home.
He yells at God. No answer. Just him and the rain. He cries himself to sleep. Time jump.
Weeks, not months, of gig work and job applications. Money and food run down. The homeless
man and the food bank. A friend invites him to the farm. He is not rescued. He has nothing
to lose and they might have food, so the last few dollars go into the tank. The farm people
nurse him back.

## How the pieces interlock

The draft already has most of these scenes. They are in a different order, and his mother's
couch catches him before he ever hits bottom. The change is to move the trip home earlier,
keep him away from his mother's driveway, and let the old man's twenty be the gas to the farm.

## New prose

### Chapter 13: Rain

In the morning the fever was down enough to drive. He did not go in to get sorted out.

He left the lot with the windows down and the heater on, because he couldn't get warm. He
had a quarter tank and no money. Home was two hours north. He did the math on the gauge
twice and it came out about even, so he drove fifty-five in the right lane the whole way,
like Gus.

He didn't go to his mother's. He went to town. He told himself it was because the needle
was on the E and town was closer. He parked on Main Street, a block down from the Bear
Flag, and sat a minute with his hands on the wheel.

He was going to go in and sit on his stool and say, Dave, I'm in trouble. That was the
whole plan. It was six words.

*[Existing scene, moved from Chapter 14: the music store door opens, Nate and Wes come out
with the keyboard case, he raises his hand, Wes looks away, Nate looks down at the case,
they load Nate's van and drive off. His hand is still up. Cut the feed and the prescription.]*

He put his hand down.

There were two guys smoking outside the Bear Flag. He knew one of them. Pruitt, who had
the tire shop, who had bought him a beer the night the realtors did.

"Hey, it's the prophet," Pruitt said. "How's the end of the world coming?"

The other one laughed.

"We still on for five years? I got a boat payment."

"I never said the end of the world," Elijah said.

"You said a lot of stuff, man." Pruitt wasn't smiling now. "My sister-in-law quit her job
over one of your videos. She's got two kids. You know that?"

Elijah didn't know that.

"She's at the Safeway now. Nights." Pruitt flicked his cigarette into the gutter. "You look
like hell, by the way."

*[Existing beat: he looks in through the glass. David is wiping down the bar. David sees
him under the neon bear, stops, then looks back down at the wood and goes on with the rag.]*

Elijah walked back to the truck.

It started. He hadn't been sure it would. He drove out of town the long way, the back road
behind his grandmother's place, because the back road didn't go past anybody he knew. His
mother was ten miles out. He could have been in her kitchen in fifteen minutes. She would
have looked at him once and put a pot on.

He couldn't do it. He couldn't walk in there like this. She had told the ladies at church
about the senator.

The madrones were on the uphill side, where they'd always been. The bark was coming off in
long red curls. He pulled onto the dirt under the biggest one and turned the truck off, and
the gauge dropped the rest of the way and lay there.

It got dark. He lay across the bench seat with his knees up and his jacket over him. It
was the same way he'd been lying in the cannery lot, and he noticed that.

He went down the list. It was not the one in the notebook. He'd been a farm kid, and the
farm was ten miles away and he couldn't go to it. He'd been a guitar player, and the band
had bought a keyboard. He'd been a guy with a stool. He'd been a data cowboy, and then a
face, and a face was worth exactly a heart from a woman with a cooling company. He had
bolted two million dollars of robot arms to a floor and he was not on the list.

He was thirty-six years old and he was lying in a truck.

"I hate this," he said.

It was just a thing to say. He said it to the dome light.

"I hate this." It was louder. "I hate this. I hate this."

Then something in him let go of the rope.

"This sucks! This is awful!" He hit the dash with the flat of his hand. He hit it again
with his fist, and the glove box fell open, and he kicked it. "I didn't do anything! I told
them the truth! I told them the truth and I was nice about it!"

He was sitting up now. He had the wheel in both hands and he was shaking it like it owed
him money.

"I wish I never saw it! I wish I never typed a goddamn word! I wish this never happened!"

His voice cracked and went up into something he hadn't heard since he was a kid.

"I wish I was never born!"

He had never said that out loud in his life. It hung there in the cab. He didn't take it
back.

He had not prayed since his father's funeral, and that time he had only moved his mouth.
He didn't know who he was talking to. He put his head back and yelled it at the roof of the
truck anyway.

"What do you want from me?"

The roof was gray headliner with a stain on it shaped like Ohio.

"What do you want? You want me to shut up? I'll shut up! You want me to keep going? I got
nothing left! Why did you show it to me? Why me? I'm nobody! I play bars!" He was crying
now and it made him furious. "Why did you do this to me? What did I do? Tell me what I
did!"

He waited.

He actually waited. He sat there with his chest going and his hands open on his knees,
like a man who has asked a fair question and is owed an answer.

Nothing answered.

The engine ticked as it cooled. Up the hill a branch moved. That was all there was. There
was no voice and no sign. No better idea came to him. Nobody was listening, or somebody
was and had decided not to say.

Then something hit the roof. One tap, like a finger.

Then another one. Then a few on the windshield, fat ones that broke and ran down through
the dust.

Then it came down all at once. It was the first rain since April, and it came down on the
roof of the Ranger like gravel off a truck. It was so loud he couldn't have heard an answer
if there'd been one.

He lay back down.

He pulled the jacket up. He cried the way he had yelled, all the way, with his mouth open.
Nobody was going to hear it over that. He cried until his ribs hurt, and then for a while
after that he just lay there and leaked.

The rain didn't care. It kept on at the same rate. It was not for him and it was not
against him. It fell on the truck and on the madrones and on his mother's roof ten miles
off, where she was asleep and thought he was working late.

He listened to it. He didn't have anything left to say, to anybody. He listened to it on
the roof and in the leaves and running in the ditch, and sometime in the night it put him
under.

### Chapter 14: One O'Clock on Saturday

He woke at first light with the windshield fogged and his neck bent wrong.

Everything he had ever been afraid of had happened. He lay there and went down it, the way
you check yourself after a fall. The job. The friends. The rooms. His own name in a
stranger's mouth outside a bar. All of it had come to pass.

He was still there.

He lived like that for five weeks.

That morning he walked two miles to a gas station with a can and spent the last of the
cannery money, which was eleven dollars. He did not go to his mother's. He drove south to
Santa Rosa, where nobody knew his truck, and parked on a side street by a park, under a
sycamore.

He turned the delivery app back on. He still had the account. He'd driven delivery for six
years, and he was good at it, and it didn't matter. In 2019 there had been forty drivers in
the county. Now there were four hundred guys sitting in parking lots staring at their
phones. Half the short runs went to the little white carts that rolled down the sidewalk
on six wheels. He got the long ones the carts couldn't do, out to the hills, eleven miles
for six dollars. On a good day he cleared thirty. The truck ate fifteen of it.

He was not the only one on that street. There was a van with foil in the windows and a
Corolla with a comforter folded in the back seat. Nobody was there because they loved
small spaces.

He applied for jobs in the cab at night with the phone propped on the wheel. Warehouse.
Feed store. Help desk. A winery that needed somebody to drive a forklift. He filled in the
boxes. The answers came back fast, some of them in under a minute, and they all started
with the word Unfortunately. He knew what was reading them. He had helped build its
grandfather.

One of them asked him to record a video introduction. He looked at himself in the little
window for a while and closed it.

He smoked what was left of the bag at night so he could sleep, until it was gone. He
showered at the Y for five dollars, and when five dollars got to be a lot he stopped.

In the third week he took the Telecaster out from behind the seat.

He had not opened the case since the last gig. He carried it into a place on Fourth Street
with guitars hanging in the window like hams. The man behind the counter turned it over,
looked down the neck, and plugged it into a little amp without asking. He played one chord.
It was in tune. That was the worst part.

"I'm not selling it," Elijah said.

"Nobody is." The man wrote on a pad. "It's a loan. We keep it as collateral. Ninety days.
You come back with the money and the ticket, it's yours again."

"And if I don't?"

"Then it's in the window."

He gave him a hundred and ten dollars and a yellow ticket. Elijah put the ticket in the
notebook, behind a gas receipt with a phone number on it.

On the sidewalk his left hand kept closing on nothing. He had been a guitar player. That
one was on the list.

On Sundays at 9:40 the phone rang on the dash. He let it stop. Then he typed *Working
late. All good. Love you*, and sent it.

In the fifth week the phone made a noise he didn't recognize. He'd logged out of the apps.
He hadn't thought about plain email.

*[Existing email from Grace, with the two changed lines above.]*

He read it twice with the door open and his feet on the curb.

She'd been worried about him. She had said so in the second line, and she was still there
in the fourth. He didn't know what to do with that. Everybody who had written to him for a
year wanted him to be something. He checked the map. It was an hour and a half north. He
checked the gauge. Then he put the phone face down on the dash and didn't answer.

That was a Tuesday. On Wednesday the app sent him a notice. It thanked him for his
service. His completion rate had fallen below the threshold for his market, it said, and
it wished him luck. He'd turned down too many six-dollar runs to the hills.

He had seventeen dollars. In Stockton seventeen dollars had been a shower. Here it was
nothing, and it was everything, because it was all there was.

By Thursday it was four dollars and a sleeve of crackers. He ate the crackers.

Those were dark days.

*[Existing scene, unchanged except "in August" becomes "in October": Friday afternoon, the
old man on the curb, the squeaky toy, the apple, the photograph, the food bank, "How
privileged are you?", "You gotta fill your own cup," the cherry pie, "Focus on the people
that say yes," the twenty.]*

*[Existing scene, unchanged: the food bank behind the church, two bags, "You come back in
two weeks, honey." He looks for the old man in the line and doesn't see him.]*

He ate a peanut butter sandwich sitting in the truck with the door open, and then he ate
another one.

Then he sat there with the twenty in his hand.

It was enough to get home. He could be on his mother's couch by three. She'd put a blanket
on him and make the chicken thing with the rice and never ask. He wanted that so badly his
teeth hurt. And he knew that if he got on that couch like this he was never going to get
off it.

*Focus on the people that say yes*, an old man had told him.

There was one. He turned the phone over.

He wrote back badly. He typed with his thumbs for twenty minutes and what he sent was six
lines. He told her he was living in his truck. He hadn't told anybody that. The important
line was the last.

*I don't want a boss. I don't want to be one either.*

She answered in an hour.

*[Existing reply: "Good. We already have a welder who thinks she's the boss and a dinner
table that thinks it is... You'd be a guy with a room. Come see. If you hate it I'll buy
your gas home. Blessings, Grace."]*

*P.S. We eat at six. There's always too much.*

He laughed at that, out loud, by himself, with peanut butter on his thumb.

He didn't know these people. It was nineteen households of strangers up a county road, and
the last time strangers had invited him somewhere he had ended up holding a rich man's
empty glass. It could be a cult. It could be nothing, an empty shed and a woman who talked
too much.

He ran it the other way. He was thirty-six. He had two bags of groceries, a notebook, a
pawn ticket and a truck that ran on a homeless man's money. He had yelled at God and got rained on.

*Can't be worse*, he thought.

He drove to the gas station on the corner, put all twenty dollars in the tank, and went
north.

Past Healdsburg the road opened up and his shoulders came down off his ears by degrees. He
had a notebook, a bag of clothes, a manuscript, and two phone numbers he hadn't called.

Somewhere past Cloverdale the check-engine light came on.

## What this leaves open

- **The stove.** Chapter 16 has the Fisher riding in the truck bed when he arrives, and it
  matters for the rest of the book. In this order he never goes to the barn. It has to come
  north later.
- **His mother and David.** The couch, the 9:40 call through the wall, and David's "You
  could just come back" are cut from here and have no new home yet.
- **The friend.** Grace invites him by email, as in the draft. She does not meet him in
  person before the farm.
- **Pruitt** and his sister-in-law are new, and so is every number in the delivery passage.
- **The guitar.** The pawn shop, its street, the hundred and ten dollars and the yellow ticket
  are invented around your line. The novel never says where the Telecaster is after chapter 15,
  so nothing later breaks. It does mean the ticket runs out about when he reaches the farm, and
  "There was a guitar" on the night of the fire is no longer his.
- **His father and the truck.** Not written in. If the Ranger was his father's, this whole
  stretch happens inside the last thing of his father's he has.
