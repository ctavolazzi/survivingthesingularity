Why this version is the strongest of the three:

- **He has to decide for someone who cannot be asked.** The book's question is "Did you ask
  anybody?" He asks three times. His mother asks for someone else. His followers tear him up.
  That night he asks God and gets rain. Three
  years later he tells Grace "it's not our responsibility to choose for them," and he is the one
  person at that fire who has done exactly that, to the person he loves most.
- **Nothing happens to him. He does it.** In the foreclosure version he loses the house. Here he
  gives it up. He is homeless because he paid for her room.
- **He sells the farm to Bidwell and never knows.** In chapter 32 the machine wakes up "drinking
  her well." He signed the paper.
- **Far less of the back half breaks than if she dies.** The 9:40 call survives, turned around:
  from now on he calls her. "Ma. I'm not okay" can still be said. The lamp ends up on her
  nightstand.
- **It plants your next book.** "The Adequate Level of Care" now has a room it started in.

## How the choice works

*This is my understanding, not legal advice.*

- **Who pays.** Medicare covers a limited stretch of rehabilitation in a nursing facility after a
  hospital stay, about a hundred days at most. It does not pay for living there. Medi-Cal does
  pay for long-term nursing care, and in California in 2024 she would not have to sell her home
  to get it.
- **So selling has to be his choice, and it is.** The Medi-Cal bed that is open is far away and
  shared. The good place near home takes private pay only. And the bank is weeks from auctioning
  the farm, so if he waits there may be nothing to sell.
- **How he can sign for her.** A person who cannot speak cannot sell a house. He needs authority
  she gave him earlier: a trust or a power of attorney with his name on it. The draft says a
  lawyer set one up after his father died. Without that he would need a court, and months.

## New prose

### Chapter 13: Two in the Afternoon

In the morning the fever was down enough to drive. He did not go in to get sorted out.

He left the lot with the windows down and the heater on, because he couldn't get warm. He had
a quarter tank and no money. Home was two hours north. He got as far as a side street by a
park downtown, pulled in under a sycamore, and turned the truck off.

He could not have told you why. He wasn't going to walk into her kitchen looking like this.
She had told the ladies at church about the senator.

On Sunday at 9:40 the phone rang on the dash. He watched it until it stopped. Then he typed
*Working late. All good. Love you*, and sent it, and turned the phone off.

He turned it back on Tuesday, to look for work.

There was one voicemail, from Sunday, 9:41. He would listen to it later. He would listen to it
a great many times.

At two in the afternoon the phone rang in his hand, and it was her number. She never called at
two in the afternoon.

"Ma?"

"It's Manny." The radio was on behind him. "I'm at the house, Elijah. I came by about the
fence. She's alive. They took her to Santa Rosa."

He didn't remember the drive. He remembered the gauge.

•

She was in a bed by a window with the rail up.

Her right hand lay on the blanket where somebody had put it. Her mouth went down on that side.
Her eyes found him when he came in, and stayed on him, and he saw her know who he was. That was
the last easy thing that happened that week.

"Ma."

She worked at it. He watched her go and get the word, like a woman reaching for something on a
high shelf.

"Barry," she said.

"It's Elijah, Ma."

She looked at him as if he had said something a little beside the point.

"She's been saying that since they brought her up." A nurse was hanging a bag. "Is that your
dad?"

"My dad was Tom."

"Brother?"

"There isn't any Barry."

The nurse nodded. It was an answer she got a lot.

The doctor was young and stood at the foot of the bed with a tablet. He did not sit down. A
clot, a big one, on the left side. She had probably been on the kitchen floor since Monday night. There had
been small ones before this, he thought, going back months. You could see them on the picture
like old hail damage.

"Had she been forgetting things?"

"Yeah," Elijah said. "Since spring."

"That would be those." He scrolled. "Her pressure's been up a long time. Her sugar. This
didn't start Monday. What does she eat?"

Elijah started to say she ate fine. She cooked for everybody.

"Ice cream," he said.

The doctor waited.

"Every night. One of the little tubs."

The doctor wrote it down. He had the next room to get to, and it showed.

He asked what she would get back. The doctor said a good many things, carefully, and the answer
was in what he left out.

•

The pot was on the stove.

It was not in the rack. It was on the back burner with a wooden spoon in it and something
burned to the bottom. Manny had smelled it from the yard. All his life Elijah had heard that
pot go into that rack.

He stood in her kitchen and looked. There were three cartons of milk in the refrigerator and
two of them were open. Her reading glasses were in the bread drawer. His phone number was on
the refrigerator door in her handwriting, on a yellow note, and on a second yellow note right
under it.

The freezer was the one thing in order. It was all Ben and Jerry's, the little tubs, a week of
them, labels out. She ate one a night, standing at the counter with the radio on, and had for as
long as he could remember. When he was small she let him have the lid.

He had been living in his old bedroom that spring. One night she asked him twice in ten
minutes if he'd eaten, and he said yes both times. Once she called him Tom, and laughed, and
said *listen to me*. She was sweet about it. She was sweet about everything. He had seen it,
and he had gone to Stockton to bolt down robot arms.

She was a woman who wrote the date on a battery before it went in the flashlight, and there was
a flashlight in every room. Her mail was in the drawer with the rubber bands. It went back to
March. Most of it had never been opened. The bank's name was in the corner window of about
every third envelope. One had been opened, and folded up again, and put back in.

The last notice wasn't in the drawer. It was taped to the front door.

Nobody in that house had used the front door since his father's funeral. They used the back.
Everybody did. The paper had been out there eighteen days with the sale date on it, facing the
road.

He went out to the barn to look at the stove, because it was the one thing on the place that
was his.

The barn was full.

There were pallets where the hay had been. White buckets, stacked five high, with the lids
taped. Rice. Beans. Wheat she had no way to grind. Four blue water barrels, forty yards from a
well. A generator in its box, and next to it the same generator in its box.

He stood there with his hand on a bucket. She had believed him. One person had, and it was her.

There was a card table by the door with a spiral notebook on it, squared to the corner. At the
top of the first page she had written herself a note.

*Elijah is worried about the computers. Be nice about it.*

Under that she had copied out his list, one through five, in her good handwriting, the way she
copied a recipe off the radio. Next to number four there was a question mark. *What deal? Ask
him.*

It was on the next page too. And the next. *Ask him.*

She never had. *It's just tools, baby.* She had not followed one word of it. She knew her boy
was frightened of something, and she knew what you did about a worry. The farm had taught her.
You put something by.

The slips were in a coffee can. *Thank you for your monthly order.* The first was dated the
week of the video. The last was three weeks old. Somebody had kept bringing it and she had kept
signing, and that was where the payment on the place had gone since spring.

The Fisher was in the back, under its tarp, behind the wheat.

He went in and sat down at the table. There was nobody to call. His father was ten years gone. There was
Dana, in Miami, his father's daughter from before he met Elijah's mother. He had not heard her
voice since the funeral, and he did not want to hear it now. He went down it twice, to be sure.

He was the call.

•

A woman from the hospital with a lanyard and a folder took him into a room with a box of
tissues on the table. She was good at it. She had done it four times that day.

His mother could not go home. She would need two people to lift her, and somebody awake at
night. The hospital would keep her until Friday. After that there was a stretch of rehab the
insurance would pay for, and after that there was not.

She had two pages for him.

The first was a bed the county would pay for. It was in Stockton. He read the word and thought
he had made it up. It was three to a room, two hours from anybody who had ever known her name.

The second was a place on the east side of town with a rose garden nobody sat in. It was ten
minutes from the farm. The woman at the front desk knew her from church. It was a room of her
own with a window. At the bottom of the page was a number for one month. The first time he
read it he took it for a year.

"Does she have assets?" the woman said. "A home?"

"She's got the farm."

"Is there anybody else in the family who should be part of this?"

"No."

"Then it's you," she said, and she was sorry about it.

•

He asked her.

He sat by the bed with the two pages and he told her all of it, slowly, the way he had once
tried to tell her about the machine. The bank. The door. Stockton. The room with the window.
What it would take to pay for the room. He said the word *sell*, and then he made himself say
what he would be selling.

"I need you to tell me, Ma. I need you to tell me what to do."

She looked at him the whole time. She understood that he was upset. He could see her
understand it. Her good hand came up off the blanket and patted his.

Then she looked past him, at the door.

"Barry?" she said.

He waited. That was all there was.

He asked them, too.

He did it in the hospital garage with the phone propped on the wheel. He had been talking to
that phone for a year. He had told it what was coming and how it was going, from hay bales and
green rooms and the front seat of this truck. A number of people he didn't look at had listened.
He had never asked them for one thing.

He did not do it well. He said his mother had had a stroke. He said what a room cost. He said,
"I don't know how to ask this. I need help." He put up a link and went back upstairs.

The woman in Tulsa sent twenty dollars inside the minute. *For your mom.* The elevator man sent
ten.

Then the rest of them got there.

*And there it is.*

*Why the fuck do you need help?*

*Says the world's ending. Wants your Venmo.*

*Fuck you. You're just asking for handouts.*

*Get a job. Oh wait.*

Somebody cut it down to the three words and ran his list under it, set to music. It went
further in a day than the plain videos had gone all spring.

The other kind came in on the side, where strangers couldn't see. A guy he had played ball
with. A girl who had booked Second Crop at the grange nine years running. *Always knew you
thought you were better than us.* And Nate, who had stood two feet to his left for seventeen
years. *Take the band's name off that page.*

He read every one. His mother had raised him to answer a letter. He couldn't answer these, and
he couldn't stop reading them.

He took the link down inside the hour. He sent Tulsa her twenty back, and the elevator man his
ten.

It didn't matter. The clip did what clips do. By morning the other mail was back, as if the
spring had never happened. A man with a podcast would fly him out. Somebody's assistant wanted
him on a stage in Austin in the fall. A lab he had never heard of asked for his rate. He didn't
have a rate. He had been flown out before, and he knew what it paid.

He stood in a hallway that smelled like hand sanitizer with all of it in one hand. In one
window he was a beggar. In the next he was a get. Forty feet away his mother was asking a nurse
for Barry. He could not make it fit in one head, and the head was his.

He didn't answer any of it.

After his father died, a lawyer in town had put the place in a trust. Elijah had signed a page
at twenty-six and never thought about it again. He found it in the drawer under the bank's
envelopes. It said that if she couldn't, he could.

The man from Sacramento came Monday, in a clean pickup, with a folder.

He was very sorry about Elijah's mother. He had the sale date and the amount owing and the
taxes, which were also behind. He had a number for what a place like this brought on a
courthouse step, and what would be left when the bank and the county were done, and how long
that took. That last one was the number he let sit.

Or Elijah could sign this week. His outfit would pay off the bank and close in five days.
There was a figure on the last page with a yellow tab beside it.

Elijah turned the hospital's second page over and did the division on the back.

"Who's the outfit?" he said.

The man told him a name with three letters and an LLC on the end. It was the same people who
had bought the Hollis place. They liked the valley, he said.

A notary came out in a Camry and they did it on the kitchen table. Elijah signed where the
yellow tabs were. The money went into an account with her name on it and his name under hers,
and the place with the rose garden took the first of it that afternoon.

He did not keep any. He thought about it for the length of time it took the notary to find her
stamp.

The buckets went with the place. He did not bring them up, and neither did the man.

### Chapter 14: Rain

He drove out the back way, behind his grandmother's place, because the back way didn't go past
anybody he knew.

The madrones were on the uphill side, where they'd always been. The bark was coming off in long
red curls. He pulled onto the dirt under the biggest one and turned the truck off. He had a
quarter tank and no place on earth he was expected.

It got dark. He lay across the bench seat with his knees up and his jacket over him. It was the
way he'd lain in the cannery lot, and he noticed that. Behind his head, through the glass, the
Fisher sat in the bed with its door wired shut.

He went down the list. It was not the one in the notebook.

He'd been a farm kid, and he had sold the farm. He'd been a guitar player, and the band had
bought a keyboard. He'd been a guy with a stool. He'd been a data cowboy, and then a face, and
a face was worth exactly a heart from a woman with a cooling company. He had bolted two million
dollars of robot arms to a floor and he was not on the list.

He was somebody's son. She had one word left, and it was another man's name.

He played the voicemail.

*Hi, baby. I watched your video.* The radio was on behind her. *You look thin. Is that Mom's
stove?*

That was all of it. She had said those words to him the first time eleven months ago. They
were the last whole sentences she had.

The phone lit up in his hand.

*Dana.*

It went through him the way it always had, his whole life. A spike, straight up from the
stomach. He was six years old and he had taken the bigger piece.

*Oh my God. She's calling.*

He watched it ring. He thought about not taking it. He could let it go, the way he had let
another call go on a Sunday.

He took it.

"Hello?"

"You sold the fucking farm?"

"Hello. Nice to hear from you too."

"Are you fucking insane?"

He closed his eyes. He breathed in, all the way.

"How dare you? How could you fucking do this? What's wrong with you? I always knew you were
such a fucking piece of shit, but I can't believe you would do this."

"Dana. My mom had a stroke."

She paused for a second.

"I don't fucking care. You sold the fucking farm. How much money did you keep, you greedy little
piece of shit?"

When he was six it had been *greedy little grub*. She had changed the one word.

"I didn't keep any, Dana."

"I don't believe you. That's a fucking lie."

"I didn't keep any. I don't have any money. I'm living in my fucking truck."

"Oh, are you doing fucking drugs?"

"I'm not doing any drugs. I don't know what I'm supposed to do, but I didn't keep any money. It
went into a fund. It's helping her live. She's living in a facility."

"You put her in a fucking home? Oh my God, you are such a fucking piece of shit."

"Well, I don't see you around anywhere taking care of her. I don't see you fucking helping."

"She's not my fucking mother. I don't need to help her. But I was entitled to some of that
fucking money from that fucking farm, you fucking asshole. How dare you? What the fuck?"

He could not have said why it was that minute and not some other one in thirty years. It was
over. He was done.

"Oh, I'm sure you're having a lot of fucking fun in Miami," he said. "Living it up with all
your fucking rich friends."

"Don't you talk about my fucking friends. And how dare you judge my lifestyle? You said you're
living in your truck, so you're homeless now?" He could hear her breathing. "Do I need to come
home?"

"You can do whatever you want."

He hung up.

It rang again before he had the phone down. He let it ring. It stopped, and it rang again, and
he let that one ring too.

Then the texts started. They came in one on top of the other, faster than he could read them.

*Hey, stop,* he typed. *Don't do this. I don't appreciate it.*

They kept coming.

He did something then that he had never done in his life. He blocked her.

It took one thumb. It took more nerve than anything he had done in a year, and he did not know
that. He thought it was the weakest thing he had ever done. He thought it was what a man does
when he has lost.

The phone was quiet.

He laughed. It came out of him like a bark. Then it was not laughing.

"I hate this," he said.

It was just a thing to say. He said it to the dome light.

"I hate this." It was louder. "I hate this. I hate this."

Then something in him let go of the rope.

"This sucks! This is awful!" He hit the dash with the flat of his hand. He hit it again with
his fist, and the glove box fell open, and he kicked it. "I didn't do anything! I told them the
truth! I told them the truth and I was nice about it!"

He was sitting up now. He had the wheel in both hands and he was shaking it like it owed him
money.

"I wish I never saw it! I wish I never typed a goddamn word! I wish this never happened!"

His voice cracked and went up into something he hadn't heard since he was a kid.

"I wish I was never born!"

He had never said that out loud in his life. It hung there in the cab. He didn't take it back.

He had not prayed since his father's funeral, and that time he had only moved his mouth. He
didn't know who he was talking to. He put his head back and yelled it at the roof of the truck
anyway.

"What do you want from me?"

The roof was gray headliner with a stain on it shaped like Ohio.

"What do you want? You took him! You took her and you left her sitting there! I sold it! I sold
her house and she couldn't even tell me no!" He was crying now and it made him furious. "I
asked her! I asked her and I'm asking you! Why did you show it to me? Why me? I'm nobody! I
play bars! Why did you do this to me? What did I do? Tell me what I did!"

He waited.

He actually waited. He sat there with his chest going and his hands open on his knees, like a
man who has asked a fair question and is owed an answer.

Nothing answered.

The engine ticked as it cooled. Up the hill a branch moved. That was all there was. There was
no voice and no sign. No better idea came to him. Nobody was listening, or somebody was and had
decided not to say.

Then something hit the roof. One tap, like a finger.

Then another one. Then a few on the windshield, fat ones that broke and ran down through the
dust.

Then it came down all at once. It was the first rain since April, and it came down on the roof
of the Ranger like gravel off a truck. It rang on the stove in the back. It was so loud he
couldn't have heard an answer if there'd been one.

He lay back down.

He pulled the jacket up. He cried the way he had yelled, all the way, with his mouth open.
Nobody was going to hear it over that. He cried until his ribs hurt, and then for a while after
that he just lay there and leaked.

The rain didn't care. It kept on at the same rate. It was not for him and it was not against
him. It fell on the truck and on the madrones, and on a roof ten miles off that belonged to
three letters and an LLC, and on a window on the east side of town where a radio was playing

He put his hand up and laid it flat against the headliner. It was cold, and it shook a little

He listened. He didn't have anything left to say, to anybody. He listened to it on the roof and

### Chapter 15: One O'Clock on Saturday

He woke at first light with the windshield fogged and his neck bent wrong.

Everything he had ever been afraid of had happened. He lay there and went down it, the way you
check yourself after a fall. The job. The friends. The rooms. The house. Her. All of it had
come to pass.

He was still there.

He lived like that for five weeks.

He did not stay in the valley. He drove up to Santa Rosa, where nobody knew his truck, and
parked on a side street by a park, under a sycamore.

He turned the delivery app back on. He still had the account. He'd driven delivery for six
years, and he was good at it, and it didn't matter. In 2019 there had been forty drivers in the
county. Now there were four hundred guys sitting in parking lots staring at their phones. Half
the short runs went to the little white carts that rolled down the sidewalk on six wheels. He
got the long ones the carts couldn't do, out to the hills, eleven miles for six dollars, with
four hundred pounds of stove in the back. On a good day he cleared thirty. The truck ate
twenty of it.

He was not the only one on that street. There was a van with foil in the windows and a Corolla
with a comforter folded in the back seat. Nobody was there because they loved small spaces.

He applied for jobs in the cab at night with the phone propped on the wheel. Warehouse. Feed
store. Help desk. A winery that needed somebody to drive a forklift. He filled in the boxes.
The answers came back fast, some of them in under a minute, and they all started with the word
Unfortunately. He knew what was reading them. He had helped build its grandfather.

One of them asked him to record a video introduction. He looked at himself in the little window
for a while and closed it.

He decided the trouble was discipline. He wrote it out on the back of a delivery receipt and
squared it in the visor, the way she would have. *5:30 up. 100 push-ups. 10 applications. One
meal. No phone after 9.*

It held four days. On the fifth he was hungry at ten in the morning, receipt or no receipt. He
had to find a bathroom twice before noon. At a quarter past nine that night he was reading the
comments.

He had taken the link down. The clip did not need him. It was still going around with his face
on it and the three words under it. Every night there were more of them, and every night he
read them all. He could not have told you what he was looking for. One that was fair.

He smoked what was left of the bag at night so he could sleep, until it was gone. He showered
at the Y for five dollars, and when five dollars got to be a lot he stopped.

There was an account with his name on it and more money in it than he had ever seen. He looked
at the balance once a week, to see that the room had been paid. He never thought of it as his.

On Sundays he shaved in the mirror at the Y and drove down to sit with her.

She was in the chair by the window with the radio going. Some days she knew him right off.
Some days it took a minute. Every Sunday, sooner or later, she looked at the door.

"Barry?"

"It's Elijah, Ma. There's no Barry."

She took that the way she had taken the end of the world. Pleasantly. A little while later she
asked again.

"Working," he told her. "It's all good, Ma."

She patted his hand.

At 9:40 that night, back under the sycamore, the phone did not ring. He watched it not ring.
Then he played the voicemail. After that he did it every week.

In the fourth week he took the Telecaster out from behind the seat.

He had not opened the case since the last gig. He carried it into a place on Fourth Street with
guitars hanging in the window like hams. The man behind the counter turned it over, looked down
the neck, and plugged it into a little amp without asking. He played one chord. It was in tune.
That was the worst part.

"I'm not selling it," Elijah said.

"Nobody is." The man wrote on a pad. "It's a loan. We keep it as collateral. Ninety days. You
come back with the money and the ticket, it's yours again."

"And if I don't?"

"Then it's in the window."

He gave him a hundred and ten dollars and a yellow ticket. Elijah put the ticket in the
notebook, behind a gas receipt with a phone number on it.

On the sidewalk his left hand kept closing on nothing. He had been a guitar player. That one
was on the list.

He looked at the stove in the bed of the truck. A man at the gas station had offered him three
hundred for it, twice. He got in and drove.

## What the voicemail, Tom, the milk and the glasses are

You said you were not sure what these were. All four are still in the draft.

- **The voicemail.** On the Sunday he lets her call ring out, she leaves a message: "Hi, baby. I
  watched your video. You look thin. Is that Mom's stove?" It is the last whole sentence he has
  of her. He plays it in the truck the night of the crash out and every Sunday after.
- **Tom.** The name I gave his father. The book never names him. In the spring before the
  stroke she calls Elijah "Tom" once and laughs. Now that her word is Barry, Tom is out of her
  room at the care home and still in the spring, and in the hospital: "My dad was Tom." It is
  why he hears "Barry" as one more slip.
- **The milk and the glasses.** What he finds in her kitchen: three cartons of milk, two of
  them open, and her reading glasses in the bread drawer. Signs she had been slipping.
