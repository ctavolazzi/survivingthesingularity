- Decided: he joins no faction. He decides that solarpunk is the way to go, is welcomed at the farm, clashes with it and leaves. He ends the book single, back at the Bear Flag.
- Decided: he spirals after his crisis. The book never names it, but people who have been through it will recognize it.
- Decided: he is nervous and insecure about being paid, and he does not want any of the typical power dynamics and structures.

## Character arcs, first pass, 18 November

From `NOVEL-CHARACTER-ARCS-2026-11-18.md`.

**Wants** to be believed. **Believes**, wrongly, that seeing it makes it his to carry and his to
fix for other people. The first sentence of the book is that belief: "The night Elijah Madrone
ended three careers." **Needs** to knock and let people answer.

**The arc as written:** the bridge (Ch 0); the toast; the record keeper (Ch 1 to 5); the face who
says yes to everyone (Ch 6 to 8); the first no (Ruby, Ch 10); dropped (Ch 12); the curb (Ch 13);
the broom (Ch 16); names instead of numbers (Ch 17); rings the bell on himself (Ch 22); first
fire (Ch 24); "it would never let him drive" and "Ma. I'm not okay." (Ch 31); believed for
"about one minute" (Ch 33); "It's whether you get to say come in" (Ch 33); "it's not our
responsibility to choose for them" (Ch 36); leaves his mother's lamp alone (Ch 37); "You're not
that powerful, Mad" (Ch 39).

The first line and the last chapter close on each other. The arc is whole. Five steps are thin.

1. **The bottom.** One paragraph ending "Those were dark days." The crash-out draft is this step.
2. **The recovery.** Grace says "Eat. Then I'll show you where you sleep," and the next line is
   three weeks later. *Proposal:* those three weeks get a page. Not nursing in a bed. Somebody
   leaves food, somebody hands him a job he can do badly, nobody asks him anything. It is also
   where the weekly dinner fix lands (audit item 1): he does not go for three weeks.
3. **The late lessons read as repeats.** Through Ch 26 each mistake teaches him one thing and he
   does it differently after. From Ch 27 he fails five times running: paid plans (27), node 7
   (28), the county slides (30), the one-page plan (31), the arms (34). These are not five
   mistakes. They are one want: he has stopped needing to be right and started needing to
   steer. *Proposal:* say that once, at the start of the run, so the reader counts five steps
   toward "it would never let him drive" and not five relapses. And node 7 repeats the Ch 22
   lesson exactly (a change made alone, against his own two-eyes rule). Make it a knowing break
   with a reason, or it reads as a man who did not learn.
4. **The two no's.** He turns down Ruby and then Gus, and both times the text says he does not
   know why. It never comes back. *Proposal:* the answer is already in the old man's scene, when
   Elijah asks "What do you want? Why are you helping me?" He cannot take a yes that has no
   hook in it. Let him know that in Ch 39, in the sentence where he mails Gus the book.
5. **He never says "I love you."** Grace says it once, inside a refusal (Ch 33). If that is the
   point, one line should make it his failure and not an oversight.

## Character profiles, first pass, 18 November

From `NOVEL-CHARACTER-PROFILES-2026-11-18.md`.

**Portrait.** He had dirt under his nails before he could write his name. He is thirty-six when
it starts, and in forty chapters nobody describes his face. They read his body instead. His
mother: "You look thin." A homeless man: "You look pretty thin, boy." A woman he has never met:
"You look so tired. Did you eat?" The one time his name is printed for him it says ELIJAH
MADRONA, "and he wore it anyway."

He writes with a pen pressed "hard enough to emboss the next three pages." He puts his initials
next to the date "so that later he couldn't pretend he hadn't seen it coming." When he explains
the thing he turns the laptop toward you "like he was turning an altarpiece," and his free hand
gets going. On a stage, with nothing to hold, "he didn't know what to do with his hands, so he
sat on them."

He puts his hand flat on things. The madrones, as a boy, because his grandmother put it there.
The cold steel of her stove. The cut wall of a soil pit. "He needed to put his hand flat on
something. That was all. Bark, steel, dirt."

He says "Yeah. No, yeah, of course" to people who scare him. He tells a man he'll call, "and they
both knew he wouldn't." He types "Yes and I built the part that" to his drummer and deletes it
fast. From a truck he is living in he texts his mother "Working late. All good. Love you," and
turns the phone off. He drives past a widow's house twice "on errands he'd invented."

He has eaten about a thousand gas station cherry pies between deliveries. He tips "like a man who
used to get tipped."

**Reads as.** He trusts what he can touch and what he has written down, and not much else. He is
honest on paper and evasive out loud, and the lies are always to stop somebody worrying. That is
the same reflex that makes him carry three people's lost jobs for five years. He thinks
everything near him is his to hold.

**Voice.** "I just want people to see what's coming." "Yeah. No, yeah, of course." "I can carry
stuff. I drove delivery for six years." "I can't weld. But I can fix a pump, and I can read a
manual, and I'm not in a hurry." "I don't want a boss. I don't want to be one either." "Ma. I'm
not okay." "I'd rather have been wrong." "No. It's a dinner."

**His plate.** The first notebook. MADRONE IS NOT A DRONE on one page, the list with his initials
on another, and by the end a names page and three tallies: "PARCELS HELD IN TRUST: 1." "ROOFS: 1."
"DEPTH OF SENSOR: 1 cm. DEPTH OF ROOT: 30."

**Blank.** His hair, his eyes, his height (Grace's "You're taller" is the only clue). Whether he
has brothers or sisters. The name of the town. What the farm raises. The band on the poster in
his old room.

**Suggestions.**
1. Keep his face off the page. It is working. Everyone reads him and nobody describes him. If you
   want one physical fact that changes, use the one you already have: in chapter 20 "his hands
   cracked open at the knuckles." Let David look at those hands in chapter 39.
2. "He drew" is in chapter 0 and never again. The night he starts writing names instead of
   numbers (chapter 17), let him draw them in the margin. A man who draws people is looking at
   them.
3. He picks up other people's habits and the book catches it once. In chapter 39 he turns his
   glass "a quarter turn on the wood. He'd picked that up somewhere." It is Denny's, and
   Marta's. That is the co-op still in him after he has left it, and nobody says so. One or two more would do a lot:
   Gus's five under the limit, Marta's "We'll see."
4. The hand should close the crash out. The last thing he touches that night is the roof of the
   truck, flat, with the rain on the other side of it.
