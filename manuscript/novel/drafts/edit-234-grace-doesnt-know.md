## Chapter 17, the fourth day (replaces "On the fourth day ..." to "He didn't say. That's Lou.")

On the fourth day Grace asked about the arms. She had offered the dry end of the equipment shed in her message on his page, after she saw his post about them. He told her they were gone. A lien sale, he said, and nothing to pay it with. The sale was not until the end of the week, and he left that part out. Those crates were the only things he owned that were worth anything, and he had known these people four days.

Grace said she was sorry. She never asked about them again.

Lou found him on the pump house steps that evening.

"Grace says your arms went at a lien sale," Lou said. "When was it?"

Elijah took too long to answer.

"Sunday," he said. "This Sunday."

Lou nodded. He said he would cover what Elijah owed on the unit, and that it stayed between the two of them.

"Why?"

Lou didn't say.

## Chapter 17, Praether's and the barn, as they read now

Getting them north was the easy part. Somebody had a flatbed, because up there somebody always had a flatbed, and Lou knew a man outside Stockton with a baler that needed to come north anyway. Lou said he'd have a word with Richard Praether first. Then he and Elijah drove out the county road in the Ranger to ask the man together.

The old man's yard was covered in foliage and rust buckets. Wavering somewhere between "Haunted House" and "condemned by the city," Richard Praether's property was, for lack of a better word, a dump.

They left the Ranger by the mailbox. Elijah leaned on the horn until a man came around the side of the pole barn. He stopped where he could see the road and both of them at once. He was seventy or so, in a canvas coat, with a white beard he had plainly cut himself. A burn barrel was going behind the house. The smoke lay down flat across the yard and got into everything.

"Lou here tells me you want to store some robot arms out in my old barn," Praether said, a rattle in his voice like a can of paint being turned over. "I don't care about that, as long as they're unplugged and turned off and the military can't access them."

Elijah and Lou looked at each other, and then at him.

"Yeah, no, there's no power," Elijah said. "There's no way for anybody to remote in. There's no access."

"Yeah. Don't care. I'll check them myself. But I just want you to know that if I do find any bugs or any problems with them, I'm not going to ask you. I'll just destroy them. I got ordnance out here."

"You have ordnance out here?" Elijah said.

"Look, kid, I don't know what business you have with these things, and I don't care. You're more than welcome to store them out here as long as they don't do anything negative. I don't give a crap. But if they start to cause me or anybody around me any problems, you probably ain't getting them back."

Lou went to argue.

"Fine. Fair. No problem," Elijah said.

"Great. Then we got a deal. Shake on it."

They shook on it.

"I can't pay much," Elijah said. "I can work."

"You know what a brush hog is?"

"Yes, sir."

"One Saturday a month, then. Don't come to the door." Praether's face had set into a big grimace. "You know, all this stuff that's happening, all that horse crap you read about in the news, these AI models. You know they're just getting ready to take over, right?"

"Oh man, I hear that," Elijah said.

"Damn right you do. Because you're smart, kid. You got a brain on you, I can tell." Praether put one finger up. "Mark my words. After what happened to me, I always try to warn people. Watch out for the military-industrial complex. Like old Ike said. That guy was on to something. Sure wish he would have been able to do something about it before it got out of hand. But all these Skylinks and Starnets, all they're trying to do is bore a little wormhole into your mind and steal your thoughts."

He had the names wrong, and he didn't care.

"I'll keep my tinfoil hat handy," Elijah said.

"You messing with me, son?"

"You messing with me? You're not one of those reptilians, are you?"

Praether looked at him. Elijah looked back.

"I'm not trying to help no lizard people," Elijah said.

That was when Praether laughed. "I like this guy." He looked at Lou. "Don't let him go."

"I don't intend to," Lou said.

"All right, yeah, we're good. You can keep your arms here. It's going to be all right." Praether wiped his eye with a knuckle. "So you're going to have them shipped up here?"

"You betcha. They'll be here on Wednesday."

"Cool. I'll have the door unlocked. You can just have whoever it is put them right in the barn. I'll keep them for as long as you need me to. Unless something else comes up. I'll let you know."

He never did.

Elijah thanked him, and he and Lou walked off down the drive.

"What the hell was that?" Lou said.

"Oh, it's okay. I speak wingnut."

They laughed.

The mailbox leaned on its post at the end of the fence, where the boards gave out. A letter lay on the ground under it.

"Oh, maybe I should," Elijah said, and picked it up. The corner said Department of Veterans Affairs. The paper had gone soft with rain, like it had been there a while. "Oh. Maybe I shouldn't."

"Yeah, maybe we shouldn't," Lou said.

Elijah put it in the mailbox and shut the little door, and they left it there.

"Richard went over to the Gulf in ninety-one," Lou said, in the truck. "He came home with something wrong in him that nobody would put a name to, and the government has been writing him letters about it ever since. Most of what he says is true. He says it loud, is all."

(scene break)

The arms arrived on time. Elijah canceled the storage unit, and Lou helped him put the arms in the barn.

The door was unlocked, like Praether had said it would be. When Lou rolled it back, the barn had kept its own air, cold gear oil and mouse. One of the rust buckets was a loader, and it started on the first try. Lou had the first crate up on the forks when a tie-down let go with a bang. Up at the house, Praether was down flat on his porch boards before the strap finished falling. He got up slowly and brushed off his knees. Lou watched him do it and said nothing.

"Hey," Lou said, when the door was shut. "Don't tell anybody you have these."

"What do you mean? Why wouldn't I?"

"You need to have an ace in the hole, Elijah," Lou said with a knowing gaze. "You don't want to play all your cards at once. Keep something to yourself. Never reveal all of your cards again. I would have thought you'd have learned that by now."

Elijah felt the heat of embarrassment wash over him.

"And if people on the farm find out you have these," Lou said, "it would cause more trouble than it's worth."

"But I mean, I can help."

"I know you want to help. That's why I like you. You just always want to help. But this is something I think you should keep between us, between the three of us. Keep it close to your chest."

"Grace thinks they went at the sale."

"Then let her think it," Lou said. "Grace most of all. She'd want to put them to work, and this place isn't ready for that."

"Okay. I trust your judgment with your people."

"They're your people too now." Lou looked him in the eyes, real serious. "Don't forget that."

## Chapter 34, the reveal

"So you've been sitting on industrial technology this entire time?" Priya said.

"You've been lying to me this whole time?" Grace said. She had turned in her chair to face him. "You've kept the arms from me this whole time? How did I not know about this?"

Elijah looked up, finally. "Grace," he said, and that was as far as he got.

She turned on Lou. "Did you know?"

"Yeah. I helped him move the arms over there." Lou's presence was somehow larger, even though he had barely moved an inch. His voice was firm, but warm. "I told our friend Elijah to keep the arms a secret. I thought it best if we didn't set up too much of a power differential while our poor boy was simply trying to heal."

Grace opened her mouth, and shut it. She turned back around in her chair.

It was like a cold draft had come in under the door. Twenty-some people were looking at Elijah, and then at each other, and nobody wanted to go first.
