### The draft (shown to CT, not approved)

Curtis found the robot operating system in September.

He had been reading about it all week, most of the night, the way he read everything. On Friday he came into the packing shed early with his phone already out, before the plates were down. For once he didn't have the end of the world in his pocket.

"It's free," he said. "That's the part I can't get over. It's an operating system for robots, and they just give it away. Arms, wheels, eyes, all of it. You can open it up and read every line."

"Thought you didn't want anything in that field that could see," Lou said. He was setting out forks.

"I didn't want theirs. This one's nobody's. No man with a laptop two states away." Curtis put the phone down on the plywood and turned it around to face them. "Seed to table. One of them plants it. One of them weeds it and picks it. Then you put a couple in the kitchen, and they cook it, and they pack it, and they freeze it. A tray shows up on your step. Four minutes in the microwave and it's dinner." He looked down the table. He was lit up like a kid at a parade. "There's so much here."

"It's not feasible, Curtis," Marta said. "It took three of us a spring to get one gantry down one bed. Then it rained."

"And it's not in the budget," Lou said. "There isn't a budget. There's a route that's paid through December."

"It doesn't matter." Curtis waved it off. "We can make more budget if we do this."

Priya had her pencil out. She hadn't written anything.

"Do you really want the optics of putting all the farmers out of work?" she said. "Do you want to take the backlash? Because I sure don't."

"We're not putting farmers out of work. We're empowering them."

Nobody picked that up. Curtis turned to Elijah.

"Back me up, bud. This is your whole thing."

It was. Once he'd have had a slide. Elijah looked at the phone on the plywood, turned around to face him.

"Look, man. I really like you, Curtis," he said. "But the women are right. This is not the right time. We do not want to be the first to do this. Trust me."

He had been first once. He had turned a laptop around on a bar, just like that phone, and slid a napkin across the wood. A week later there was a hand on his shoulder and three glasses in the air. On the fourth floor at Claypot they had called going first winning. He had watched what first cost, and who paid it. It wasn't the men with the glasses.

"Our time will come," he said. "We don't want to be the first. But we might want to be the second."

"Or third," Lou said.

"Whichever number it is, we just need to make sure the farm is protected," Priya said. She wrote something down now. "This is coming. We should stay on top of it."

Curtis picked up his phone and looked at it. He put it in his shirt pocket.

"Fine," he said. "I know you guys are right." He slid down in his chair. "I want my Star Trek future so bad."

"We know you do, Curtis," Lou said. "We'll get there. You have to give people time to catch up on their own."

"You can't force anybody before they're ready," Marta said. "It's wrong."

"I don't know," Grace said.

She had been quiet through all of it. She was squaring a stack of napkins that was already square.

"Maybe sometimes people choose the wrong thing."

She said it lightly, to the napkins. Marta let it go by. Elijah couldn't. He had sat on the edge of bed four in July and heard about a porch.

"Sure they do," he said. "It's still theirs to choose."

"Is it." She didn't look up. "If you're standing right there watching."

"You knock. That's what you told me. He gets to slam it in your face."

"I said you knock, Elijah." Now she looked at him. "I didn't say you walk back down the steps and call it respect."

"That's not what I said."

"It's what it comes to."

It was the start of the hitch voice. His face had gone warm. He had an answer, and it was about doors.

"Okay," Lou said. He put both hands on the table and pushed himself up, slowly, for the knee. "Who wants dinner? We got squash in the oven."

Curtis was the first one out of his chair.

"That I'll be first for," he said.

He turned thirty-nine on a Friday, and he didn't mention it.

He came down to the packing shed like any other Friday. The plywood was set, and nobody was sitting. They were all standing around the far end of the table with their backs to him. For a second he thought it was a meeting about him.

"How did you…"

He stood there with his hands at his sides. He tried to think when the last one was, and he couldn't get to it. He opened his mouth to say they shouldn't have. Nothing came out. Then his face went, all at once, in front of everybody.

It was Curtis who crossed the floor.

Curtis got both arms around him, route jacket and all. It was a big hug from a big man, and it went on. When Curtis stepped back to look at him, his own eyes had gone red at the rims.

Elijah stared at him. This was Curtis. Curtis had told him at his first dinner that he was the kind you fence out. Curtis read the end of the world off his phone and enjoyed it. He annoyed every person at that table at least once a week, and he apologized to the women by name.

"I love you, man," Curtis said.

Elijah waited for the joke. It didn't come.

"I appreciate you. You always listen to me, Elijah." Curtis still had him by both arms. "I know what it's like to not have much of a home to go back to. All of us do. It's the reason why most of us are here."

There was one fence around his mother's place now, and lights on poles. Elijah tried to answer, and it came out as a sound.

Curtis pulled him in tight. He didn't fight it. He put his face in the big man's chest, in the route jacket, and let it out. It wasn't quiet.

Then there was a hand on his back, and he knew it was Grace's without looking. Then Denny. Then Tyler, and Wren, and Frank, and Reuben, and Irene, and Lou's arm around the outside of all of it. Marta came last. She put one hand on the pile, the way she'd check a load.

Priya stayed where she was.

"I'm in," she said, and lifted the crutch an inch off her lap.

"That candle's going over," Marta said, from somewhere in the middle. "Somebody blow it out before it's in the frosting."

They let him up. He blew it out. He didn't make a wish. He couldn't think what he'd ask for.

Lou cut the cake and passed him the first piece.

### The draft (shown to CT; "this is fine")

On the first of April he dug the pit, and Priya came down to read it.

She did that some months. She squatted at the edge with a sample bag and looked at the wall of it the way other people look at a painting. She had him squeeze a handful. She asked what it told him. He told her, and she said, "Mm," which from Priya was a B.

She stood up and stepped back to write it down, and her boot came down on the gantry rail. The rail was wet. Her foot went one way and the rest of her went the other, and there was a sound like a carrot.

"Ow," Priya said, from the ground. Then, with great precision, "Ow, ow, ow."

Lou was at the head of the beds with his coffee, where he always was. He set the cup on a post and came down the row. He didn't run. He was there very fast.

"Don't get up," he said. "Elijah. There's a first aid kit in the shed. Go get it. Should be splint material with it. It's a big one. Grab the big one."

Elijah ran. The big one was under Marta's bench, a case the size of a toolbox, with an inspection date on the lid in her handwriting.

When he got back Grace was on her knees in the mud at Priya's head, with both hands around her forearm. Lou was down at her foot, on the titanium knee. He had the toe of her boot in his fist.

"Can you feel that?"

"Yes."

"Wiggle them for me. In the boot."

"They wiggle, Lou. It hurts when they wiggle."

"Good. Boot stays on."

He opened the kit and went through it with one hand. There was no splint in it. "Huh," he said. He looked around once, and pulled a bed stake out of the ground at the end of the row, and snapped it in two over the rail. He laid one half on each side of her leg, from under the knee to past the heel. He folded his own jacket in between. Then he took the triangle bandages and rolled them into long strips without looking, the way he cracked walnuts, and tied her off above the ankle and below it and around the foot. The knots were flat, and every one sat on the outside of the stake. He didn't tie over the place that hurt.

He took the toe of her boot in his fist again.

"Still feel that?"

"Yes."

"Then that's all I'm doing."

It had taken about two minutes. His hands hadn't hurried once.

Marta had the truck backed down the path by then, with the passenger door open. Lou and Elijah made a chair of their arms and carried Priya to it, and Lou's knee clicked the whole way. Marta looked at the stake and the knots. She looked at Lou. She pulled the belt across Priya and checked it twice.

"Foot up on the dash," Lou said. "Keep it up there."

"Somebody finish the pit," Priya said, through the window. She was gray. The pencil was still behind her ear. "Thirty centimeters. Write down what you find."

Marta took her off to the hospital. It was a long way into town, and she took the washboard slowly.

The three of them stood in the path and watched the truck go. Lou was closing the latches on the kit.

"Where'd you learn to do that, Lou?" Grace said.

"Combat."

Her head came around. So did Elijah's.

Lou looked from one of them to the other. "I'm a veteran," he said. "Did you guys not know that?"

"No, Lou."

"Oh." He seemed honestly surprised. "To most people it's obvious."

"Well, you're not like any veteran I've ever met," Grace said.

"Amen to that," Elijah said.

"Well." Lou got up off the knee in two stages and collected his coffee from the post. "You guys need to get out more."

He went back up the row. Elijah waited for the rest of it. With Lou there was usually a story. There wasn't one.

He got back down in the pit. At thirty centimeters there was a white root. He wrote it down.
