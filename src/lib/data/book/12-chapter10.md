The co-op needed money the way every real thing needs money, not dramatically, just constantly, and in Elijah's first spring there it was Denny, of all people, who said the obvious thing out loud at a Friday dinner: "People pay for what we know. We give it away to anyone who walks into the shed. Why does the walking matter?"

So they decided to put the co-op on the internet, and Elijah, who had spent years studying exactly how the attention machine worked, who had pages of arithmetic on engagement in his notebook, volunteered to run it. This is the story of how he was wrong in a brand-new way, which by then he'd learned to recognize as the shape of his education.

He did everything correctly. That was the problem. He studied what performed: the pacing, the thumbnails with the circled arrows, the seven-second hook, the open loops ("what we found in the soil SHOCKED us, but first"). He wrote Denny a script for the greenhouse-controller story that was, by every metric he knew how to measure, optimal. Denny read four lines of it on camera, stopped, and looked over the phone at Elijah like a man being asked to wear someone else's dentures.

"Nobody talks like this," Denny said.

"The algorithm," Elijah began.

"I'm not talking to the algorithm. I'm talking to a guy on his couch who just got the letter I got in 2019." He put the script face-down on the workbench. "Point the phone. I'm just going to say what happened."

He told it in one take, hands in frame the whole time because he talks with them, the plasma table ticking as it cooled somewhere behind him. What happened was eleven years in a warehouse, the arms coming in, the county's folder, the second ladder, the second folder. And then the part Elijah hadn't scripted because Elijah didn't know it yet: what it was like, at fifty-one, to bolt together a machine that *feeds people* and feel the specific weight of a tool in your hand versus a lanyard around your neck. "Everybody's asking what the machines are going to do to us," Denny said to the phone, at the end. "Wrong question. I spent years as the answer to that question, and it nearly killed me. The right question is what we build while everybody else is busy asking. Here's ours. Come see it. Bring your kid."

Elijah posted both versions, because he was still a data scientist in his soul and you always run the A/B.

The optimized cut did what optimized things do now: it landed in a feed that felt, to him, mostly machine-written already, synthetic scripts read by synthetic voices over synthetic pictures, an ocean of beige competence, and it *matched the ocean*. Forty-one views. He watched it between the other videos in his feed and heard the resemblance: the same urgent pauses, the same little promises withheld until after the next cut. He had spent two weeks teaching a human being to sound like a format.

Denny's single take, shot vertical with a thumb over the mic for the first three seconds, moved the way true things move now: slowly for a day, then handed person to person like contraband. The comments weren't comments, they were *testimony*: forklift drivers, paralegals, a woman who'd trained her own replacement twice. Several wrote that a friend had sent the link. One asked where to get the controller's repair card. Elijah couldn't turn any of that into a law about what the internet rewarded. He could answer them.

Elijah watched the numbers climb for a while, and then, Sal's rule, closed the laptop and went to find Denny, who was in the shed pressing enclosure cases, unaware he was becoming a public figure at roughly a thousand people an hour.

"It's working," Elijah said. "The real one. Not mine."

"Course not yours." Denny didn't look up from the press. "You tried to make bait. Bait's a picture of food." He stamped another case. "I put out food."

They made it official at the next Friday dinner: the channel was Denny's. Elijah ran the pipes: the posting, the backups, the links to the free plans and templates riding under every video like cargo under a truck. The voice, the face, the editorial line, the *ownership*, were Denny Osei's, and the co-op wrote down how revenue would be shared and how members could keep their lives out of the footage. Denny could say no to the camera any day he wanted. Years after a spreadsheet deleted him from a warehouse, he owned a distribution channel the size of a small radio station, built on the one asset no model could inflate away: he'd been there, it had happened to him, and you could hear it.

That evening he recorded a short correction to the repair card. The first version had named the wrong terminal. No hook, no music, no suspense. He pointed to the screw and said which one he meant.

## The Foundations

If your days go to consuming content about things you can't control, the days are gone and nothing's changed. The line between the people who come through this period with some footing and the people who get flattened by it is a habit: **create more than you consume.**

Before anything else, the fine print that isn't fine print: nobody earns dinner this way. There's no moral accounting in which a person who makes more deserves to eat more. Reading, resting, watching, and receiving care are parts of a life. A restaurant needs people who want to sit down and eat; a musician hopes somebody will listen. We don't need a new contempt for receiving in order to value making. Create over consume is a way to take your time back, not a toll.

You don't need deep pockets to start. You need a result you can point at.

## Step 1: Start with your hands

Change your surroundings before you try to change your thinking.

1. **Clean something up.** Find a neglected stretch of your neighborhood: a park edge, an alley, an empty lot. With permission where it's needed, clean it, and finish the whole route, not just the photo: collect what the local trash and recycling service will take, get it into that stream, and come back in a week to see whether it stayed clean. Chapter 17 has more on doing this well.
2. **Document it.** Take before-and-after photos. Write down what it took. That's the beginning of turning raw reality into something you made.
3. **Optional: own a few watts.** If you'd like, a small solar panel with a charge controller and a battery pack sized to keep a phone and a radio alive is a good first build. Get someone who's done it to walk you through it, or buy a ready-made kit. It's a project, not an entry fee.

## Step 2: Civic connection

The time you spend on digital fear is time you could spend in your physical community. Find a place that needs human hands: a community garden, a food pantry, a neighborhood center.

- **Direct exchange.** Show up and work. Labor moves you from the world of digital opinion to the world of physical results.
- **The human filter.** Working alongside people is how you find the ones who also want to be useful. You won't find them on a screen. You'll find them on the ground.

## Step 3: Find your node

As the habit grows, you'll need a place to work. That doesn't mean renting a shop. It means finding space nobody's using.

- **Look for underused spaces:** barns, empty garages, forgotten community halls, the church basement that's dark six days a week.
- **The intergenerational exchange.** Find an older neighbor whose house has more room than they use. Offer real help: fixing the leak, doing the yard work, driving to appointments. In exchange, ask to use the spare room or the garage as a workspace. You get space; they get reliable help and company. Write down what each of you is agreeing to, so it stays a good deal for both.

## Share a result people can judge

Denny's first useful act on camera was to stop performing someone else's idea of him. He had knowledge a machine could help him spread, and the right to decide whether he wanted to spread it at all.

When you share what you made, say what you tried, what it cost, what happened, and what you still don't know. A failed attempt with clear conditions can save the next group real money. And get consent before someone's hardship becomes part of your story. People don't owe the internet their hardest day in exchange for help.

## The slop-ocalypse

The internet is filling up.

If you've spent any time online lately, you've felt it: search results padded with machine-written affiliate pages, feeds full of uncanny synthetic images, comment sections where bots argue with bots. Call it the slop-ocalypse. And underneath the annoyance is a real feedback loop with real math.

A large language model learns the statistics of the text it was trained on, the probability of each next word given the ones before, and it generates by sampling from what it learned. Now flood the internet with that model's output, and train the next model on the flooded internet. In 2024, a team led by Ilia Shumailov published a study in *Nature* showing what happens when models train, generation after generation, on data produced by earlier models. [Shumailov et al., AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y)

Model it like this. Call the real human data distribution $P_0(x)$. Each new generation $k$ learns from samples of the one before:

> $$P_k(x) = \int q_k(x | y) P_{k-1}(y) \, dy$$

where $q_k$ stands for all the small approximation and sampling errors of model $M_k$. Two things happen.

**The tails get pruned.** Models favor high-probability outputs and under-sample the rare ones. Over generations, the spread of the distribution shrinks. The weird hand-made engineering trick, the specific local history, the odd brilliant idea, those live in the tails, and they're the first to go.

**The errors compound.** A model's small biases become the next model's training data, and the next. In the stylized limit the researchers describe, the distribution collapses toward a single, generic spike: the most average possible answer, over and over. A copy of a copy of a copy.

![Model collapse generation by generation: a wide, rich human-data distribution at Generation 0 narrows to a piled-up mean at Generation 1 and finally to a single degenerate spike at Generation 5](/book-images/ch10-model-collapse.svg)

*Model collapse across one training lineage. The tails, the edge cases and the genius, go first.*

The practical conclusion isn't that the machines are doomed. The labs know about this, and they work hard to keep real human data in the mix. It's that real human data just got more valuable. Something that actually happened, recorded by the person it happened to, is now the scarce input. That's your leverage.

And the energy bill is real too. Generating a page of text on a big model costs on the order of a kilojoule. Tiny for one page, enormous across trillions of automated pages, most of which nobody will ever read. They're running hot. You can run cold: a paper book on small-engine repair, or a saved offline copy of a good manual, keeps delivering for no energy at all.

## Algorithmic judo

Hiding in the woods with paper books isn't the plan, though. Millions of real people are still in the feed, scrolling past the synthetic flood, looking for any sign of an actual human. You can reach them. You just can't do it by impersonating the machine.

Post a dry technical document, "How to Wire a Small Solar Kit and Run a Local Model," and the recommendation system will likely bury it. Its job is to keep people watching, and a dry manual makes people close the app. So you practice algorithmic judo: you wrap useful, practical knowledge inside a human story strong enough that the system carries it for you, then you hand the reader something real. You use the machine's own momentum to throw it.

![The Algorithmic Judo pipeline: a high-utility practical core wrapped in a true human story is scored highly by the algorithm and delivered to a person who unlocks the core](/book-images/ch10-algorithm-unlock.svg)

*Algorithmic judo. A dry manual scores near zero; wrapped in a true story, the algorithm carries it for you.*

Three rules.

**Rule 1: The hook has to be true.** The system boosts what people can't look away from, and what people can't look away from, it turns out, is each other, actually telling the truth. Don't write "Here's how to test your soil." Don't write a manufactured outrage either; the cargo has to be true, or the whole thing is just another picture of food. Write what happened: "I retrained twice and the ladder moved both times. Here's the machine we built instead, and here's how to build one." That's Denny's video. The machine has flooded the world with pictures of food. The remaining economy of human attention runs on the difference between that and a meal.

**Rule 2: Pivot to the practical.** Once they're reading, move quickly from the story to the steps: the parts list, the wiring diagram, the schedule, the phone number of the food pantry. The reader came for the person and stays for the thing they can use.

**Rule 3: Point off the platform.** Don't end on "like and subscribe." End with something physical: "Write this on an index card. Put the phone down. Find two neighbors who'll help you try it this weekend." The feed is a temporary delivery truck, not the destination.

## The analog blind spot

A platform can log every click that crosses its servers. It can predict your next purchase with unsettling accuracy. But it has a big blind spot: the analog commons. It can't hear a conversation held around a physical table. It can't index a hand-drawn plan passed from neighbor to neighbor. It can't easily see two neighbors trading a morning of carpentry for a box of tomatoes. (The tax authorities, for the record, still count barter as income. Invisible to an algorithm isn't the same as outside the law.)

That's the real play: use their expensive digital network as a temporary, free distribution channel to build durable physical ones. Their silicon, our stone.

## Make the handoff small

If the thing you made helps, leave behind something another person can actually use: a corrected pickup schedule, a short guide, a labeled record, a contact who agreed to be contacted. If it fails, write down why and stop spending on it until there's a reason to try again. Don't create a project on someone else's behalf that they now have to maintain.

The next chapter introduces your robotic court: machines that work for you, on hardware you can own. Its first job is food.

## Precedent P-13: The Abbot and the Press (Sponheim and Mainz, 1492 to 1494)

Forty years after Gutenberg, the abbot Johannes Trithemius wrote *De Laude Scriptorum*, "In Praise of Scribes." Hand-copying, he argued, was devotional work worth keeping, and he doubted that paper would last as long as parchment. He also saw that a printer would never bother with plenty of texts that still deserved to survive. [History of Information, Trithemius](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=337)

Then he wanted people to actually read his argument. So he had it printed: Peter Friedberg's press in Mainz, 1494. [Paderborn University Library, 1494 printed edition](https://digital.ub.uni-paderborn.de/eab/content/structure/1387797)

The legend calls that hypocrisy. It's something more useful. Trithemius didn't think printing was evil; he thought it didn't do everything, and he used it for the job it did well. And the old skills weren't gone either. Forty years earlier, in August 1456, a craftsman named Heinrich Cremer wrote his name into a paper copy of the Gutenberg Bible now in France's national library, recording that he'd done its illumination, rubrication, and binding. [Bibliothèque nationale de France, Gutenberg Bible](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/la-bible-de-gutenberg) The press made the text. A human hand still finished the book. The unit of change is often smaller than the whole occupation: one task moves, another stays, and the terms change for the people doing them.

**The mechanism.** Watch what a new machine's critics do, not only what they write. Using the new tool while defending what the old one did well isn't hypocrisy; it's the normal sequence. The question is always what each method is actually for.

**The rule.** Choose a medium by the job it does. You don't get to sit out the tools of your era on aesthetic grounds; you only get to choose whether you use them to make something or merely to complain.

**The practice.**

1. Run the abbot test on yourself, once. For one week, tally two numbers each day: hours spent consuming commentary about the transition, and hours spent making anything at all. Don't moralize the numbers. Just get them. Your ratio is your actual position, whatever your opinions are.
2. Ship one made thing this week using a tool you have complaints about. A document, a repaired fence, a video, a loaf, a raised bed. Size doesn't matter. End the week as someone whose hands touched the new world instead of someone who only reviewed it.
3. When you read the next elegant essay about AI ruining everything, check the workflow before you absorb the thesis: how was it written, published, and distributed? Then keep whatever part of the argument survives. The abbot was right that some things deserved hands.
