# APPENDIX D: THE PRECEDENT LEDGER

*Every chapter of this book closes with a precedent: a documented case of people meeting a technological wave before this one. This is the full index. The serial numbers run in reading order, P-01 through P-23. The dates run from the first farms to the week the web was called a letdown. Same wave, different water. None of them proves a date for AGI or a law of progress. Each one is a question to bring to your own situation.*

## How to run the Ledger

A ledger you read once is trivia. A ledger you operate is an instrument. The difference between a reader and a practitioner is procedure, not sincerity. Here's the procedure.

1. **Read every entry twice.** The first read is a story about them: monks, weavers, film executives. The second read is a story about you with the nouns changed. You'll know the second read happened because it's slightly uncomfortable.
2. **Locate yourself.** At any given moment you're living inside one or two precedents more than the others. Use the symptom index below to find today's, and reread that entry before you make today's decision.
3. **Name your seat.** Every precedent has three seats: the incumbent defending the beachfront, the panicker demanding the wave stop, and the builder working out new terms. Say out loud which seat you're in right now. The rules exist to move you to the third.
4. **Name the difference, too.** For every entry, write down one way your situation is like it and one way it isn't. Who benefited, who carried the cost, and what would count against the lesson? A precedent is a prompt for investigation, not permission to ignore a present objection.
5. **Run the rule within twenty-four hours.** Find one real decision on your desk this week and pass it through the rule. Small is fine.
6. **Do the practices as written.** Each entry closes with three actions sized for a week. If you only have time for one, do the one that makes you defensive.
7. **Teach one entry to one person.** Pick the precedent your friend, your kid, or your coworker is living inside, and tell it to them as a story, the way Marta tells Gary and Sal. Panic spreads person to person. So does this.

## The rules, in one breath each

- **P-01.** "Kids these days" usually means power just moved toward people who don't need permission. Find out what they know, then test the specific worry.
- **P-02.** Don't ask how impressive the demo was. Ask what the world looks like when it's boring, and what has to be built to get there.
- **P-03.** Timelines, mine included, err by orders of magnitude. Plan by doors, not dates.
- **P-04.** Read the rule before you turn it into a morality play. Then ask who wrote it, and what they sell.
- **P-05.** A capability lasts only as long as someone sustains it, and its reach never settles its purpose.
- **P-06.** People survive being decentered. Ask whose authority needed the old map.
- **P-07.** Measure the task without pricing the person as obsolete, and audit your own ledger before someone else does.
- **P-08.** Spend your energy on position, not on whether, and count welfare separately from adoption.
- **P-09.** Take the livelihood claim seriously before judging the tactic. Fight for terms, not against physics.
- **P-10.** Stop arguing about the machine's soul and start writing its invoice.
- **P-11.** Persuasion wears your values. Ask who profits if you feel this.
- **P-12.** Name the dependency and the disruption. Decide in advance which side of the cascade your street is on.
- **P-13.** Choose a medium by the job it does, and make something instead of only complaining.
- **P-14.** Don't refuse the new machine. Use it to pay for what you want to keep.
- **P-15.** Ask whose land and whose opportunity. Then start your paperwork before the confusion clears.
- **P-16.** Look for the version that ships as a kit, and count the work outside the box.
- **P-17.** Seeing the wave isn't the hard part. Find the line item that charges people for inconvenience: it's a countdown clock.
- **P-18.** Your product is mortal. Your capabilities travel, if you can name them in time and have somewhere to stand while you do.
- **P-19.** Standby capacity is real, and a vegetable harvest is a contribution, not a complete diet.
- **P-20.** Make it clear enough to discuss, and own enough of your press that nobody else gets the last edit.
- **P-21.** Build a toolset you understand, can repair, and can't be locked out of.
- **P-22.** A premortem that works looks like paranoia that wasn't needed. Judge the reasoning, not the silence.
- **P-23.** Today's version of a compounding technology is the worst it will ever be. Let results change the plan, including the optimistic one.

## Sourcing, and the blacklist

Every entry's sources are in Appendix B. Historical interpretation stays distinct from the events it describes, company histories are labeled as company histories, and where only a catalog record or a later account was available, the entry says so.

Several famous technology quotes are fabrications, and they were left out of this book on purpose. You won't find the Western Union memo calling the telephone "a toy," Bill Gates's "640K ought to be enough for anybody," the 1894 "nine feet of manure" prophecy, the Bavarian medical board's decree that watching a train go past would give bystanders brain disease, or "everything that can be invented has been invented," because none of them survives contact with a primary source. The Bavarian decree deserves naming specifically: it's the most-cited example of railway technophobia on the internet, and no archival record of it has ever been produced. Losing it costs nothing. The documented resistance to the railway, from canal interests fighting it in Parliament to the "railway spine" diagnoses of the 1860s, is real and was always the better material. History is a strong enough argument without inventing any of it.
