# Appendix G: Your First Year

*Every precedent in this book ends with three practices, seventy-two in all. Here they are in an order you can live: twelve months, each with a theme and a handful of actions. The tag after each one (P-12, say) points back to the story it came from, so you can reread why it matters. Move a month if your season or your life needs it. Don't skip the uncomfortable ones; they're usually the ones doing the work.*

## Month 1: Get your bearings

1. Turn off notifications, put the phone in a drawer that closes, and do one real thing before noon. (P-11)
2. For one week, tally two numbers each day: hours consuming commentary about the transition, and hours making anything at all. (P-13)
3. Start a timeline file. Every time an expert publishes an AI timeline, paste it in with the date and the author. Add mine. (P-03)
4. Start a receipts file: every time a credentialed voice calls AI a fad or a bubble, save the headline, the author, and the date. (P-24)
5. Write down the next three times you hear that AI is rotting people's brains, and note what the speaker can no longer supervise. (P-01)

## Month 2: Know your numbers

1. Run the energy audit on paper: what comes in (calories, kilowatt-hours, dollars), what goes out (labor, attention, rent), and who captures the spread. (P-07)
2. Map your tin: the five things your household can't go a month without, and where each one actually comes from. (P-12)
3. Run a household Y2K. One hour, everyone affected present: "It's a year from now and our income got cut in half. What broke first?" Fix one thing this month. (P-22)
4. Set a preparation budget and write it down like a line item: the share of income and hours you'll put into skills, soil, savings, and community. (P-22)

## Month 3: Know your work

1. List the tasks in your job the way a buyer would, then ask a coworker what the list missed. (P-07)
2. Write your capability inventory: one page, no job titles or products allowed. (P-18)
3. Run the beachfront audit: one column for everything you'd have to admit is depreciating if the wave is real, a second for what admitting it would cost you today. (P-17)
4. Write down what you know that isn't written anywhere: the judgment calls and failure modes. Knowledge is leverage while it's still yours to trade. (P-09)
5. If any of your income depends on a license or regulation that keeps competitors out, assume that protection is temporary and plan accordingly. (P-04)

## Month 4: Learn the tools

1. Hold a frontier hour every week: one hour using the newest tools, not reading about them. (P-05)
2. Ask someone at least fifteen years younger than you who uses AI daily for twenty minutes of watching them work. Say nothing for the first ten. (P-01)
3. Ship one made thing this month using a tool you have complaints about. A document, a repaired fence, a loaf. (P-13)
4. Build the Communicator from Appendix E, or at least try Tier 0: one reference you can open with the network off.

## Month 5: Plant something

1. Activate one plot, however small: a raised bed, three buckets on a balcony, a community-garden plot. Measure the harvest honestly. (P-19)
2. Keep one part of your life off the ratchet on purpose: food you can grow, heat you can make, a skill that works without a subscription. (P-08)
3. Read Chapter 15 before you dig, and find out what your ground used to be.

## Month 6: Find your people

1. Take the neighbor census: who within walking distance can fix an engine, stitch a wound, grow food, or wire a panel? Learn it in person. (P-12)
2. Make your garden a network: find the two or three people nearest you who grow anything, and trade one variety. (P-19)
3. Before joining any movement, ask it one question: "What are we building, and what terms are we demanding?" (P-09)

## Month 7: Count the food

1. Build the Rung 1 page from Chapter 19: how much edible food your town throws away, beside how many households can't count on eating. Publish it. (Chapter 19)
2. List what's already free at the door where you live: the library, the school, the roads. Each was once a decision someone fought for. (P-23)
3. Find out what your employer or your grocer does with food it can't sell, and where it could go instead. (Chapter 14)

## Month 8: Own something

1. Write your one-page catalog: every tool you own that you understand, can repair, and can't be remotely disabled. (P-21)
2. Close one gap: learn an owned tool down to the repair level, or replace one rented capability with an owned one. (P-21)
3. Move one expense from rented energy to owned energy: insulation, a bicycle, a properly sized panel and battery. (P-07)
4. Do one kit project you'd normally hire out, permitted where permits apply, documented as you go. (P-16)

## Month 9: Tell it

1. Write your pamphlet, even if it's four pages: the one thing you know from experience that would help the person two years behind you. (P-20)
2. Own your printing press: an email list and a site you control, set up now, even if nobody's reading yet. (P-20)
3. Run the tavern test: read it out loud and rewrite every sentence you'd never say to a neighbor over a fence. (P-20)
4. Leave a kit behind: the steps, the parts, the prices, and the page you wish you'd had at the start. (P-16)

## Month 10: Find ground

1. Open the filing office: one evening of real research into three places you might live or grow, with real prices and real water and zoning notes. (P-15)
2. Look for today's programs before the crowd does: county land banks, community land trusts, conservation leases, garden plots. (P-15)
3. Put a date on it: the day you'll have made a position decision, buy, lease, join, or stay. (P-15)

## Month 11: Go on the record

1. Name one need you'd add to the free-at-the-door list, and write down who would have to decide it. (P-23)
2. Go to one meeting of that body and say out loud, on the record, that the people who live here should get what they need to survive. (P-23)
3. Draft the one clause you'd want in your own trade's deal with the machine, in a single sentence, and say who pays it. (P-10)
4. Find out whether your trade already has a fund or royalty pool, and whether you're enrolled. (P-10)

## Month 12: Look back, then forward

1. Date-stamp your reasoning: one paragraph for each big decision this year, what you knew, what you assumed, and why you acted. (P-22)
2. Ask the Fujifilm question on the calendar: has the wave moved, and which capability do you lead with next year? (P-18)
3. Keep the standby system warm: save seed from one crop and keep the beds alive over winter. (P-19)
4. Go back through this list and pick the one that made you most uncomfortable. That discomfort is a compass. Do that one first. (P-24)

Then start again. The second year is easier, because you won't be doing it alone.
