# Appendix H: Talk About It

*This book is meant to be argued with, out loud, with the people you live near. Here are questions for a book club, a co-op meeting, a classroom, or a kitchen table, and a short list of what to read next. Take the questions in any order. The best conversations usually start with the one that makes somebody in the room uncomfortable.*

## Questions for the Introduction

1. The author says "we don't have to live like this." What, specifically, do you think we don't have to live with? What do you think we do?
2. The United Nations says the world grows enough food for everyone, and the USDA says 30 to 40 percent of the American food supply goes uneaten. Where does the food in your town go when it doesn't sell?
3. "Money should be optional. Not abolished. Optional." What would change in your own week if you didn't need money to eat? What wouldn't?

## Questions for Part I: What is the Singularity?

1. The book defines the Singularity as the point where intelligence stops being scarce. Has the price of any thinking work you know of already started to fall?
2. "A curve gives you a date. A door gives you a Monday." What door, if it opened, would change your work or your household most?
3. In June 2026 the government restricted two AI models and lifted the restriction eighteen days later. Was that a panic, a reasonable precaution, or both?
4. The nine stages get more speculative as they climb. Which stage do you think is furthest from happening? Which is closest?

## Questions for Part II: How Humans React

1. Chapter 7 says "the Entitled" and "the Ready" are mindsets, not kinds of people, and most of us carry a little of both. Where do you notice each in yourself?
2. The Luddites understood exactly what the machines would do to their pay, and still lost. What would they need to have done differently?
3. Musicians ran newspaper ads against "the Robot" in 1930 and enrolled two million people. Is your trade running its own version now? Would you sign the coupon?
4. What's one thing your household depends on that you couldn't replace for a month? Who near you could help if it failed?

## Questions for Part III: How to Survive the Transition

1. Which is harder where you live: getting land, getting tools, or getting people to work together? Why?
2. The book wants the machines that feed people to be owned by the people who depend on them. What would that look like for one machine in your town?
3. France stopped supermarkets from destroying edible food in 2016. What would it take to do the same where you live?
4. Run the premortem from Chapter 18 on a plan your group already has. What fails first?

## Questions for Chapter 19 and the Conclusion

1. Which of the nine rungs is your town already standing on, even partly? Which is the next one it could reach?
2. Rural co-ops wired America's farms when power companies wouldn't because it didn't pay. What need in your community doesn't get met because meeting it doesn't pay?
3. Britain made medical care free at the point of use in 1948. What's already free at the door where you live, and what would you add to the list?
4. The Conclusion asks: how will the people affected share in the benefit? Ask it about the last automation decision you saw. What was the answer?

## What to read next

Most of these are free to read online.

- **Henry David Thoreau, *Walden* (1854), the chapter "Economy."** The original argument that the cost of a thing is the life you trade for it, worked out against the price of a house. [Free at Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/205)
- **Oscar Wilde, *The Soul of Man under Socialism* (1891).** A short, funny, serious essay about letting machines do the necessary work so people can do the rest. [Free at Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1017)
- **Peter Kropotkin, *Fields, Factories and Workshops* (1899, revised 1913).** The case for putting the factory amidst the fields, and for regions that can feed and supply themselves. [Free at Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/64353)
- **John Maynard Keynes, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930).** A famous economist predicts the end of the economic problem, and worries about what we'll do next. [Free to read](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/keynes/1930/our-grandchildren.htm)
- **Aneurin Bevan, *In Place of Fear* (1952).** The man who launched the NHS explains why care should never depend on the means to pay. [Chapter 5, transcribed](https://publicmatters.org.uk/2019/02/05/aneurin-bevans-1952-essay-on-the-nhs-chapter-5-of-in-place-of-fear/)
- **Charles Mackay, *Memoirs of Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds* (1841).** Bubbles, manias, and panics, and how people recover their senses "slowly, and one by one." [Free at Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/24518)
- **Vernor Vinge, "The Coming Technological Singularity" (1993).** The short essay that set the modern terms of the argument, including its four roads. [Free at the author's site](https://edoras.sdsu.edu/~vinge/misc/singularity.html)
- **The *Whole Earth Catalog*, Fall 1968.** Access to tools, as a book. Flip through it and see how much of this one it predicted. [Digitized issue](https://wholeearth.info/p/whole-earth-catalog-fall-1968)
- **Katja Grace and colleagues, "Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI" (2024).** What the people building these systems actually expect, and how fast their forecasts moved. [Free on arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843)
- **Brian Merchant, *Blood in the Machine* (2023).** The Luddites as they actually were: skilled workers who understood exactly what the machines were for.
- **Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir, *Scarcity* (2013).** How not having enough takes up room in your head, and why that matters for every argument about work and welfare.
- **Shoshana Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019).** The business model that turns your attention and data into someone else's product.
