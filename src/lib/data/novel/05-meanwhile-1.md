# Meanwhile

Aaron Bidwell did not read. He had a person for that.

The person sat across from him at forty-one thousand feet with a tablet and a stack of one-page summaries. Six years on the job had taught the reader one rule. Start with what he owns.

“Claypot,” the reader said. “Design software. You’ve held the controlling piece since 2019.”

“Have I been there?”

“No.”

“Good. Go on.”

“They announced a product this morning. It makes pictures. They trained it on everything their customers ever stored with them. Twenty years of it. They’re calling that the corpus.”

“The what?”

“Corpus. The body of work. Everything anyone ever made with the software.”

Bidwell looked out the window at Nevada, which he also owned some of.

“And the customers just gave it to them.”

“They agreed to terms.”

“That’s the same thing. What’s it worth?”

“They project four hundred million in new revenue in the first…”

Bidwell lifted one finger. The reader stopped.

He was quiet for a while. He was not a stupid man.

“So they had a pile of what everybody did,” he said. “Lying around. And nobody knew it was worth a dime until a machine showed up hungry.”

“Roughly, yes.”

“What else has one?”

“A corpus?” The reader thought. “Hospitals. Courts. Trucking. Anything that keeps records.”

“Does farmland have one?”

“Farmers keep records. Yields, water, what the soil did. Mostly in notebooks. Mostly in their heads.”

“So nobody owns it.”

“Nobody’s collected it.”

“Hm,” Bidwell said.

He held his empty glass out into the aisle without looking, and somebody took it, because somebody always did.

“Find out what the dirt costs,” he said.
