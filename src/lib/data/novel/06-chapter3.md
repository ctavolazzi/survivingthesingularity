# Chapter 3: 9:40 on Sunday

The papers got worse after midnight. Not harder. Worse.

Elijah had built a ritual out of it by then, three weeks after the all-hands: dinner, dishes, one beer he mostly didn’t drink, and then the folding chair in front of the tower, reading with the notebook open on his knee. He’d started with the survey papers, the respectable ones with their hedged abstracts, and worked his way down the citation chains like a man following a creek into a canyon.

Recursive self-improvement. Automated machine-learning research. Systems proposing edits to their own training loops. Every paper had a sentence in it somewhere, written flat, that should have been printed in red. In one it was *the model was permitted to modify its own optimization procedure.*

Somewhere down one of those chains he found the lecture.

It was twelve years old and the sound was bad. A small woman stood at a whiteboard in a room with maybe thirty people in it, and she never once looked at the camera. The comments under the video called her the Architect. Everyone in his field did. She had drawn half the diagrams he’d been reading for three weeks, and then she had co-founded PorusAI and stopped giving lectures.

She wrote two sets of letters on the board and underlined each one.

“AGI,” she said. “Artificial general intelligence. People make this complicated. It is not. It is a machine that can do any work a person can do sitting down.”

She tapped the second one.

“ASI. Artificial superintelligence. A machine that does that work better than all of us put together. Including the work of building the next machine.”

Somebody in the room asked a question the microphone didn’t catch. She waited a long time before she answered.

“I am often asked if I am afraid of it,” she said. “I am not. I am afraid of us.”

Elijah wrote it all down. Then he watched it again.

The vertigo didn’t come from any one paper. It came when he stopped reading and worked out where it was all going.

He knew exponentials. Not from math class, from the farm. He’d watched star thistle take the fallow field behind the barn the summer his father got sick. Nothing, nothing, a patch you could clear in an afternoon and kept meaning to. Then one morning the whole field was silver-green to the fence line, and an afternoon wasn’t going to do it.

The curve lies to you at the beginning. It sits there looking manageable right up until the knee.

Every system so far improved because people improved it. Grad students, coffee, conference deadlines. The loop ran at the speed of human careers.

But every lab he could name was working, in public, with a straight face, on closing that loop. On getting the machine to improve itself. Her second definition. And the moment that loop closed, the speed limit wasn’t careers anymore. It was watts and silicon, the same wall his tower had hit, except the things hitting it would be designing their own better cooling.

*Compound interest on thinking,* he wrote. Under the five stages he added two more.

*6. It improves itself.*

*7. It leaves.*

Then he sat very still, because he was dizzy.

---

The phone rang at 9:40 on Sunday, because it always rang at 9:40 on Sunday.

His mother lived ten miles out of town, on the farm. She called every Sunday at 9:40, when her day ended, whether she had seen him that week or not.

“You sound tired,” she said, before hello. Behind her voice he could hear the kitchen radio, the one that had played the same country station since before he was born, and water running, and the clank of the pot she never let anyone else wash.

“I’m okay, Ma. How’s the well?”

“Pump’s doing that thing again. Manny says it’s the pressure switch. Your uncle says it’s the pump.” A pause, the sound of the pot being set in the rack. “The Hollis place sold. Some outfit from Sacramento. Nobody’s seen a person yet, just trucks.”

He watched his screen dim itself and tried, for the third Sunday in a row, to build the bridge. This time he had the Architect’s sentences.

“Ma, you know the computer stuff I do. There’s a thing they’re building. A machine that can do any work a person can do sitting down.”

“Like the phone tree at the feed store.”

“No. Any work. And then the next one does it better than all of us put together, and it builds the one after that. Not us making them better. Them, doing it, to themselves.” He heard how it sounded and pushed on anyway. “Remember the thistle, the back field? How it looked like nothing and then it was everything? It’s like that, except the field is thinking. And when it’s done with us, Ma, I don’t think it fights us. I think it leaves. Builds its own way off the planet and goes, and we’re just… left here. With each other.”

The radio played. The water ran and shut off.

“Honey,” his mother said, gently, in the voice she used to use for fevers, “they had that at the Hollis dairy years back. The robot milkers. Cows liked them fine. It’s just tools, baby. There’s always somebody has to fix the tools. That’ll be you. You always were the smart one.” He could hear her smiling. “Are you eating? You sound thin.”

Elijah stood at the window with the phone against his ear and understood that he had failed, and that he was going to keep failing, and that it wasn’t her fault.

She had room in her head for tools, weather, blight, banks, every disaster the farm had ever taught her, and he was trying to hand her something with no handle on it anywhere. He didn’t have a sentence that would carry it. He hadn’t had one for the realtors either. They had only needed to watch it work.

“I’m eating, Ma,” he said. “Tell Manny it’s the pressure switch.”

After she hung up he stood there a long time. Outside, over the parking lot, the sky had that washed orange glow, but high up through it he could pick out one moving point of light, a satellite dragging itself across the dark.

*It won’t be dramatic,* he thought. *That’s what nobody gets.* It would look like this.

He went back to the notebook and wrote, under the seven stages:

*It won’t take the machines away first. It takes the people who see it coming, out of the world everyone they love still lives in.*
