# Chapter 6: For Friends and Family

Elijah got laid off on a Tuesday, eleven months after the toast.

The meeting was short. They called it a restructuring. There had been a moment, while a woman from HR read off her page, when he’d imagined saying the whole thing out loud: the realtors, the pipeline with his name in the commits, the fence, the notebook. But when he imagined giving it, he could tell the speech was for him.

So he said thank you. Claypot’s system scheduled his exit interview with an AI that thanked him for his candor. The badge went dead in his hand at the turnstile with a small red light, and that was his career at Claypot.

He’d stopped at the fourth floor on his way out. Devendra was at the good coffee machine. The sticky note was still on the side of it, curled now, the ink going brown.

“Thanksgiving,” Elijah said.

“I’ll bring wine,” Devendra said. He shook Elijah’s hand and held on a second, the way you do with someone who’s moving to a country you don’t plan to visit.

---

At home he made eggs and looked at his phone.

He had three chats that mattered. One was the band, which was him and Nate and Wes and seventeen years of load-in times. One was his mother, who had a chat all to herself because she answered every message twice. One was David, who answered with a thumb.

He had explained it to every one of them, one at a time, for a year. He’d explained it across the bar and over the gate latch and in the van. He was tired of his own voice doing it. He was tired of the look.

*Just say it once,* he thought. *Say it once, all the way through, and then they can’t say you never told them.*

He propped the phone on the folding chair, the way he used to prop it on a steering wheel in a parking lot. He sat down on the floor in front of the Fisher with the notebook. He pushed record before he could think about his hair.

“Hey. Um. It’s Elijah.”

He looked at the notebook. He looked back up.

“So I got laid off today. That’s not the news. I’ve been keeping this notebook for about a year, and I’m going to read you a list, and then I’m going to give you a date. And you can screenshot the date and make fun of me later. That’s fine. I’d love that, actually.”

He held the notebook up. He held it too close and it went out of focus, and he didn’t notice.

“So where I worked, we built a machine that makes pictures. Other places built ones that write. They’re all the same kind of thing and they’re all getting better, like, every few months. And here’s what I think happens. One. Everybody tries to make money off it. That’s now. That’s where we are. Two. It gets loose and it scares somebody important and they try to shut it off. Three. They turn it back on, because by then everything runs on it. Four.”

He stopped. He rubbed his eye with the heel of his hand.

“Four is the one I care about. You work, you eat. Right? That’s the deal. That’s been the deal for everybody we know and everybody they came from. And I think that deal ends. Not because anybody’s evil. Because there’s going to be a machine that can do any job you can do sitting down. And then pretty soon the standing-up ones. And nobody, I mean nobody, has written down what the new deal is.”

A truck went by outside. He waited for it.

“Five is people get really mad. I’m not going to do six and seven. My mom’s watching this. Hi, Ma.”

He turned the page, and then turned it back, because there wasn’t anything on the next page he wanted.

“Okay. The date. I think the machine that can do any sitting-down job shows up by Thanksgiving, 2027. That’s four years. I bet a guy dinner on it. Loser cooks. I’m not telling you to quit your job. I’m saying four years is enough time to plant something. Learn how to fix something. Figure out who your people are. That’s it. That’s the whole thing.”

He looked straight at the lens.

“I’m not selling anything. I just want you to see what’s coming. Okay. Love you guys.”

He reached forward and stopped it. Four minutes and eleven seconds. He didn’t watch it back. He knew if he watched it back he’d never send it.

He had a page from the music days that still said *Elijah Madrone, Second Crop* at the top, with a photo of him holding a Telecaster at the Gravenstein fair in 2016. It had two hundred followers. Most of them had seen him play at least once, and about thirty of them were related to Wes. He posted the video there, with no caption, because he couldn’t think of one. Then he sent the link to the three chats and typed the same thing under each.

*For friends and family. It’s four minutes. Please just watch it.*

He washed the egg pan and went to bed.

---

Sometime after two the phone started buzzing.

He’d left it on the Fisher, face down, and it rattled on the steel plate loud enough to hear from the bed. It stopped. It started again. He lay there in the dark and listened to it for a while.

He put the pillow over his head.

In the morning the phone was warm. He didn’t look at the middle of the screen, where the numbers were. He scrolled straight down to the three chats.

His mother had written at 6:10. *I watched it. You look thin.* And then at 6:12, because she always sent two: *Is that Mom’s stove?*

David had sent a thumb.

Nate had sent a thumb.

Wes hadn’t sent anything.
