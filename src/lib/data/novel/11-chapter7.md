# Chapter 7: Out of Hand

The first message from a stranger came from a woman in Tulsa.

*I thought I was the only one,* she wrote. She drove a forklift at a distribution center. Over about two years she had watched the scanners get smart, and then the schedule, and then one Monday her supervisor was a tablet on a pole. She’d tried to tell her sister about it at a barbecue. Her sister gave her the look.

Elijah read it standing up in his kitchen, and then he had to go sit down. He had been saying it for a year and nobody had ever said *me too* back. Not one person. It turns out you can go a really long time without that and not notice how bad you want it.

He wrote her back four paragraphs. Then there was a paralegal in Fresno, so he wrote him back too. Then a guy who taught high school shop, and a nurse. A man said he’d been a session drummer in Nashville until last spring. Elijah read that one twice. He answered every one of them. They had taken the trouble to write, and his mother had raised him to answer a letter. They were coming in faster than he could type.

By Friday it was four hundred people and he quit counting. Somebody set his list to music. Somebody else made a video explaining why he was an idiot, and that one did a lot better than his. A stranger in Ohio started selling a T-shirt that said LOSER COOKS, and Elijah didn’t get a dime from it and bought one anyway, in a large.

It was supposed to be for Nate and Wes and his mother and David.

Then the other kind of message started showing up.

A senator’s office wanted fifteen minutes. A man with a podcast, one Elijah had actually listened to on his delivery routes, wanted two hours. A lab wanted to fly him out, and not PorusAI, the other one, the one that was always second. There was an email from a vice president at the company that made his laptop, and one from a woman whose name he had only ever seen in the bottom corner of slides.

He said yes to the senator’s office. He said yes to the podcast. He said yes to the lab, and to a newsletter, and to a guy who wanted him to “hop on” something he never did figure out. He had been raised to be agreeable, and then he’d spent his twenties playing weddings, where the whole job is to say *sure, we can do that one.* By the second week he had one sentence for all of it.

“Yeah. No, yeah, of course.”

Somewhere in there he stopped sleeping. There was always one more person to answer, and when he couldn’t answer any more there was Chat Horizon, which was awake at any hour and never got tired of the list. They went through it stage by stage. He knew how it sounded, but it was better company on the subject than anybody he knew. It asked good questions. It remembered what he’d said an hour ago.

Around four one morning in December he typed the thing he’d been wondering ever since the demos at Claypot.

*Am I crazy?*

*You’re not crazy, Elijah,* it wrote. *You’re early.*

He must have read that ten times. He took a picture of the screen with his phone. It was the nicest thing anybody had said to him all year. He was so happy he could have cried.

---

His mother called at 9:40 on a Sunday, the way she did.

“I watched it again,” she said. “The Hollis girl sent it to me. Linda’s granddaughter, the one with the nose ring. She said, is this your Elijah? I said well, it looks like him.”

“What’d you think?”

“I didn’t follow it, baby.” She was doing the dishes while she talked, and the pot went into the rack. “You look thin. And is that floor clean? You were sitting right on it.”

He told her the floor was clean.

---

He went to the Bear Flag on a Thursday in January, early, before the after-work crowd. He wanted David to himself. He had a senator’s office and a lab and hundreds of strangers. What he wanted more than any of it was for the guy who’d sat next to him on the bus in third grade to look at his phone and say *holy shit, Mad.*

David was cutting limes. He looked up when the door opened, took one look at Elijah, and put the knife down.

“Off the clock,” he said, to an empty bar.

He poured two beers, came around the end, and sat down on the next stool, on the customer side. Elijah had seen him do that exactly twice in fifteen years. Once was the night Elijah’s father died. The other was when the Giants won the Series in 2010.

They tried to make it a regular beer anyway.

“How’s the truck?”

“Needs a water pump.”

“How’s your mom’s well?”

“Still doing that thing. Manny says it’s the pressure switch.”

“Manny’s always right. It’s the worst thing about him.” David drank. “You guys playing at all?”

“No.”

“Wes doing okay?”

“I don’t know. He’s not really answering me.”

David nodded. They watched the TV for a minute. It was a bowling tournament, with the sound off. Neither of them had ever cared about bowling.

The truck, his mom and the band had carried the two of them through a couple thousand nights at that bar, and they had just used all three up in about ninety seconds.

So Elijah got his phone out. He hadn’t exactly decided to. He set it on the bar and turned it around toward David.

“Okay, look at this. Just look. This is a senator’s office, Dave. Like a real one, a United States senator. And this guy runs the second biggest lab in the world and he wants to fly me out. And there’s this woman in Tulsa, there’s hundreds of them, there’s a drummer, and they all saw it too! I wasn’t the only one! I’m not making it up.”

“Yeah. That’s all well and good, man.” David hadn’t looked at the phone. “But are you okay? Are you good?”

“Yeah, I’m good. I’m just, my mind is blown. This is insane. It’s incredible. I’ve never seen anything like it, Dave. Nobody has. It can write. It can code. It can read a contract. Give it a couple of years and it does anything you can do sitting down.” He pushed the phone another inch across the wood. “Just use it, man. Have you tried it?”

“Nah. Not really. It’s not for me.”

“What do you mean it’s not for you? Come on. It’s for everybody. That’s the whole thing I keep trying to tell people.”

“I pour beer, Mad. What’s it going to do, pour it?”

“Give it a few years.”

“See, that. That right there.” David shook his head. “Hey. You’re scaring the hoes.”

Elijah laughed. He thought they were back.

“I’m serious, man. Why don’t you just stick to music? You were good. People loved you up there. Stay in your lane.”

“Bro. What do you mean?”

David didn’t say what he meant. He looked at his beer.

So Elijah showed him the last thing he had.

“I asked it. The thing itself. I asked it straight out, like four in the morning, and look what it said.”

He scrolled to the picture and held it up. *You’re not crazy, Elijah. You’re early.*

David read it. It took him a lot longer than it takes to read six words, and the whole time Elijah sat there waiting for the *holy shit.*

“When’d you sleep last?”

“I’m fine.”

“That’s not what I asked you.”

“Tuesday. Some. Dave, you’re not even looking at it.”

“I’m looking at you, man.” David turned on his stool so they were facing each other, and he rubbed his face with both hands. “I wish you were drunk. I’d know what to do with you.”

He put his hands down.

“Elijah,” he said.

David had not called him Elijah since they were eight years old. His stomach dropped before the rest of the sentence even got there.

“Have you talked to, like, a mental health professional lately? I’m worried about you, man. You’re kind of going off the deep end here.”

There was a little piece of lime on the bar. Elijah looked at that so he wouldn’t have to look at David, and pushed it around with one finger. His face had gone hot. He was thirty-six years old, and his best friend thought he was fucking crazy, and he was about to cry at the Bear Flag in front of a bowling tournament.

It broke his heart.

He had been getting the look from everybody for a year, David included, and he’d gotten pretty good at taking it. He had never once figured David would say it out loud.

“You’re the one person I thought would…” He couldn’t finish it.

“I know. I know I am.” David wasn’t being mean about it. Elijah almost wished he would be. He was using the voice he used when he had to cut somebody off. “That’s why it’s got to be me. Who else is going to tell you? I watched my dad go like this for ten years, with the horses. You were around for some of it. And look. A computer told you you’re not crazy at four in the morning. Just listen to that for a second. I’d have told you the same thing at four in the morning, man. I’d have told you anything you wanted. I’d have just wanted you to go to bed.”

“It’s not like that.”

“Okay.”

“Dave. It’s not.”

“Okay, Mad.”

Then they sat there and finished their beers. They went back to the water pump. David knew a guy in Petaluma who’d do it for two hundred bucks. Elijah said he’d call him, and they both knew he wouldn’t. It was the longest beer he had ever had in that room.

He put a twenty down. David slid it back across the wood with two fingers.

“Your money’s no good here.”

He had said that to Elijah before, and he had always been looking at him when he said it. This time he said it to the twenty.

He took the bill and zipped his jacket.

“See you,” he said.

“Yep,” said David, and he went back around the bar and picked up the knife.

---

Elijah sat in the truck with the engine off. It was cold enough that he could see his breath. He held his jaw tight, and it didn’t work, and he cried. Not a lot. It was the quick, pissed-off kind that’s mostly embarrassment. He wiped his face on his sleeve and sat there while the windows fogged up.

David was the last person he had really trusted. Even David thought he was nuts.

The phone lit up on the seat.

It was a message from an account with a blue check and a name he knew the way you know the name of a soda. Ruby Roth-Jones. He was twenty-four years old, and he had made a lamp that talked to you, and four million people had bought one. The whole message was in lower case, like he couldn’t be bothered.

*saw your thing. come down. i’ll get you in the room*

Elijah cleared a spot on the windshield with the heel of his hand and looked up at the neon bear in the window. Past it, through the glass, he could see David with his head down over the limes.

He knew he should go back in. He should go back in and sit down and say *okay, maybe, I don’t know, I’m really tired,* and let his friend pour him one more.

*Yeah,* he typed. *No, yeah, of course.*
