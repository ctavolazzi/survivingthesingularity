# Chapter 8: Out of His League

The lanyard said ELIJAH MADRONA, and he wore it anyway.

It was February, in San Francisco, in a building on the water that used to be a place where they fixed ships. Somebody who worked for Ruby Roth-Jones had sent him the pass. He’d put the new water pump in the truck himself, in his mother’s yard, and driven down that morning. The garage across the street cost forty-eight dollars a day. He sat in the truck for a minute with the ticket in his hand, doing the math on two days, and then he went in.

There was a room just for the speakers. He hadn’t known he was a speaker until a young man with a tablet took him there. It had little sandwiches with no crusts, and a cheese he couldn’t name, and sparkling water in tall cans from a company he’d never heard of. In the corner a robot arm was making lattes. It was slow and it spilled, and six people stood in line anyway, filming it.

He ate four of the sandwiches. He hadn’t had lunch, and the food in that room was better than anything in his kitchen.

The first talk was a man from Washington with a flag pin. It was an election year. He said America would lead. He said nobody in this administration was there to slow anybody down. He said the word *future* nine times. Elijah counted. He did not say the word *rules* once. When he finished the room stood up.

Then people started finding him.

That was the strange part. They knew his face. They had all read the list. A man in a quarter-zip stopped him by the coffee and said, “Stage two. The plug. When?” as if Elijah did the weather. A woman said stage four had gone around her whole office. Somebody’s assistant asked if he’d sign a notebook, a blank one, new, and he did, feeling like a fraud.

Nobody argued with him. Not one person. He’d spent a year being told he’d calibrate, and here were three hundred people in good shoes nodding before he got to the end of the sentence.

It took him until about two o’clock to notice there were two kinds of people in the room.

One kind wanted it slowed down. They wore a lot of gray and they said *off-ramp* and *guardrails*. The other kind wanted it faster. They wore sneakers that cost what his water pump cost, and they said *ship*. Both kinds wanted a picture with him. Both kinds asked him the same question, a little too casually.

“So which are you?”

“I just want people to see what’s coming,” Elijah said.

“Exactly,” said a gray one, and squeezed his arm.

“Exactly,” said a sneakers one, an hour later, and squeezed the same arm.

They would not stand near each other. He watched a gray one cross the whole length of the hall to avoid the coffee line a sneakers one was in. It was like a wedding where the families had already been to court.

A woman was next to him at the urn, filling a paper cup. Forties, a founder of something to do with cooling. She’d been laughing with four people a minute ago. She looked at the room and spoke low, not really to him.

“You know what this is? Nobody in here has a friend.” Then somebody called her name, and her whole face came back on, and she went.

He said yes to things. They were small.

A picture with a man whose company he didn’t catch. A quote for somebody’s slide, which they typed while he was still saying it and cleaned up a little. A seat on the four o’clock panel, because someone’s flight from Austin had been canceled. And a letter. A gray one held out a tablet with a long statement on it about responsible pacing, and a line at the bottom for his finger. He read about half. It seemed fine. People were waiting behind him. He signed it with his finger, and his signature looked like a kid’s.

“Yeah. No, yeah, of course,” he said, each time.

The panel was four chairs and a moderator with a very calm voice. The lights were hot. Elijah had never been on a stage without a guitar in front of him, and he didn’t know what to do with his hands, so he sat on them.

“Elijah, you’ve become sort of the people’s forecaster,” the moderator said, and the room laughed nicely. “Everybody here keeps saying AGI. For the folks at home, what precisely does it mean?”

He knew this. He’d watched the Architect’s old lecture about thirty times. He had her sentence written in the notebook, which was in his bag, which was under his chair.

“It’s, um.” He looked at the lights. “It’s when the thing can do, like. Most things. Most of the things a person does. At a job.”

“Most things.”

“The sitting-down ones.”

The moderator smiled and waited to see if there was more. There wasn’t. The sentence had left his head completely, like a chord he’d played ten thousand times and then one night just didn’t have.

“Well, I think that’s refreshingly honest,” the moderator said, and turned to the woman on his left, who defined it in forty seconds with two citations.

The room was kind about it. People patted him afterward. *So real,* somebody said. It was worse than if they’d laughed.

He went out a side door that said STAFF and stood on a loading dock over the water. It smelled like diesel and low tide. He felt better right away.

“You answer everybody.”

There was a kid sitting on an upturned milk crate by the dumpster. He looked like he’d come to fix the wifi. Black hoodie, hood up, white sneakers with nothing on them. He was eating the same little sandwiches out of a napkin, and his fingernails were bitten down so far that the tips of his fingers looked sore.

“Sorry?”

“The replies. Under your video. You answered all of them. I read them. All four hundred, and then you kept going.” He had a quick, light voice, like someone who’d never had to wait for a turn. “The forklift lady. The drummer. Some guy named Earl who thinks you’re a demon, and you wrote Earl three paragraphs. You have to stop that. It’s the nicest thing I’ve ever seen anybody do and you have to stop.”

“Why?”

“Because there’s four hundred of them and one of you, and next month it’s forty thousand. It’s arithmetic.” He stood up and wiped his hand on his jeans and held it out. “Ruby.”

Elijah knew that. He’d known it from the first word. It was just that the pictures were all of a guy on a stage next to a glowing lamp, and this was a kid by a dumpster with mustard on his sleeve. Ruby Roth-Jones was worth more than the county Elijah grew up in.

“I bombed in there,” Elijah said.

“You were the only one who didn’t sound like a press release. They’ll clip it. It’ll do fine.” Ruby put his hands in the hoodie pocket. “What’s with the stove?”

“What?”

“In the video. You’re sitting in front of a wood stove. Old one. There’s no pipe coming out of it. Why do you have a wood stove with no pipe in an apartment?”

Only his mother had asked about the stove. Not the senator’s office, not the podcast, not one of the replies.

“It was my grandmother’s.”

“Does it work?”

“It would. If I had somewhere to put it.”

“What’d your mom say? About the video. You said hi to her.”

“She said I look thin.”

Ruby laughed. It was a real one, a bark, and it made him look about fifteen. Then he asked about the farm, and what they grew, and whether Elijah could actually drive a tractor or if that was a thing people said. For ten minutes on a loading dock the richest person Elijah had ever met asked him questions and listened to the answers. He was good at it. Elijah noticed that, and then forgot he’d noticed.

“You hate this,” Ruby said, nodding back at the door.

“No. No, it’s great. Everybody’s been really…”

“You hate this. It’s okay. Everybody in there hates it, they’re just better at it than you.” He balled up the napkin and made the shot into the dumpster without looking. “Come on. The real people are at dinner.”

“I’m supposed to be here tomorrow too. And I’m parked in the, there’s a garage.”

“Leave it. I’ll have somebody move your truck.” He was already walking. At the end of the dock there was a black car with a driver in it. Elijah would have sworn it had not been there before. Ruby opened the back door himself and stood there holding it, like a kid holding a gate.

“Get in, Madrone. You can be sad about the panel later.”

Elijah stood on the dock with his misspelled name around his neck. He knew he was out of his league.

He got in.
