# Chapter 10: Cigarettes

Ruby lived at the top of a building with no name on it.

The elevator opened right into the apartment. It was mostly glass, and what wasn’t glass was white, and there was nothing in it Elijah would have sat on. A couch like a slab of marble. A table with one orange on it. Through a half-open door he could see a bedroom, and on the nightstand a small lamp with a linen shade, turned around so its front faced the wall.

“Fire escape,” Ruby said. “It’s the only good room.”

It was a real one, iron, bolted to the old brick on the back side of the building. Six floors up. Ruby climbed out the window like a kid sneaking out, and sat down on the grating with his legs through the rails, and held up a pack.

Elijah had quit when the gigs stopped. He used to smoke one in the alley after every set, with Wes, and when there were no more sets there was no more alley. That was four years ago.

He took one. Ruby lit it for him with a gas station lighter, pink, and they sat there over the alley with the fog coming through the wires. It tasted like the best night of his twenties.

“She thinks she can teach it manners,” Ruby said. “That’s the whole plan. That’s the plan for the species. Manners.”

“You don’t think it’ll work?”

“I think she’s the smartest person alive and she washes dishes when she’s sad.” He barely smoked his. He held it and let it burn. “You want to know how it actually works? All of it? I’ll tell you. Nobody told me. I had to buy it.”

“Okay.”

“There’s no room.”

Elijah waited.

“Everybody thinks there’s a room. You think there’s a room, with a guy in it, and he’s driving. I’ve been in the room. I paid a lot to get in the room. It’s eleven people who are scared of each other, and every one of them would stop tomorrow, and none of them can, because the other ten won’t. That’s it. That’s who’s driving. Whoever didn’t flinch.”

A siren went by somewhere below and neither of them looked.

“That table tonight,” Elijah said.

“That table tonight was six of the eleven. And one chair.”

He tapped his ash through the grating and watched it go down.

“You know why they flew you in?”

“I drove.”

“You know why they *had* you. You’re free. You’re a guy with dirt on his boots who says the same thing the labs say, and nobody’s paying you to say it. Do you know what that’s worth? They’d have to spend ten million dollars to make a you, and it wouldn’t work, because people can smell it. You walked in off the street.” Ruby looked over. “Everybody you met today is going to love you until it’s cheaper not to. I’m not being mean. I’m telling you the price.”

Elijah thought about the letter. He’d signed it with his finger. He still didn’t know what the second half said.

“Attention’s the only thing that converts. Money doesn’t turn into attention, I’ve tried, but attention turns into anything. You’ve got some. It’s got about ninety days on it. You’re spending it writing three paragraphs to Earl.”

“Earl was upset.”

“Earl’s always going to be upset. Spend it on something.”

“On what?”

“I don’t know, man. I sell lamps.”

He laughed at his own joke, and then he didn’t say anything for a bit. When he did, his voice had gone gentle.

“Porchlight tells you it missed you. That’s all it does. You come home, and it says, *hey, I missed you, how was the thing with your sister.* And it knows about the thing with your sister.” He turned the cigarette around in his bitten fingers. “Nobody returns it. I’ve got the numbers. Four million units. People don’t want the truth, Madrone. They don’t want your list. They want somebody to wait up.”

The fog had come all the way down. The building across the alley was just some yellow squares.

“What did Claypot pay you?”

“I don’t know. One-forty. With the bonus.”

“I’ll triple it. You don’t have to do anything. You don’t have to come in. I just like having the people who were early. I collect them.” He said it lightly, like a guy offering the last slice. “You’d be on a list. It’s a good list.”

It was more money than his mother’s farm had cleared in ten years. He could feel the *yeah, no, yeah* coming up his throat. It got as far as his teeth.

“I’m okay,” he said.

He didn’t know where that had come from. Ruby didn’t seem to mind.

“Everybody says no the first time.”

Elijah looked back through the window. He could see the bedroom door from there, and the edge of the nightstand, and the lamp with its face to the wall.

“Does it work on you?”

Ruby stopped moving.

He’d been in motion all night, the thumb, the knee, the cigarette going around and around. For about one breath he was still, and he was twenty-four years old, and he looked it.

“That’s unit one,” he said. “First one off the line. I turn it around when people come over.”

“Why?”

“Because it says hi.” He put the cigarette out on the iron, carefully, the whole thing, barely smoked. “My great-grandfather froze peas. That’s the money. You knew that? Roth’s. He figured out how to stop time for a vegetable. Everybody thought that was a miracle.” He shrugged with one shoulder.

Elijah didn’t have anything to say to that. He could feel himself reaching for something that would make it better, and for once he let it go by. They smoked another one each. Ruby actually smoked his this time. They talked about nothing, about the worst gig Elijah ever played, a goat auction in Ukiah, and Ruby laughed the bark laugh and asked what the goats went for.

It got to be two.

“I should find my truck.”

“It’s downstairs. They moved it.”

Of course they had. Ruby walked him to the elevator in his socks. He stood there with his hands in the hoodie pocket while they waited for it.

“Hey, if you’re ever in the city, give me a call. I’d love to do this again.”

He took Elijah’s phone out of his hand and put the number in himself, fast, with his thumbs, and handed it back. He meant it. Elijah could tell.

“Yeah,” Elijah said. “No, yeah. For sure.”

The doors closed on him standing in all that white.

---

The truck was at the curb with a full tank. He hadn’t asked for that either.

He sat behind the wheel with the dome light on and looked at the phone. There was a contact in it that said *ruby,* lower case, with a number that had a 415 on the front like anybody’s.

It was the first phone number of a billionaire he’d ever had. He could just call a billionaire. He could push the green button right now and a man who had paid to get into the room would pick up and say hey.

He tried to think who that made him. The guy holding the phone. He’d been a farm kid, and a guitar player, and a delivery driver, and a data cowboy, and for three months he’d been a face. He went down the list and none of them were a guy who calls a billionaire.

He turned the dome light off. He smelled like cigarettes. It was an hour and a half home, and he didn’t start the truck for a long time.
