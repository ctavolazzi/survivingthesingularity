# Chapter 11: Two Million Dollars of Robot Arms

He wrote a text to Ruby ten times and sent none of them.

*Hey man, great meeting you.* Delete. *Thanks for the smokes.* Delete. *Hey it’s Elijah, the stove guy.* He looked at that one for a whole minute. Every version sounded like a guy who wanted something. He couldn’t figure out how to write to a billionaire without wanting something, and he couldn’t figure out what he wanted.

That was most of the spring.

He let the apartment go in April. The savings were for emergencies, and at some point he noticed he was the emergency. He and Manny took the Fisher back down the stairs, which was worse than up, and it went into his mother’s barn under the same tarp. He went into his old bedroom, under a poster of a band he’d opened for once.

He tried to be useful. He made plain little videos, sitting on a hay bale, explaining one word at a time. What a model was. What training meant. Why the power bill mattered. He didn’t say anything scary and he didn’t do the list. A lot fewer people watched. The ones who stayed were the forklift driver in Tulsa, and nurses, and a guy who fixed elevators. They asked real questions. He liked them a lot. Ruby would have said he was spending it on Earl.

The ninety days ran out about when Ruby said they would. The senator’s office stopped answering. The lab never sent the ticket.

Then some folks called about a car factory.

They were refitting an old tomato cannery outside Stockton to build small electric trucks, the kind a farmer could afford and fix with a socket set. Elijah loved that. He drove down. The guy who ran it was named Trent and wore a vest, and Trent walked him through acres of empty concrete talking about *story*. They wanted him in the ads. They wanted him standing in a field next to a truck that didn’t exist yet, saying he’d seen what was coming and this was it.

“I don’t want to do that,” Elijah said. “I’m just trying to learn and understand.”

Trent looked at him like he’d answered in Portuguese.

“I can carry stuff,” Elijah said. “I drove delivery for six years. I’m good at carrying stuff.”

So they let him carry stuff.

---

The warehouse was in Oakland, by the port, and he got there at six in the morning because the email said six.

There was a twenty-six-foot U-Haul backed up to the dock with the door rolled open. Inside, on pallets, were six robot arms. They were orange. They were folded up on themselves like big sleeping birds, each one about the size of a refrigerator, and an old guy in a canvas jacket was walking down the row yanking on ratchet straps.

“You the rider?” the old guy said.

“I guess so. Elijah.”

“Gus. Grab that end. I don’t like this one.”

They redid the strap. Gus was sixty-something, with a white beard cut short and hands that had grease in the knuckles that was never coming out. He checked every strap twice and then stood back and looked at the load the way Elijah’s mother looked at a pie.

“Two million dollars,” Gus said. “And when it goes wrong it’ll be a strap. It’s always a strap, man. It’s never the two million dollars.”

“These are two million dollars?”

“About three-thirty apiece. I got them for a lot less. A plant shut down in Fremont and they were going to scrap them. I can’t watch that. It’s like watching somebody shoot a horse.” He pulled the door down and latched it. “You want to drive or you want me to drive?”

“They’re yours?”

“Somebody’s got to own them. Get in.”

Gus drove. He drove like a man with eggs in the back, five under the limit in the right lane, and at the first gas station, in Castro Valley, he got out and opened the door and checked all six straps again. The cab had sunflower seeds in the cup holder and a thermos that said IRON DERBY on the side, with most of the letters worn off.

Elijah looked at the thermos for a while.

“Wait,” he said.

Gus grinned at the road.

“*Wait.* You’re the guy with the hat. Sunday Driver. You had the robot that didn’t have a weapon, it just pushed everybody into the wall, really slowly…”

“Nine seasons. It never once did anything interesting. Folks loved it.”

“I watched that with my dad. Every week. He thought you were hilarious. He said you were the only guy on TV who drove like him.”

“That’s the nicest thing anybody’s said to me since the divorce.” Gus was delighted. He hit the wheel with the flat of his hand. “Your dad still around?”

“No.”

“I’m sorry, man. He had taste.”

They went over the hills with the windmills, slow, trucks going around them. Elijah found out the rest in pieces, the way you do in a cab. Gus told every story against himself. He’d built a part for the show, a little box that told a motor how fast to spin, because the ones you could buy kept catching fire. It turned out everything with a propeller wanted one. That was the money. He called it an accident. He said it like a guy describing how he’d backed into a mailbox.

“How much of an accident?” Elijah said.

“A dumb amount. I’ve been trying to get rid of it for thirty years and I can’t. That’s not me being modest. I’ve given away more than I ever made, and I’ve got more now than when I started giving.”

“How?”

“Because it’s a machine.” He took a hand off the wheel and turned it over, palm up. “You put a pile of money in a box with some rules, and it makes more money, and you give that away, and the pile’s still there. It’s bigger. It doesn’t sleep and it doesn’t need me. You know that thing on your list? The one that improves itself and nobody can keep up with it?”

“Six.”

“I’ve owned one since 1994. It’s called an endowment.” He laughed, a little grimly. “Everybody down there’s losing their mind about a machine that makes itself bigger. The folks I know have had one for a hundred years. That’s the whole secret, man. That’s how it actually works. The money makes money. Nobody had to get smart.”

Elijah wrote that down. Gus glanced over and didn’t say anything.

“So what do you do with it?”

“I’ve got a museum. An old Elks lodge, up the valley, town of nine thousand. Paintings by people from around there. Nobody was going to see it, so I bought some short buses.” He was grinning again. “Little ones. One’s got a kiln in the back. One’s got a printing press, an old iron one, weighs more than the bus. We drive to a school or a fair or an old folks’ home, kid makes a cup, we fire it, kid goes home with a cup. I couldn’t give the stuff away fast enough standing still. So I put it on wheels.”

“A bus line of art buses.”

“Four of them. I’m getting a fifth. It’s the only smart thing I ever did on purpose.”

They stopped again in Tracy. Straps. Gus bought two coffees and wouldn’t take the three dollars.

Somewhere past that, on the long flat part, Elijah told him about the night in the city. He hadn’t told anyone. He told him about the kitchen and the fire escape, and when he got to the names Gus nodded like they were neighbors.

“The Roth boy. I knew his grandmother. Evelyn ran that company for forty years in a hairnet. She’d have unplugged that lamp and handed him a shovel.” He shook his head. “He’s not a bad kid. He’s a kid with nobody to tell him no.”

“And her?”

“I gave her lab some money. Back when nobody would. I didn’t understand a word she said, I still don’t. She came to the lunch and did the dishes.” He looked over. “She do your dishes?”

“She did the caterers’ dishes.”

“That’s her. She can’t stand to be thanked.”

“He offered me a job,” Elijah said. “Ruby. For doing nothing. Triple what I made.”

“You take it?”

“No.”

“Good. The ones for nothing are the expensive ones.”

They rode a while. There were almond orchards on both sides, in rows so straight they flickered.

“Hey,” Gus said. “Let’s do a show together.”

Elijah looked at him.

“I’m serious. I’ve been thinking about it since Castro Valley. You, me, a bus, a couple of these arms in the back. We drive around and show folks what the stuff actually is. No hype. You explain it, I break it. I worked on Iron Derby, I did a bunch of other shows, I know everybody who’s left. I’d like to have you on. I’ll fly you out to the shop next week. Let’s do it.”

It was a good idea. Elijah could see it. He could see the bus. It was the first thing anybody had offered him in six months that he could actually picture his mother watching.

He could feel the *yeah* coming.

“You know what,” he said, slowly. “I’m still figuring some stuff out. I’m really grateful for the opportunity, but I’m not sure if that’s the right move for me right now. Thank you very much, but I’m going to have to pass at the moment.”

He braced. He figured the air would change, and Gus would be very nice to him, and then he’d never hear from him again.

“Your choice,” Gus said. “I can’t force you to do it.”

He reached over and turned the radio up a little. It was a ranchera station. That was all. He was the same guy he’d been a mile ago. He asked if Elijah wanted the rest of the sunflower seeds.

Elijah sat there with the bag in his hand.

---

They backed up to the cannery dock a little after ten. Trent’s guys came out with a forklift. Gus got down, opened the door, and checked the straps one last time, even though the trip was over.

“See?” he said. “Not a strap. I’m almost disappointed.”

When the last arm was off the truck, he took a pen out of his jacket and wrote on the back of a gas receipt and handed it to Elijah.

“Here’s my phone number. Give me a call if you ever want to talk about anything.”

“Okay. Thanks. I mean it, thank you.”

“Sure.” He climbed back up into the cab. He had a bus to go look at. He started the engine and then leaned out the window.

“Oh, by the way. I saw that you’re working on a book. Really cool, man. Love that for you. Let me know when it comes out.”

The truck pulled off across the lot, five under.

Elijah had said that once. On the podcast, in the second hour, when he figured nobody was still listening. He stood on the dock in the heat with a gas receipt in his hand.

*Holy shit,* he thought.

That was two. Two billionaires, two phone numbers. He’d said yes to almost everybody who scared him since October. And he’d just said no to the kind one, and he didn’t know why.

“Hey, forecaster!” one of Trent’s guys yelled. “You gonna help or what?”

He put the receipt in the notebook and went to help.
