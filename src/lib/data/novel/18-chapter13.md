# Chapter 13: One O’Clock on Saturday

In the morning the fever was down enough to drive. He did not go in to get sorted out.

He left the lot with the windows down and the heater on, because he couldn’t get warm. He had a quarter tank and no money. Home was two hours north. He got as far as a side street by a park downtown, pulled in under a sycamore, and turned the truck off.

He was there a week.

He could not have told you why. He didn’t have the gas to get home, and he didn’t have it in him to ask anybody for it. He had asked four people for a hand already. He’d been sleeping in the truck for three weeks, but that had been a job. Now he was just a man living in a truck.

He smoked what was left of the bag. He lay across the seat with his feet out the window and smoked until he quit thinking about David, and when it wore off he rolled another one. He slept in the daytime. He ran out of food on Monday. That didn’t matter much, because he wasn’t hungry. On Wednesday he was hungry, and it mattered.

On Sunday at 9:40 the phone rang on the dash. He watched it until it stopped. Then he typed *Working late. All good. Love you,* and sent it, and turned the phone off.

Those were dark days.

On Friday afternoon the cab was too hot to sit in. He was on the curb beside the truck with his elbows on his knees when an old man came up the sidewalk, talking to himself.

He had a white beard and a long coat, in August, and a duffel bag over one shoulder. He looked like a wizard who had been out in the weather a long time. He shuffled. He was in the middle of an argument with somebody who wasn’t there.

*Oh, come on, man,* Elijah thought. *No. Not right now.*

The old man sat down on the curb next to him. It took him a while. Elijah didn’t move. He didn’t care. He had lost about everything he had. He had nothing left to lose but his life, and he didn’t care all that much about that, either.

“Hey. What’s your name?”

“Elijah.”

“Elijah.” The old man seemed to enjoy it. “How biblical.”

He went into his coat pocket, and went through it, and came out with a little rubber squeaky toy, the kind you buy for a dog. He held it up between them and squeaked it once.

“What do you think about this?”

Elijah looked at it. “That’s nice,” he said. “I like it.”

The old man nodded and put it away. Then he looked at Elijah for a while.

“You look pretty thin, boy. Have you eaten today?”

“No.”

The old man pulled the duffel around into his lap and worked through it with both hands. He came out with an apple. It was red and it didn’t have a mark on it. Elijah had heard a few stories about taking an apple from a stranger.

“No thanks. I’m good, man. It’s all right.”

“All right. Fine. Suit yourself.” The apple went back in the bag. “Where are you from?”

“California.”

“Well, we’re all from California. What part?”

“The northern part.”

“All right. Fine. Keep your secrets.” He scratched under his beard. “Me? I’m from Alabama.”

“Alabama? What the hell are you doing all the way out here?”

“Long story. I used to have a wife. She died.”

“I’m so sorry to hear that.” And then, because he had run out of manners sometime that week: “How’d she die?”

The old man was going through his coat again. He went through it for a long time and didn’t answer, and Elijah had time to wish he hadn’t asked.

“Cancer.”

He had found what he was looking for. It was a photograph, soft at the corners. He handed it over. She was beautiful. She was laughing at whoever was holding the camera. Elijah held it by the edges, and the two of them sat on the curb and looked at her.

The old man told it short. After she died he couldn’t stay in the house. Then he couldn’t stay in the town. He had a cousin in Bakersfield who had work, and then the cousin didn’t have work, and that was a long time ago now. He took the picture back and buttoned it into the pocket.

“So how come you haven’t eaten today?”

“I don’t have any money.”

“Well, you got food stamps?”

“No. I don’t really understand how any of that stuff works.”

The old man set the duffel on his knee and held it open so Elijah could see in. There were cans in it, and bread, and a jar of peanut butter.

“Look. All this food came from the food bank.”

“What do you mean?”

“The food bank. You know where the food bank is, don’t you?”

“No. I don’t know what that is.”

The old man stared at him.

“How the fuck did you get all the way out here on the street, and you don’t know what a food bank is? How privileged are you?”

Then he laughed, and slapped Elijah on the shoulder.

“I’m joking. Lighten up a little, kid.”

Elijah didn’t like being called kid. He got his feet under him to go.

“Look,” the old man said. “Food bank opens up at one o’clock on Saturday. One o’clock. They just give you food. You don’t need to apply. You don’t need to nothing. They just hand it out. You got a way to get there tomorrow?”

Elijah thought about the gas gauge.

“My feet.”

“All right. You got bags and stuff?”

Elijah looked at him then.

“What do you want? Why are you helping me?”

“Somebody did this for me.”

“Well, nobody cares about me. Everybody hates me.”

“Ah,” the old man said. It was more of a sound than a word. “Sounds like really you just hate yourself. You can’t even get yourself to the food bank to get some food in you.”

Elijah didn’t say anything. He sat back down. A bus went by.

“Are you a coffee drinker?” he said finally. It was all he could think of.

“No.”

“Why not? You don’t like the taste?”

“I hate it. Makes my heart bump.”

The old man looked him up and down.

“Look, kid. This world’s a hard place. It’s tough out here. And I don’t mean to be rude, but you don’t exactly look like you’re cut out for the streets.”

Elijah laughed. It surprised him. “No. I’m not really from here.”

“I can tell.”

And then it came out of him. It wasn’t the list. He was done with the list.

“All I did was try to teach people about this thing. I tried to tell them what was coming and open their eyes to it. And they all just fucking turned their backs on me. They treated me like shit.”

“Look. People don’t know what the fuck they want. People want everything to be smooth. Smooth ride, smooth sailing. You rock the boat just a little bit, you’re the bad guy. It’s all sunshine and rainbows, and they look at you like you’re about to bring the storm down on them.”

Elijah nodded. “Yeah. That’s kind of what I went through.”

“All right, look. You gotta take care of you first, before you take care of other people. Otherwise they will take and take and take and take from you until you have nothing left to give. You can say yes a thousand times, and you are the good guy. You are the savior. But you say no once? Now you’re the bad guy.” He tapped Elijah’s knee with one finger. “You gotta fill your own cup.”

“Yeah, but there are people out there with three hundred cups. And they’re all full. Why don’t they want to help?”

“They do. Some of them, anyway. They just want to see you help yourself first. They don’t want to give stuff away to people that are going to waste it.”

“That doesn’t make any sense. They have a hundred fucking cups. They have so much more than they need. Why won’t they help?”

The old man didn’t answer that. He started rummaging in the duffel again, and this time he came out with something in a wrapper. It was a cherry pie, the gas station kind. Elijah had eaten about a thousand of them between deliveries.

“Take it.”

Elijah was hungry. He took it, and he ate it right there, in about four bites.

He wanted to give something back. He had one thing.

“I got a spliff here.”

“You got a spliff?”

“You want to split it with me?”

“I don’t need that. No thanks.”

“Okay. Then what do you want? There’s got to be something I can do for you.”

“Sometimes people just want to help,” the old man said. “Don’t you?”

So Elijah told him some of it. Not a lot. A little. He told him about a video, and a friend who had asked him if he’d seen a professional, and a parking lot. He was just getting going when the old man made the sound again.

“Ehh. Look, forget all that. Don’t focus on the people that say no. Focus on the people that say yes. You gotta focus on the people that want to help, not the people that don’t.”

“Yeah, but I don’t understand why they don’t want to help.”

“Does it matter? The important thing is to find the people who do. Like the food bank ladies. The nice food bank ladies over there, they help me out every other week. They keep me alive.” He nodded to himself. “There are people who want to help, young man.”

“I wanted to help,” Elijah said. It came out bitter.

“See? What’d I tell you? There’s one.”

Elijah didn’t have an answer for that.

“A lot of these people out here, they think they’re helping, and what they actually want is to destroy. You seem like a good kid. You got the energy of Jesus about you.”

Elijah rolled his eyes. “Oh, God.”

“Ah, fine. Do what you want. But remember this.” The old man got a hand on the bumper of the truck and started getting up. It took him as long as sitting down had. “If you spend all your time focused on what you can’t do, you’ll miss what you can. Don’t be like me.”

He stood there a second getting his balance. Then he went into the coat one more time and came out with a twenty-dollar bill, folded up small, and held it down to him.

“No. I can’t take that.”

“I been watching that truck sit there all week. It don’t run on nothing.”

“I can’t take your money, man.”

“Sure you can. Somebody did it for me.”

Elijah took it.

He said thank you. The old man waved it off and went on up the sidewalk with the duffel, and before he got to the corner he was arguing with somebody again. Elijah watched him go. He had never asked his name.

---

The food bank was in the parking lot behind a church, and there was a line by 12:30.

Nobody asked him for anything. There was no form. Two women at a folding table were handing out bags, and when he got to the front one of them looked at him the way his mother would have, and handed him two.

“You come back in two weeks, honey,” she said. “We’re here every other Saturday.”

He looked for the old man in the line. He didn’t see him.

He ate a peanut butter sandwich sitting in the truck with the door open, and then he ate another one. Then he drove to the gas station on the corner, put all twenty dollars in the tank, and went north.
