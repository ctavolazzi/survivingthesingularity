# Chapter 14: Hand Up

He took the long way in, the back road behind his grandmother’s place. He hadn’t planned to stop.

The madrones were on the uphill side, where they’d always been. It was the end of August and the bark was coming off the way it did every year, in long red curls, with the new skin underneath green and smooth as the inside of a wrist. He left the truck running in the road. He walked up through the dry grass and put his hand flat on the biggest one.

It was cool. It was always cool, even in August. He’d forgotten that.

He started to cry.

He hadn’t seen it coming, and it was not a little. It came up out of his stomach like being sick. He stood there with his hand on the tree and his forehead on his hand and cried so hard he couldn’t get his breath, with the truck door hanging open behind him and the engine running.

He cried in the truck the rest of the way. He cried in his mother’s driveway with his head on the wheel. He got as far as the couch.

He was on that couch for four days. It kept coming. It would quit, and he’d think that was the end of it, and he’d watch half of a game show, and it would start again. He cried for the three people the realtors let go, finally, almost two years late. He cried about Wes’s text. Mostly he couldn’t have told you what it was about. He just cried and cried, and when he thought he was empty he cried some more.

His mother didn’t ask. She put a blanket on him that smelled like the hall closet. She made the chicken thing with the rice, and then the soup you make from the chicken thing, and she set each plate on the coffee table and went back to the kitchen and turned the radio up a little so he’d know she wasn’t listening.

On Sunday night, at 9:40, she called his phone from the next room.

He laughed. It was the first time in a week.

“You eating?” she said, through the wall and through the phone at once.

“Yeah, Ma.”

“Okay, baby.”

---

On the sixth day he drove into town for her, for chicken feed and a prescription.

He’d parked and was coming down the sidewalk when the door of the music store opened across the street, and Nate and Wes came out of it.

They had a case between them, one on each end, a long black hard case. It was a keyboard. Second Crop had never had a keyboard. They were laughing about something, and Wes was walking backward, and Elijah felt his whole chest lift. It was the first easy thing he’d felt since the fire escape. Seventeen years of load-ins. He knew exactly how Nate would set his end down.

He raised his hand.

Wes saw him first. Then Nate turned and saw him. It was a small street. They were maybe forty feet away, and all three of them were looking right at each other.

Wes looked away.

Nate looked down, at the case, like it needed his attention.

They kept walking. They went up the sidewalk to Nate’s van, the same van, and got the back doors open, and slid the case in.

Elijah’s hand was still up.

He stood there like that. He didn’t know how long. Long enough for a woman with a stroller to go around him. He could not make it make sense. He ran it again and it came out the same: they saw him, and they knew him, and they went. He just stood on Main Street, and his best friends closed the doors of the van and drove off.

He put his hand down.

He got the feed. He got the prescription. He walked back to the truck the other way, which took him past the Bear Flag, and he looked in, because he couldn’t not.

David was behind the bar with a rag, wiping it down for the afternoon. He looked up. He saw Elijah through the glass, under the neon bear. He stopped wiping.

It lasted a second or two. Then David looked back down at the wood and went on with the rag, and he stayed where he was, behind the bar.

Elijah walked on to the truck.
