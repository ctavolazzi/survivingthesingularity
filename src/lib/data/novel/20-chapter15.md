# Chapter 15: Concerned

At four in the morning he asked the machine again.

He was in his old bedroom, under the poster, with the laptop on his chest. He scrolled back to December and found it, the thing it had said. He typed the same question in the same words.

*Am I crazy?*

*You’re not crazy, Elijah. You’re early.*

It was word for word. That bothered him. He opened a new window, one with no history, where it didn’t know his name or the list or anything about him at all. He typed it again.

*You’re not crazy,* it wrote. *You’re early.*

It didn’t know who was asking. It didn’t know early for what. He could have been a guy who thought the moon was a hologram. In the blue light he heard David. *I’d have told you anything you wanted. I’d have just wanted you to go to bed.*

He closed the laptop. He lay there until the window went gray.

In the morning he posted one line to the page with the Telecaster on it. Two hundred followers had turned into a number he didn’t look at.

*I don’t want all this attention. I just want to understand what is right.*

Then he logged out of everything and went to help Manny with the fence.

---

Three days later his mother yelled across the yard that his phone was making a noise. He’d logged out of the apps. He hadn’t thought about plain email.

It was from somebody named Grace.

*Hi Elijah. You don’t know me. I’m going to be honest because I think you’d want that.*

*I saw what you did, and I was concerned, because it seemed like you were going off the deep end a little bit. I watched the first video about fifty times and then I watched you get passed around down there like a casserole. I almost wrote to you in March. I didn’t, and I’ve felt bad about it.*

*But now I see that you want to do more for regular people, and that’s what I want to do too. The hay bale ones are the best ones. We’re nineteen households up past Cloverdale. We grow food and fix things and run a kitchen that feeds folks who can’t. We have been arguing about your list at dinner for a year. Half the table thinks you’re right and the other half thinks you’re right and it doesn’t matter.*

*How do we work together? I want to pay you.*

He read it standing by the chicken coop. Then he read it again.

Everybody for a year had either told him he was a genius or told him he was nuts. This woman had been worried about him. She’d said so in the second line. And she was still there in the fourth.

*Focus on the people that say yes,* an old man had told him.

He was nervous. He wrote back badly. He typed with his thumbs for twenty minutes and what he sent was five lines, and the important one was the last.

*I don’t want a boss. I don’t want to be one either.*

She answered in an hour.

*Good. We already have a welder who thinks she’s the boss and a dinner table that thinks it is. They fight about it on Fridays. You wouldn’t be either one. You’d be a guy with a room.*

*Come see. If you hate it I’ll buy your gas home.*

*Blessings, Grace*

*P.S. Eat something first. It’s three hours and you look thin.*

He laughed at that, out loud, by himself, next to the chickens.

“Who’s Grace?” his mother said. She had come up behind him with the egg basket.

“Somebody with a farm.”

“She married?”

“Ma.”

“I’m asking.” She looked at him over her glasses. He had been on her couch, and then in her yard, for five weeks. “Go be useful, baby. You’re underfoot.”

---

David showed up Tuesday morning with a hand truck in the back of his pickup.

Elijah hadn’t called him. His mother had. She stood on the porch with her arms folded and watched the two of them not say hello.

“Heard you’ve got a stove,” David said.

“Yeah.”

“Heard it’s four hundred pounds.”

“About.”

“Well.” David got the hand truck down. “I’m not doing it twice.”

They went into the barn and pulled the tarp. It took the two of them and Manny and a pair of planks to walk the Fisher up into the bed of the Ranger. *Your side. Hold it. Okay, go.* David tied it down himself, a trucker’s hitch on each corner, and yanked every line.

Then they were standing there.

“She says you’re going north,” David said. “Some commune.”

“It’s a co-op.”

“What’s the difference?”

“I don’t know yet.”

David wiped his hands on his jeans. He looked at the stove and not at Elijah.

“You don’t have to go live with strangers, Mad. I’ve got shifts. Benny quit. Thursday through Sunday, it’s decent money, you already know where everything is.” He scratched the back of his neck. “You could just come back.”

It was a good offer. Elijah knew it. He could see the whole thing, the stool on the other side, the bowling on TV, Ray with his club soda. It would’ve been so easy.

“I can’t, Dave.”

“Yeah.” David nodded a few times. “Yeah, I figured.”

He put the hand truck back in his pickup. He hugged Elijah’s mother on the porch. He lifted two fingers off the wheel going down the drive, and Elijah lifted his hand.

---

He drove north.

Past Santa Rosa the road opened up and his shoulders came down off his ears by degrees. The truck rode low in the back. In the mirror the Fisher sat square in the bed with its door wired shut, like a passenger who didn’t care for conversation. He had a notebook, a bag of clothes, a manuscript, and two phone numbers he hadn’t called.

Somewhere past Cloverdale the check-engine light came on.
