# Chapter 16: Swarf

The address Grace sent turned out to be a shed.

It was an old apple-packing shed off the county road, with a roll-up door open to the heat. Elijah parked on the gravel with the engine light still on and the Fisher riding low in the back. He’d expected a farmhouse. A porch, maybe, and a woman on it. Inside the shed there were no apples. There was a plasma table in the middle of a cut, throwing sparks in a fan. There were welding curtains, and steel racked by size along one wall. The hand tools were shadow-boarded so carefully they looked like an evidence display.

A woman in a leather apron stood at the plasma table with her back to him, watching the cut. She was fifty-something, with thick forearms.

“Looking for the mechanic?” she said, without turning around. “Two miles up, left at the grange hall. This isn’t that.”

“Grace asked me to come.”

“Grace asks a lot of people a lot of things.” She still hadn’t turned. “She’s on the route. It’s Tuesday.”

“This is the co-op?”

She turned around then and looked at him, one long level pass, boots to haircut.

“Co-op. Nineteen households. We make what we can’t afford to buy and fix what the market says to throw away.” She nodded at the plasma table. “Right now, brackets for a neighbor’s solar rack. You need something made?”

And Elijah went off.

He hadn’t meant to. He’d meant to say hello. But he had spent a year being told he’d calibrate, and then most of a year being agreed with by people who weren’t listening. And here was a stranger who made things and fixed things for her neighbors. He told her what was coming, fast: the models, the list, the end of the deal. And then he started pitching.

He heard himself say the shop could scale. He said platform. He said there ought to be an app, a network of co-ops, a marketplace layer. He could build it in a month. This thing could be in fifty towns by spring. They could get ahead of the wave. He said *first-mover,* and heard it come out of his mouth in Ruby’s voice, quick and light. Somewhere in there he ran out of gas. The woman, whose name was Marta Okonkwo, had crossed her arms and was watching him the way she’d watched the plasma cut. She was waiting to see if the machine would finish or fault.

“You done?” she said.

He was done.

“Whose app?”

He opened his mouth.

She held up a gloved finger. “Okay. One. Nobody here needs an app. Maria next door needs her rack to survive a snow load. That’s the bracket. Two. Scale.” She said the word like a fish she was throwing back. “Everything that ever screwed the people I know got big first. The plant I gave fifteen years to scaled. It scaled itself right to a labor market that didn’t include us. You know what doesn’t scale? Nineteen households who all know whose kid is whose. That’s not a limitation, college. That’s the load-bearing wall.”

“I’m not,” he started, and she talked straight through him.

“Three. You drove up here to tell me a machine is coming for everybody’s job.” She pulled her glove off, finger by finger. “In 2009 I watched a company do to nine hundred of us what you think your machine is going to do to everybody. There was no machine. There was a spreadsheet and a tax break. So I know something you don’t, which is what it’s like after. And you know something I don’t, which is apparently the schedule.” She jerked her chin toward the sparks. “That could be worth something. Not an app.”

In the quiet the plasma table ticked as it cooled. Elijah stood there and felt the pitch die.

“I grew up on a farm,” he said. “I can’t weld. But I can fix a pump, and I can read a manual, and I’m not in a hurry.”

Marta looked at him for another second. Then she picked up a push broom that was leaning by the curtain and held it out.

“Swarf goes in the red bin,” she said. “We’ll see.”

---

He swept until the sun was low.

Swarf was the metal shavings. He worked that out by himself, which he was a little proud of. It got into everything. He did the floor, and then under the benches, and then the gravel apron outside the door, because nobody told him to stop. Marta went back to her brackets and didn’t look at him once. He would have bet money she knew exactly where he was the whole time.

Out by the road there was a gatepost with a strip of orange flagging tape tied around it, the plastic kind surveyors use. It had been there a while. The sun had faded it almost to pink.

A flatbed came up the drive at dusk, pulling a little enclosed trailer with KITCHEN painted on the side by hand. It stopped crooked. A woman got out and went around back and started fighting the hitch.

“Come on, you son of a bitch. Come *on.* I greased you. I greased you Sunday.”

She was in her thirties, in rubber boots and a canvas coat with an apron under it, with a dark braid coming apart down her back. There was orange tape on the hitch pin. There was orange tape on the handle of the cooler in the truck bed. She kicked the trailer tongue, hard, and it dropped, and she said “Thank you,” to it, sincerely, and turned around and saw him standing there with the broom.

Her whole voice changed. It went soft and warm, like a hand on a kid’s forehead.

“Oh,” she said. “Oh, shit. You’re him. You came.”

“Hi. Yeah. Elijah.”

“I know. Grace.” She crossed the gravel and took his forearm in her hand, just held it, the way some people shake. It went through him like a shot of something. “You’re taller. You look so tired. Did you eat? I told you to eat.”

“I had a thing in Ukiah.”

“A thing.” She looked at the broom, and laughed, a big surprised laugh with her head back. “She gave you the broom. Okay. That’s good. That’s really good. That means she didn’t say no.”

“She said we’ll see.”

“That’s her yes. You’ll learn. It’s the only one she’s got.”

He had to say it. He’d practiced it for three hours on the 101.

“About the pay.”

Something crossed her face, quick.

“It has to go to the table. Everything goes to the table. It will, they’ll do it, I just have to bring it right.” Her hand squeezed his arm. “I’m good for it. I promise. I wouldn’t have asked you up here.”

“You ordered a college,” Marta said. She had come to the roll-up door and was standing in it, wiping her hands with a rag.

“I invited a person.”

“Did the table invite a person?”

“The table’s going to love him.” Grace didn’t turn around. She was still looking at Elijah, smiling, with a little worry in it now. “Ignore her. She’s like that till Christmas.”

He looked past her at the gatepost.

“What’s the tape for?”

“Hm? Oh.” She glanced at it and away. “Old habit. Come on. Eat. Then I’ll show you where you sleep.”
