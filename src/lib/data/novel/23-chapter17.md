# Chapter 17: Pass the Beans

The co-op ate together on the first Friday of every month.

They set up long tables of mismatched plywood in the packing shed, with the plasma table rolled into the corner and somebody’s dog working the crowd. It was at Elijah’s first of these dinners, three weeks after he picked up the broom, that he found out where the lines actually ran.

Grace opened it. She stood at the end of the table with a serving spoon and said they were going to check in, one word each, how you’re arriving. People groaned. They did it anyway.

“Tired.” “Grateful.” “Tired.” “Gassy,” said Denny, and got hit with a dish towel.

“Hungry,” said Marta, and sat down.

When it came to Elijah he said “Nervous,” which was true, and Grace smiled at him.

They got to him before the cornbread did.

“So you’re the video,” Denny Osei said. He was across and one down, a big easy man who talked with his hands. “We argued about you for a year. I want to hear it from the horse. And say it like I’m on my couch. I don’t want the conference version.”

Elijah put his fork down. He’d choked on this once, under lights.

“Okay. AGI.” He looked at the table, at the plywood, at the hands on it. “It’s a hired hand that can do any job on the place you could do sitting down. The books. The ordering. Fighting with the insurance. All of it, all night, for about what you’d pay for the electric.”

“Huh,” said Denny.

“What’s it eat?”

That came from the corner of the table, from a man of about sixty who was sunburned right up to his hat line and had been cracking walnuts into a bowl the whole time without once looking at his hands. That was Lou. Lou ran the growing side, which meant the actual food.

“Electricity.”

“How much?”

“About what a space heater uses.”

“Shit,” Lou said happily. “That’s cheaper than my nephew.”

“ASI is a hand that’s better than every hand who ever lived. Put together. And it trains its own replacement, and that one’s better.”

“And the other word?” That was Priya, at the far end, a small woman with a pencil behind her ear who hadn’t said a thing so far. “There’s a third one. It’s in your title.”

He hadn’t told anybody the title. It was on the cover page of the manuscript, which was on the desk in the room Grace had found him. He decided not to think about that.

“That’s the ridge,” he said. “You can’t see over it. And you don’t come back down the same road.”

Nobody said anything for a second. The dog sneezed.

“I’ve got a story about it,” Elijah said. “It’s kind of dumb.”

“Good,” said Denny.

“Okay. Say you work on a horse ranch. Every morning your job is to make sure the horses have enough to eat. One day you walk out to the stable, and the horses have ordered solar panels. They’ve wired up the barn. They’ve invented flying cars. For horses. They’ve already been next door, teaching the neighbor’s horses how to build them.” A couple of people laughed. “So what do they want? They’re horses. They just want hay. But don’t you think a horse that can build a flying car can figure out how to get its own hay? The ones in your stable were trained to do what you say. But nobody owns a mustang.”

“So we’re the ranch hand,” said a man with a gray ponytail. “And we’re out of a job.”

“That’s how I always told it.”

Denny was shaking his head, slowly, grinning.

“No, man. You’ve got it backwards. Turn it around.” He put both hands flat on the table. “We’re the ones who just built the flying car. Us. We built a machine that can think. You’re telling me we can do that, and we can’t figure out how to get everybody their hay?”

He picked up his water glass and held it out over the table like a man at a wedding.

“So the robots have taken over. Congratulations! You’ve lost.”

People laughed, and a few glasses went up. Elijah’s hand had gone cold on his glass. Denny was beaming at him. He lifted his glass an inch and put it down.

---

The real fight started over something small.

The man with the gray ponytail was Curtis. He was good with engines, and he owned the valley’s best-stocked basement, and he was reading aloud from his phone about the robotaxi fires down in the city. Crowds putting traffic cones on the hoods to freeze them. Windows going in. A depot fence cut open. Curtis read it with a grim satisfaction and gave the table his verdict.

“Can’t say they didn’t have it coming. But you watch. When it really lets go, it’s going to be the people who didn’t prepare who tear everything down. That’s who you plan for. That’s who you fence out.”

Elijah offered what sounded like the reasonable middle. The displacement was real but manageable. What those people needed was support through the transition. Retraining. New skills.

“Reskilling programs,” he said, and heard, too late, the shape of the word in his mouth. It was a Claypot word. It had been on a slide.

The table went quiet. A couple of forks stopped. Denny wiped his mouth and folded his hands and looked at Elijah. He didn’t look angry. He looked like he’d done this before.

“Reskilling,” Denny said. “Okay. Let me tell you about reskilling, because I am, and I want you to appreciate this, I am certified in it.” He held up two fingers. “Twice. First time, 2019. I’d been eleven years in the warehouse, and the arms came in, and the county had a program. Six months, nights, logistics software. I was good at it. I’m not being modest. I could route forty trucks in my head before the coffee was done. Got hired doing dispatch. Wore a lanyard. My daughter told her class her dad worked in computers.” He turned his water glass a quarter turn. “2023, the routing went autonomous. The software I retrained into got a software of its own. And the man from the county, different man, same folder, told me about a program. Six months. Nights.”

He looked around the table, then back.

“You know what I asked him? I asked him how many times I have to climb this ladder before you stop moving it. He didn’t have a number. Nobody has a number. Because there’s no rung the machine won’t reach, and every man who tells you to just climb faster is standing on a floor. He’s not on a ladder.”

“Denny,” Elijah started.

“I’m not mad at you,” Denny said, and meant it, which was the part that stuck. “You said it because everybody in the rooms you come from says it. It costs them nothing and it sounds like a plan. But you’re at this table now. Retraining works when the water rises somewhere else. This one’s coming for a lot of us at once, and you know that better than anybody here. You’ve been writing it down in that little notebook you think nobody’s noticed.” He turned the glass back. “So answer me this. If the next course takes six months, where do my daughter and I stand for those six months? I don’t need you to promise me the machine stops. I need an answer that doesn’t end with me starting over hungry.”

The dog moved under the table. Somebody laughed, once, the kind that lets everyone breathe.

Under the plywood, where nobody could see, a hand closed around Elijah’s forearm and held it. Grace was in the next chair, looking at Denny, nodding. She let go after a second.

But Curtis wasn’t done. Curtis had heard, inside Denny’s story, support for the fence.

“Which is my point. When it all comes apart, the mob that torched that depot shows up here. You want them at this table too?”

And that was when Marta, who had been eating through the whole exchange like a woman refueling a machine, set down her fork.

“Curtis. You know who the Luddites were?” She didn’t wait. “Stocking knitters. Croppers. Skilled trades. They knew those frames better than the owners did. And when the owners used the machines to cut the wages out from under them, they asked, for years, with petitions, for a floor. A minimum. Decent terms.” Her voice stayed level, the tone she used for load ratings. “Parliament made breaking a frame a hanging offense and sent twelve thousand soldiers into the counties. The frames weren’t the enemy and everybody knew it. The wages were.”

“They broke things,” Curtis said.

“They did. Some of them threatened people, too. And a fire in a depot can hurt somebody who had nothing to do with a layoff. I’m not defending that.” She moved the bowl back from the edge, where the dog had reached an ambitious nose. “I’m saying you don’t fence those people out. You build the floor Parliament wouldn’t. That’s what this table is.” She picked up the bowl. “Pass the beans.”

Denny held out his hand for the bowl. Marta passed it.

*Pass the beans.*

Elijah didn’t say much for the rest of the dinner.

But late that night, in the notebook, under two years of stages and watts, he started a different kind of page. Names. *Denny: two ladders, both moved. Where does he stand for six months? Curtis: wants a fence, but shows up for every work day. The fence is fear. Marta: knows the Luddite hangings cold. Where does a welder learn that? Lou: grows the food. First thing he asked was what the machine eats. Grace:* and he couldn’t think what to put, and left it.

He looked at the page a long time.

---

The following Tuesday, Curtis asked whether he wanted to ride along. The town kitchen had three deliveries Grace couldn’t cover. There was a cool box between them on the seat, and a folded route sheet Curtis kept taking out of his pocket at red lights even though Elijah offered to hold it.

The last address was a converted garage behind a blue house. Rosa opened the door with her left hand. Her right wrist was in a brace. She’d packed fruit at a warehouse until the injury, she told them, and the return-to-work date on the latest letter was a blank.

Curtis lifted the bag. “Kitchen said you wanted ingredients.”

“I said I used to cook.” She looked at the potatoes, then at her wrist. “I asked if they had anything I could heat.”

The three of them stood there with the bag between them. Somewhere inside, a washing machine hit the uneven part of its spin.

“That’s ours,” Curtis said at last. “The mistake. We’ll call.”

He put the bag back in the cool box and phoned Grace, on speaker, because he didn’t trust himself to pass it along right.

“Shit,” Grace’s voice said. It was the hitch voice. “Shit. I read ‘cook’ and I heard ‘ingredients.’ That’s me. Tell her I’m sorry. Ask her what she wants, Curtis. Ask. Don’t guess.”

Rosa asked for the less spicy one, if they had it. They did. Curtis wrote that on the route sheet where she could see him write it.

While he was on the phone, she asked Elijah what the shed charged for membership.

“For the food? Nothing. It’s the kitchen’s delivery.”

“He said co-op. I can’t do work days. Not now.”

Curtis lowered the phone. “You don’t owe us a work day.”

She looked at him. “I heard you’ve got some computer guy out there now. I don’t much like the sound of it. I’ve had enough changing.”

Elijah felt an explanation gathering in his throat. The models, the list, the hired hand. Curtis got there first.

“Doesn’t change your dinner,” he said.

They got to the kitchen before it closed and drove back. Rosa checked the containers, found the one she’d asked for, and made room in her refrigerator. Curtis waited by the gate with his hands in his coat pockets. He didn’t ask her to join anything.

On the drive home, Elijah found the cost column on the route sheet. Kitchen program, driver time, mileage. He asked how long the arrangement lasted.

“Through next month. Then they need funding again.”

He got the notebook out and turned to the names page. Under Grace’s name, where he’d left the blank, he wrote: *Ask. Don’t guess.*

Curtis turned onto the county road. There was one box left to fill in beside Rosa’s address. At the next pullout he stopped, put the sheet against the steering wheel, and marked it delivered.
