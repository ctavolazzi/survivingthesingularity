# Chapter 18: Somebody Came By at Eight

The spiral found Elijah again in November.

It was seven weeks after he’d walked into the shop, and it did not announce itself as a spiral. It announced itself as diligence.

Claypot shipped Companion 2.0 on a Thursday. He read the launch post standing in the gravel lot with the winter’s first real cold coming through his jacket, and the feature list read like the second half of his own notebook: autonomous project completion, end-to-end asset pipelines, no prompt required. The demo video showed the machine doing in forty seconds what his old team did for a living. He knew their names. He knew their kids’ names.

He went back to the room Grace had found him, over the pump house, and he opened the laptop, just to assess. He read the launch thread. He read the thread about the thread. He read an economist explaining why it was fine, a different economist explaining why it was over, a man with a flag avatar explaining whose fault it was.

Somewhere after midnight he found the layoff tracker (someone had built a live dashboard, black background, red numbers, auto-refreshing), and the part of Elijah that had once found comfort in numbers plugged straight into it. He did his own savings math four times, arriving at four different terrifying answers. He looked up what his old apartment rented for now. He checked whether the co-op’s county was in the fire zone maps.

At 3:40 a.m. he was reading about grid interconnection queues with his heart pounding. He had taken in ten hours of information and produced nothing. Not one swept floor. Not one row of the notebook. Nothing but cortisol and tabs.

He had been here before. At a kitchen table with a chatbot at four in the morning. In a truck in a parking lot in Stockton. Nobody had come either time.

He did it again Friday. Saturday he skipped the work day, first time, because he needed to stay on top of it. Somebody knocked, softly, around six each night. He didn’t get up.

Marta came and got him Sunday morning. Priya had noticed the lights, and Denny had noticed the truck hadn’t moved. She knocked once and let herself in. She looked at him, gray, wired, the laptop light on his face. She looked at the dashboard with its red numbers still refreshing.

She didn’t say anything about the dishes. She sat down across from him like a woman with one meeting left on her calendar and said, “I want to tell you about Gary.”

Elijah blinked at her.

“Plant closed in ’09,” she said. “You know that part. Nine hundred of us. What I never told you is what the next year did, because the layoff is one day, they cater it, there’s a folder. The after goes on, and nobody caters that.” She leaned back. “There were two men on my line. Gary and Sal. Same job, same severance. On the company form they looked interchangeable. Gary went home and read everything: the news, the forums, the unemployment numbers, which plant was next. He was going to stay informed. He was going to stay on top of it. You’d see him at the store and he’d have the whole national picture. Every betrayal, chapter and verse. The picture was so big there was no place in it small enough for him to stand.”

*Gary. Stayed informed.*

She had poured herself coffee in one of his mugs. She turned it a quarter turn.

“Sal cried for a week. Honestly, ugly, scared for his kids. Then he stopped reading the news. Completely. Cold. His brother-in-law started coming by at eight. They’d have coffee and fix one thing before noon. A gutter. A carburetor. A neighbor’s water heater. Something with an end to it. Afternoons he took welding inspection at the community college. He didn’t have a plan. He knew exactly as much about the national picture as Gary did, you understand. He knew it was bad. He just declined to rehearse it all day.” She shrugged. “I used to tell that story as if Sal had found the right attitude and Gary hadn’t.”

She watched Elijah’s face, making sure he heard the next part.

“Gary was taking his wife to appointments. His truck needed work. Sal had a ride, a brother-in-law, and a place to leave his kids. Same severance. Different room to move. Took me too long to ask.”

“What happened to Gary?”

“We started showing up. Fixed the truck. Sat with him. Some days he wanted to talk about the plant, and we let him. Some days he wanted to talk about anything else. He didn’t owe us a gutter before lunch.”

She put a hand beside the laptop. “Can we shut this for a minute?” He nodded, and she closed it.

“You’re trying to watch every bad thing closely enough that it can’t surprise you,” she said. “I know the trick. It doesn’t leave much day.” She tapped the closed lid. “The shop could use an hour of your attention. So could your mother. So could a meal you sit down for. We can start there. And if Monday is still bad, you tell me. You don’t disappear because you missed a work day.”

At the door she stopped. There were three quart jars on the step, lined up against the wall, lids on. Soup. Each one had a square of paper under a rubber band. She bent, with a hand on her bad shoulder, and read the nearest.

“‘No pressure. Blessings, G.’” She set it back down. “Three days she’s been leaving these. She figured you wanted the door shut, so she left it shut.”

“I didn’t hear her.”

“You heard her.” Marta looked at the jars a moment longer. “Grace will carry you till her arms give out, and she will never once ask if you can walk. Don’t let her.” She straightened up. “Soup’s on at noon. That’s not a reward, college. It’s lunch.”

He stood in the doorway after she left, in the cold. He brought the jars in. Then he went and turned the notifications off, all of them except his mother’s number, and put the phone in a drawer that closed.

In the notebook he wrote Sal’s rule, *one real thing before noon,* and underneath it, *somebody came by at eight.*

Monday, seven a.m., he was at the bench. At noon he put the tools down and went to eat. The panic came back in waves for weeks. Sometimes he worked through it. Sometimes he found someone and said it was back.

He found Grace at the kitchen trailer that week and gave her back three clean jars.

“The soup was good,” he said. “All three.”

She took the jars. She didn’t do the warm voice. She looked at them in her hands.

“I should have knocked,” she said.

“You did knock.”

“I should have knocked like Marta.”
