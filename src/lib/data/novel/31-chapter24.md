# Chapter 24: First Fire

The containers came up the hill on a tilt-bed in the last week of August, one at a time.

There were two of them, forty-footers, still wearing their ocean stickers, and the driver set them down on Marta’s piers with a gentleness that seemed impossible for eighteen thousand pounds of steel.

The piers had been poured three weeks earlier, and Elijah had checked their levels so many times that Marta finally took the tubing away from him. “They’re right,” she said. “You watched me make them right. Trust the transit or go ask the internet.” She had told him that if the piers were five millimeters out, the boxes would twist and the cut would never line up. The piers were off by one millimeter.

It went up on the co-op’s lease, on the worn patch where Walt Calder had once parked a combine, and every cut was photographed and every dimension logged, because the deal the co-op had made with itself was that nothing got built only once.

But it was also Elijah’s. Nobody made a ceremony of saying so. He’d had rented rooms and borrowed corners and a bed over a pump house, and now he had a rectangle of dirt that was his.

“What the fuck is a shouse?” he’d asked, the first time he saw it on the whiteboard.

“Shop. House,” Marta said. “A shop that’s set up to keep the person running it alive.”

He had wanted to weld on it. He had wanted it badly, after nearly a year of watching Marta lay beads like the steel was agreeing with her. He’d practiced evenings for a month on scrap, and his practice pieces looked, he thought, honestly good.

He asked for a seam on the header beam, the long spine that would carry the roof across the cut. Marta didn’t say no. She said, “Bring me your coupon,” and put his best practice weld in the big bench vise, and slipped a length of pipe over the free end, and broke it with one pull.

The bead snapped clean down its own centerline. Inside, the break was bright and grainy and full of small round holes, like honeycomb cast in nickel.

“See the sparkle,” she said. It wasn’t a question. “It sat on top of the steel and never became the steel. From six feet it looks like mine.” She set the two halves on the bench where he could take them. “There’s nothing wrong with you, college. You’re a couple hundred hours and a certification test from a structural bead, and the rain is eight weeks out. And this seam,” she put her glove flat on the beam, “holds a roof over someone’s kid. I don’t weld those seams tired, and I don’t let anybody weld them proud.”

He kept the two halves. After that he welded one coupon a month and brought it to her, and she broke it, and he kept those too.

So Marta welded the spine of his house, and Elijah went to his lane, which turned out to be real work he was good at. Before the insulation went in, he laced the walls with temperature sensors, so the shell could report on itself for the rest of its life. He put leak sensors low in the wet room. He built the little dashboard the whole co-op used after that, the one that showed a floor plan with a nervous system, and a blank spot wherever a sensor stopped talking.

Marta gave the finished panel a long look and said, “Huh.”

The day of the cut, Denny ran the plasma torch, and the inside walls came out in glowing rectangles, and light went through the two boxes and made them one room. Sixteen feet of open span where no room had been.

Everyone stood in it. Sixteen feet by forty isn’t a big space, and every person standing in it had lived in bigger.

The stove went in last. The Fisher had sat in the corner of the shed for a year. They bought the new firebrick. They bought the chimney new, all of it, the one line in the budget nobody would let him salvage.

---

The first cold night came in the last week of September.

He laid the fire the way his grandmother’s hands had taught his hands, before he was tall enough to see the top of the stove. The draw caught on the first match. The steel began to tick as it heated.

People drifted over without being organized. Reuben brought chairs. Lou brought a sack of walnuts and cracked them two at a time in one fist. Somebody set a drying rack well back from the stove and hung a pair of socks on it. Tyler sat on the floor. Curtis stood with his back to the heat and his hands behind him and looked, for Curtis, content.

Grace came. She stayed by the door. She had a mug in both hands and she leaned on the frame the whole evening, laughing at the right places, a good fifteen feet from the stove. He noticed where she stood.

He took one photograph from inside. The stove lit, the socks, the new brick glowing through the gap in the door, the dashboard on the wall behind it showing green. He sent it to his mother at 9:41, on a Sunday. For nearly three years he had not found the words to tell her what was happening to the world. The phone rang before the checkmark showed.

“That’s the Fisher,” she said. No hello. The radio was going behind her. “Is that pipe double-wall? The Hendersons had a chimney fire in ’88 and your grandmother talked about it for ten years.”

“Double-wall inside. Insulated through the roof. The inspector signed it Tuesday.”

It was quiet on the line for a bit, with the radio under it.

“It looks right in there,” his mother said, in the voice she used for things that were settled. “It looks like somewhere.”

He wrote it in the notebook after she hung up. Under *PARCELS HELD IN TRUST: 1,* a new line.

*ROOFS: 1.*

---

By eleven everyone had gone but Grace.

She was still in the doorway. He was banking the fire. He didn’t say anything about it, and after a while she came in, and took the chair Reuben had left, and set it down about eight feet back.

“I haven’t sat this close to one in six years,” she said.

He waited.

“I worked for a fire district. Before here. I wasn’t on an engine, I was the one they sent ahead. When the wind was coming, I drove the roads with a clipboard and knocked on every door and told them it was time.” She turned the mug around in her hands. “Most people go. Some don’t. There was a man at the end of a road, older, lived by himself, had a nice place. I told him. He was polite. He said, ‘My house, my choice.’”

The stove ticked.

“So I wrote REFUSED on the sheet. That’s what you write. And I tied a piece of flagging tape on his gate, so the crews would know somebody had been there and he’d been told. And I went to the next door, because there were forty more on that road.” She looked at the fire, finally, straight at it. “That’s what the tape is. Somebody checked.”

“Grace.”

“It came through that night. When they let us back up, the tape was still on the gate. It was about the only thing on that road that was still a color.”

He didn’t say he was sorry. He sat down on the floor by the stove where Tyler had been.

“I was married to a captain,” she said. “Good man. He had a line. I’d come home from a day like that and he’d say, ‘You can’t make them leave, Grace.’ Every time. He thought it was comfort.” She shook her head. “I quit the job and I left him inside of one month. I couldn’t tell you which one I was leaving.”

“He wasn’t wrong, though.”

“No. He wasn’t wrong.” She said it flatly. “I still left.”

Elijah put a split of oak in and shut the door on it. Then he told her about the Bear Flag. He’d never told anyone up here. He told her about the laptop and the napkin, and the hand on his shoulder a week later, and three glasses in the air.

“Three people,” he said. “A coordinator, a marketing person, a kid with a camera. I never even found out their names.”

“You showed them something true,” Grace said. “And they chose.”

“You knocked. And he chose.”

They both heard it. He looked at her, and she looked at him, a little too long.

She looked away first. She laughed.

“God. We’re fun.”

“I met a guy,” he said, to be saying something. “In the city. He makes a lamp that talks to you. You come home and it says it missed you. It asks about your day. Four million people bought one.”

She started to laugh at that, and it quit halfway.

“So nobody has to come home to a dark house,” she said.

“That’s the pitch.”

“That’s not the worst thing I’ve ever heard.”

He thought about a small woman at a sink. *The part where it is kind.* He didn’t say it. It was warm, and it was late, and Grace picked up her chair and moved it a foot closer to the stove.
