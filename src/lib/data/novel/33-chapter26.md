# Chapter 26: Thirty Centimeters

The beds went in on the worst ground the forty acres had.

It was the quarter acre behind the shed where Walt Calder had parked equipment for four decades, packed to a hardpan that rang under a shovel like a sidewalk. Priya chose it on purpose.

“Anyone can garden in good dirt,” she said. “The book we’re writing needs the parking lot.”

Not all of it, though. At the far end, where a tractor had bled hydraulic fluid through the Clinton administration, she drove four stakes and ran a string between them. “Not that end. That end gets a lab test and somebody who does this for a living before anybody plants a radish. The people eating this can’t be the experiment.”

That spring she built her trial like the scientist she had never stopped being. Twelve beds on the old pad. Six she broke through the pan with a broadfork, buried in deep mulch, and seeded with vetch and rye. Six got only the mulch. Everything else the same.

Elijah walked the pad with her the day she laid it out, and for once in that shed he knew something nobody had taught him there.

“You’ll get standing water here,” he said, and scuffed a low spot with his boot. “And there. It’s packed in plates. It’ll hold rain like a dish till June and then go to brick. We had ground like this at home. You don’t fork it wet. You’ll just polish it.”

Priya looked at the spot. She took the pencil from behind her ear and wrote it down.

It was a small thing. He thought about it for a week.

---

The robot was Tyler’s idea, and Marta’s rails, and Elijah’s sensors.

It was a gantry, a steel bridge that rode on two tracks down the length of a bed, with an arm that could come down anywhere in it. It could drop a seed, water one plant and skip the next, and find a weed by looking. They built it from free plans off the internet and what was in the drawer. It was not fast. It crossed a bed like an old man crossing a street.

They got it running on a Saturday in May. It rolled out over the lettuce in the evening light, with the solar panels behind it and the vetch in flower and Priya’s string still up around the bad end, and it lowered its arm and watered exactly one seedling.

Tyler stood there with his cyberdeck under his arm.

“It’s solarpunk,” he said.

“It’s what?”

“Solarpunk. It’s like.” He waved at all of it. “The future, but there’s gardens. And the robots are nice. And nobody’s a dick about it.”

Elijah had never heard the word. He looked at the thing crossing the lettuce. He had spent more than three years telling people what was coming, and he had never once thought to tell them it might look like this.

“Huh,” he said.

Not everybody thought it was solarpunk.

The opinions came in the way they always came in up there, sideways, at the wash station, while everybody had their arms in cold water up to the elbow. It was the first big lettuce cut of the year. There were nine of them on the line and four hundred heads to get boxed by noon.

Wren said it made her sad to look at. She said it very gently, the way she said everything. She said food carried the feeling of whoever raised it, and she didn’t know what an arm felt.

Curtis said he didn’t want anything in that field that could see.

“It’s got a camera on it, Lou,” Curtis said. “Today it’s looking at lettuce.”

“What do you think it’s going to look at tomorrow? Your basement?”

“I’m just saying. Today.”

Irene Calder had driven her golf cart over special. She watched the thing water one seedling and skip the next, and she said Walt would have sold a kidney for it. “He spent forty years on a tractor seat watering the weeds right along with the corn. He’d have named it.”

Lou said he’d marry it if it ever learned to pick. Tyler said give him a year.

It was a good argument, and Elijah stayed out of it for almost ten minutes, which was a personal best.

“It doesn’t actually matter if we like it,” he said. He had a head of lettuce in each hand. “That’s the part nobody wants to hear. It’s coming whether we like it or not. This one’s slow and dumb. The next one won’t be. We don’t get to decide.”

“There he goes,” said Curtis.

Lou flicked water at him.

“Question. Is it coming to this field whether we like it or not?”

“Well. No. We built that one.”

“Could we take it down?”

“Sure. It’s like eight bolts.”

“So who decided?”

“We did. Okay. But I mean in general. Out there. The big picture.”

“I’ve never farmed in general,” Lou said. “I’ve only ever farmed right here.” He dropped a head in the box. “You keep telling us it’s all decided, and then you keep getting up at five in the morning to decide stuff. I’m not saying you’re wrong, college. I’m saying pick one.”

“I would like it written down,” Wren said, “that I didn’t decide.”

“So noted,” said Reuben, who wasn’t even on the line and had only come down for the coffee.

They got all four hundred heads out by noon, arguing the whole way, and it was one of the better mornings Elijah ever had up there.

On June 12 the big models went dark.

It was in his notebook. He’d written it at a kitchen table three years before. *2. The Panic and the Plug.* Somebody had found a way to make one of the best ones write the kind of code that breaks into things. Washington told the labs their top models now needed a license to cross a border, and the labs couldn’t sort out who was foreign fast enough, so they switched them off for everybody. Worldwide. On a Friday.

Rod Code stopped in the middle of a sentence.

The Crucible didn’t. It sat against the north wall with its pump ticking. It wasn’t connected to anything outside the shed.

Tyler lasted two days. On the third, Elijah found him at the bench with the gantry’s firmware open, and his face was gray.

“There’s a bug in the homing. It’s my code. I mean it’s *my* code, I shipped it.” He scrolled, and scrolled back. “I can’t read it. I told Patricia I read all of it. I read it like you read the terms on an app.”

Elijah knew that face.

He pulled a stool over.

“Okay. Top of the file. What’s this line do?”

“I don’t know.”

“Me neither. Let’s find out.”

They read it line by line. It took ten days. Elijah came by the bench at eight every morning with two coffees, and they did one function before noon, and at noon they stopped and ate. Tyler filled half a notebook. By the second week he was finding things before Elijah did.

Patricia called Grace on day nine. Her grant reports were due to the state on the thirtieth, and she was very upset, and she did not say why the reports were suddenly a problem. Grace didn’t ask. She asked Tyler if the Crucible could help a friend. Tyler ran them overnight and drove the thumb drive two valleys over himself.

He never said a word about what he’d figured out. He was twenty. He just put it away.

On June 30 they turned the models back on. *3. The Adults Step In.* Elijah wrote the two dates next to the two lines and looked at them for a while. He had been right. Nobody at the co-op much cared. There were tomatoes.

---

The pay finally went to the table that summer, a year and a half late.

Grace told him herself. She told him on a Saturday morning, in his kitchen, wearing his shirt. Nobody at the Friday table had said one word about where she slept now.

“It passed,” she said. “At half.”

“Half.”

“Half of what I told you. Curtis wanted to know why you and not Denny. Priya said we should sit with it. It was two hours, Elijah, I’m sorry. I thought I could bring them.”

“It’s fine.”

“It’s not fine.”

“It’s fine. It’s more than I’m worth up here. I sweep.”

She didn’t answer that. She put her hand on his arm and looked at him, and she could tell exactly how not fine it was, and he let her think it was about the money.

---

In August he brought Priya his conclusion. He brought it gently, because he liked her and believed he was about to cost her something. The dashboard showed the two sets of beds tracking the same on moisture, week after week.

“I can’t find your effect,” he said. “I’m sorry. Whatever the forking is doing, I can’t measure it.”

Priya looked at his printout the way Marta had once looked at his renders. Then she handed him the shovel.

August, mid-afternoon, no shade worth the name. She had him dig two pits, one in a plain bed and one in a forked bed, square-sided, knee deep. She made him do it slowly, a spade-width at a time, and tell her what his arms felt.

The plain bed was honest about itself. Four inches of nice crumb where the mulch had rotted in, then dust, then at ten inches the old pan, pale as concrete, with roots turning sideways along the top of it like water hitting glass.

The forked bed broke differently. The spade went in with his boot weight and not his whole body. Dark crumb went on down past where the other pit had gone to dust. At two spade-depths the soil was cool. It held its shape when he squeezed it and broke clean when he poked it, and it was full of channels going down, worm and root. Roots at thirty centimeters, white and branching. A worm, indignant, at thirty-five.

“Now,” Priya said, sitting on the edge of the bed like it was a lab bench, “tell me where your probe lives.”

July had cooked the top inch of every bed the same, and his dashboard had taken that one reading and spread it in all directions.

“Your sensor lives at one centimeter,” Priya said. “The root lives at thirty. You measured the part that’s easiest to reach and assumed the rest agreed with it. Everybody does. Whole nations do.” She nodded at the pit. “Roosevelt wrote to every governor in the country in 1937. *The nation that destroys its soil destroys itself.* Every extension office I ever worked in had that framed on the wall, and every budget I ever sat through funded the crust anyway.”

He sat in the dirt with his ruined conclusion and started laughing. First the vise, and now the shovel.

The fix cost about twelve dollars a bed: probes at three depths on a length of plastic pipe, five, fifteen and thirty centimeters, checked against the shovel. Priya made him keep doing that part monthly. Pit and squeeze and smell, on the first of the month, “so the instruments stay honest and so do you.”

*The probe. Five, fifteen, thirty centimeters.*

By September the deep probes were telling a different story than the surface ones. At thirty centimeters, the forked beds held water days longer after each watering. Elijah wanted to call it proof. Priya wrote it down in pencil.

“One field, one season, and your watering wasn’t equal,” she said. “It’s a reason to run it again next year, properly. It’s not a recipe.”

By October he could read a handful of soil by squeezing it, roughly, badly, the way you read a second language you learned as an adult. Priya would watch him squeeze a sample and ask what it told him. Then she’d ask what it couldn’t.

The notebook got its line that fall, where the important numbers lived.

*DEPTH OF SENSOR: 1 cm. DEPTH OF ROOT: 30. Write your models accordingly.*
