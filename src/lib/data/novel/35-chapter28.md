# Chapter 28: Node 7

The first hard storm of the winter came up the valley on a Friday.

The barometer fell like a dropped tool, and by dark the county was gone.

Grid power went first, a whole hillside of windows going out between one gust and the next. The cell towers lasted forty minutes longer on their batteries and then they went too, and the valley was back in 1890, except for nineteen households, where the lights stayed on.

Somebody brought the printed contact list to the shop table. Grace started the soup. She started it in the twenty-quart pot, which meant she’d already decided it was for the whole road.

The mesh held. Elijah had been building toward that sentence for a year, little radios laid node by node across the ridgelines, and on the night it finally mattered the network did what it was designed to do. It shrugged.

The co-op’s map stayed lit, house to house, short messages hopping the valley on watts you could count on your fingers. Marta’s shop stayed lit. The greenhouse alarms stayed armed. The whole nervous system he’d been laying into walls and fence posts for a year kept right on reporting, calm as a pulse.

Except node 7.

Node 7 was the ridge repeater, the high hop that stitched the north houses into everyone else, and node 7 was his, in every way that stung. His design. His install. And, the previous Sunday, his update, done alone, in a hurry, ahead of the weather, without a second set of eyes. It was just a repeater. The rule was for things that touched the open internet. He had let himself believe that.

The storm found the ridge at eight o’clock. At 8:04 the map showed a hole where the north houses used to be.

The north houses checked in on the voice radios, one by one. Everyone was fine. The hole was only in the map. But the hole was his.

He suited up. Nobody climbed the ridge alone, and Curtis was already lacing his boots. Marta handed Elijah the handheld radio and the small pack, pointed at his headlamp, and said the thing she always said instead of luck: “Talk before you touch.”

The ridge trail was a different place in a storm. The rain went sideways with gravel in it, and the madrone trunks were slick as wet brick. The wind leaned on them, let go, and leaned again.

He climbed with the multimeter zipped inside his coat to keep the display warm, Curtis’s light steady behind him, and the radio crackled at his collarbone every quarter mile, Marta running the net from the shop the way she’d once run a floor. Short, no chatter.

And he could hear, in the gaps, the system working around the hole he’d made. Priya’s greenhouse threw a low-temperature alarm at 8:40. It went around the dead ridge the long way, south through Frank’s node, and Denny answered it. He was hauling the backup battery to keep the winter starts alive. All of it happened without him, around him, because the whole design assumed any one node, any one person, could be the one that failed.

It was a strange thing, hearing something he’d built get along without him.

Node 7’s enclosure was where he’d bolted it, on the old fire-lookout stub, and the node was dark. Curtis held a garbage bag over them for a rain fly. Elijah got the lid off, his fingers going stupid with cold, and keyed the radio. He and Marta debugged it across two miles of storm, and she asked the question she always asked.

“Power or brain? Halve it.”

Battery voltage: 12.9, holding under load. The battery was good. Halved.

“Downstream.” That wasn’t Marta. It was Tyler, too loud, new to the button. She had him on the net beside her. “Check the, um. The converter. Under load.”

Five point one volts, solid. Halved again. So: brain.

Elijah plugged in the service cable and read the startup log out loud. The little computer woke up. The radio never did.

Sunday’s update had changed how it started, and he’d taken one good transmission as a test. He hadn’t cut the power and brought it back. The storm had run that test for him.

The fix took twelve minutes because he had, at least, packed like Marta taught. The laptop, the cable, and a copy of the last version that worked, in the dry bag next to the spare fuses.

Restore, restart, cut the power, start again. The status light went to its slow steady breathing, and in his collar the radio said, in Priya’s voice, “North houses just came back. Hello, ridge.”

Below them the whole county was black to the horizon, except for the lights of the co-op’s houses.

Curtis was still holding the bag over him. His arms had to be dead.

“First dinner you came to,” Curtis said. He had to nearly shout it. “I said fence them out. I meant you. Laptop people. Show up when it gets bad and eat what we put by.”

“I know.”

“I wanted a fence for people like you.”

Elijah waited.

Curtis looked up at the garbage bag snapping in the wind, and at his own two hands holding it.

“Now I’m holding your rain fly.”

Elijah stood up into the wind to pack the tools, and Marta’s voice came up the hill one more time. “There’s a line on the shop wall. Old catalog. ‘We are as gods and might as well get used to it.’ The man changed it, later. Get good at it.” A crackle, wind on her end too, the shop door open to the weather. “Gods, hell. Gods is the easy part, college. Gods with cold fingers and a multimeter, that’s the job. Getting good at it means next time you touch a node, somebody’s watching. Come down. Grace has soup.”

They came down. There was soup. Grace had fed half the county road by then, out of the back of the kitchen trailer, by lantern, and she handed him a bowl without stopping and touched his wet sleeve as he went by.

The rule got its amendment on Sunday, in Reuben’s careful language. It covered everything people depended on now.

The storm blew out by noon Saturday. The county’s power was back Tuesday. By then the talk on the mesh was seed orders. Curtis asked who still needed soup taken over.
