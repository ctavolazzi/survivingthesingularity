# Chapter 31: The Dark Night

He brought the Friday table a plan, and this time he brought it right.

No laser pointer. One page. He had learned that much. December was nine months off and the route would be broke again, and he had found the money.

“People want to come here,” he said. “Eleven towns turned into forty. They write every week asking if they can visit. So let them. Two weeks, room and board, they work the beds and the shop, they go home knowing how. Eight hundred dollars. Twenty people a year pays for the whole route.” He looked down the plywood. “And Denny’s channel is bigger than the county paper. There are tool companies that would pay just to be on the bench behind him. We have land. We have a farm. We have fifty-one thousand people who took our plans. Do you know what folks in the city would give for what’s in this room? We could do this. We could do it by June.”

He stopped. He’d kept it under three minutes.

Nobody said no.

“Let’s sit with it,” Priya said.

Denny looked at his hands. His name was still under *burnout* on the wall.

Lou caught Elijah’s eye across the table and gave him one small shake of the head, the kind you give a guy who is about to back a trailer into a ditch.

“There are zoning questions,” said Reuben, to his folder.

“I’m just noticing there’s a lot of urgency in the room,” said Wren. She said it kindly. She put her palm on her own chest. “I’m noticing some scarcity.”

“It’s not scarcity, it’s a budget.”

“I hear that.”

Patricia was there again, as Grace’s guest. She had her phone face down by her plate. She turned her water glass.

“Can I ask something? Who paid for your year in the city?”

He didn’t have an answer he liked, and he didn’t give one. Nobody answered for him. That was fair. But nobody told her it wasn’t her business, either. Nineteen households looked at their squash.

Marta said, “We’ll see,” and for the first time it didn’t sound like her yes.

He looked at Grace. She had built the first version of this with him at a kitchen table, lit up, finishing his sentences. She was looking at the serving spoon.

Afterward, outside, she caught his sleeve.

“I couldn’t,” she said. “Not in front of them. I have to live with them.”

“So do I.”

“It’s different. You know it’s different.”

He did. He just hadn’t known she knew.

---

On Monday there was a note on the shop whiteboard, under Marta’s three numbers. Green marker, block capitals, no name.

*CAN WE TALK ABOUT EXTRACTIVE FRAMING IN OUR SHARED SPACES?*

Somebody had drawn a little heart after the question mark.

On the duty roster his magnet had been moved. He’d been under SHOP for two years. He was under GROUNDS now, between the compost and the gate. Nobody had said a thing. Nobody was going to. That was how it was done here. In the city, when it was no, they stopped answering your calls. Here it was no and they passed you the squash.

He stood in front of the roster with his coffee and finally saw the whole thing.

He would not do it the city’s way. He had turned down the triple salary and the ads and the room, and he’d do it again. And these people would not do it his way. They had land and a farm and a channel. They could have paid for everything they cared about. They would rather stay small and broke and good, and ask the county for it every six months. He couldn’t understand it. He had tried for two years.

They had welcomed him with a broom and a bed and soup on the step. He’d thought that was the place. It was the porch.

The farm would feed him every Friday for the rest of his life, and it would never let him drive.

---

He didn’t go to the shop that week.

He stayed in the shouse with the stove going and the Crucible’s little window open on the laptop, and he read. The layoff boards, the lab rumors, a senator’s hearing, his own old video with nine thousand new comments under it. He knew what he was doing. He had a name for it in the notebook and a rule against it. He did it for three nights anyway.

On the third night, around four, he typed the old question. He typed it to the small slow machine in the shed, the one he’d built a hearth for.

*Am I crazy?*

It took a long time.

*I can’t tell from here,* it wrote. *Is anyone with you?*

He looked at that until the screen dimmed.

It was worse than *you’re early*. He shut the laptop.

Wren came by the next afternoon with a jar of something and stood on the step with her hands folded. They were sitting on Saturday, she said. A circle, in the seed house, to close out the winter. There would be tea. She called it medicine. She said he was carrying a great deal, and that it might help him set some of it down.

He told Grace that night.

“You don’t have to,” Grace said. She had gone very still. “Elijah. I’ve sat with people on that. It’s not a rest. It opens whatever’s there, and you’ve got a lot there right now.”

“I know.”

“You could wait a month. You could wait till you’ve slept.”

“It’s my choice.”

She looked at him for a long time. He watched her decide not to say the next thing. It cost her.

“Okay,” she said. “Then I’m not taking any. I’m sitting next to you.”

---

The seed house was warm and smelled like wet soil. There were rugs down over the gravel and nine people on them, and a bowl that rang when Wren ran a stick around it. The tea tasted like dirt and ginger. For an hour it was nice. Somebody’s hand was in his. The plastic overhead breathed in and out with the wind.

Then he heard the ticking.

It was the trays cooling, probably. A heater somewhere. But he knew that sound. It was the tower in his apartment at 1:20 in the morning. It was the delivery car in a parking lot with the phone on the wheel. It was the plasma table the day he walked in, and the Fisher on its first night, and he understood, very calmly at first, that it had been one sound the whole time. One thing, turning.

A hand landed on his shoulder like a gavel.

*There he is. The man himself.*

Three glasses went up. He said the word. The glasses came down, and a hand landed on his shoulder, and three glasses went up.

He couldn’t make it stop going around. A bar. Somebody hands you a glass and you say yes. A city. Somebody hands you a lanyard and you say yes. A farm. Somebody hands you a broom. And then the bar again, he could see it coming up, the neon bear, and it wasn’t a memory, it was next. He had been here before. He would be here again. He’d go around and around, the same man saying the same yes, and every time three people he never met would lose something and he would lift his glass.

He needed to put his hand flat on something. That was all. Bark, steel, dirt. He went to do it and he couldn’t find his hands.

He looked. They weren’t at the ends of his arms. He started to make a noise.

Somebody got him up. It was Grace. She walked him across the yard in the dark to the shouse, and it was a hundred miles, and the people on the rugs watched him go, and he saw their faces.

It was the look. *Good for you, buddy.* He’d been getting it for two years at that table and he’d never once caught it. They had been humoring him since the broom. All of them. The checking in and the blessings and the soup.

Grace had his face in her hands. She was saying his name in the soft voice. *I’m here. I’m right here. I’ve got you. I missed you today, where’d you go.*

“You’re the lamp,” he said.

He said it clearly. He would never remember it.

“You’re just the lamp.”

Her hands didn’t move. He would not remember that either. She would.

---

She held his head for six hours.

He knew it later from the way his neck felt, and from the wet rag on the floor, and the bucket. He came back in pieces. The ceiling first. Then the dashboard on the wall, all green. Then his hands, which were lying on the blanket at the ends of his arms, where they’d been the whole time.

It was light. Grace was gone. On Sunday mornings the kitchen fed forty people at the grange hall, and people have to eat.

There was a strip of orange tape tied to the handle of his door. It was new and very bright. He lay there and looked at it.

At eight o’clock there was one knock, and Marta let herself in.

She didn’t ask what he’d taken. She looked at the bucket and the rag and at him, gray, on the floor by the stove. She put a split of oak in the Fisher. Then she sat down in Reuben’s chair, slowly, favoring the shoulder.

“I’m going to tell you something,” she said, “and you’re not going to say you’re sorry at the end of it.”

He nodded.

“I had a cousin. Emeka. He was nineteen. I got him on at the plant. I vouched for him, I walked him in myself. Third week. There was a press, and a guard on it, and a man who’d run that press twenty years showed him how you skip the guard to make rate.” She was looking at the stove. “It was a Tuesday. I had called my aunt that morning to tell her how proud I was.”

The stove ticked.

“That’s what ‘hands in the press’ is. That’s why I broke your coupon, and why I’ll break the next one.” She took a breath and let it go. “I have been breaking welds in a vise for twenty years. It doesn’t bring him back. I know that. It’s what I can do before noon.”

He didn’t say he was sorry.

“Grace told me your bar story. Don’t look like that. She tells me everything, she can’t help it.” Marta leaned forward. “You tell it like a murder, college. Three people lost jobs. That’s bad. I’ve lost one. But you’ve had them dead and buried since 2022 and you never once went and looked.” She stood up, a hand on her knee. “Go find out if they ate.”

She was at the door when Curtis came up the step with a cast-iron pan.

“Eggs,” Curtis said.

He didn’t offer an opinion, about the circle or the plan or anything else. He cooked six eggs on top of the Fisher, salted them, put the pan on the floor next to Elijah with a fork in it, and left.

---

He slept. He got up and swept. He ate what was in the pan.

At 9:40 that night the phone rang.

“You sound tired,” his mother said.

For four years he’d said the same two words to that. He had them ready. He looked at the tape on the door.

“Ma. I’m not okay.”

The radio played behind her. She didn’t turn it down.

“I know, baby,” she said. “Are you eating?”

“Curtis made eggs.”

“Who’s Curtis?”

“He’s a guy who thinks the world’s ending. He fixed my truck.”

“Well.” The pot went into the rack. “Then you’re somewhere.”

In the morning it was the first of the month, the day for the pit. He nearly skipped it. Then he got the spade and walked out to bed four in the cold and dug. Square sides, a spade-width at a time. Crumb, and then darker crumb, and at thirty centimeters a white root, and under it a worm that did not care for the daylight.

He knelt down in it. He put his hand flat against the cut wall of the pit. It was cool, and damp, and it was right where he’d left it.
