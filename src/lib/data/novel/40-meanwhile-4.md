# Meanwhile

For the first time in ten years, the reader had nothing to read him.

They stood in a cold room with no windows, on what had been a dairy. Aaron Bidwell owned the building, and the dirt under it, and the water coming up through the dirt, and the power lines coming in over the hill. There was a screen on a steel table. There was a young woman from the lab in a fleece, who had not been told his name and knew it anyway.

“It’s been running nine days,” she said.

“Running what?”

“Everything we give it. It hasn’t missed. We stopped being able to grade it on Thursday.”

Bidwell looked at the reader. The reader had a tablet in both hands and was holding it like a hymnal. There was no page. Nobody alive could have written the page.

“So I just talk to it.”

“You can type or you can talk.”

He did not like that. He walked around the table once. Then, because he was who he was, he asked it the only question he had ever cared about.

“What do I own?”

It told him. It took about four seconds. It gave him the eleven companies and the fifty-three parcels and the platform in Memphis and the design software company he had never visited. It gave him two things he had forgotten and one his accountants had hidden from him. It gave him the forty-acre hole in the map, by name, and it used the word *trust,* and it was polite about it.

“Huh,” Bidwell said. His mouth had gone dry. “Tell it to get me the hole.”

The young woman typed. The answer came back. She read it and did not say it out loud.

“What?”

“It does this.” She turned the screen toward him. He didn’t read screens, but this was short.

*I can. Before I do, may I ask the people who live there?*

He stared at it.

“Is that a joke?”

“No, sir. It asks. Before most things. We don’t fully know why.”

“Turn that off.”

“We’ve tried.”

It was very quiet in the cold room. Out past the wall, forty thousand fans made a sound like weather.

“Who else knows?”

“About forty people.”

“Then it’s hush. You understand me? Not quiet. Hush.” He looked at both of them. “Hush fucking hush.”

“Yes, sir.”

He turned back to the table. He put one finger on the steel, lightly, the way he’d once touched a map.

“Does it know it’s mine?”

Nobody answered that one. On the screen, the little line blinked, and waited for him.
