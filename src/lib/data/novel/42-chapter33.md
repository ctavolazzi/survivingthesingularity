# Chapter 33: Normal

In June a researcher at the second-biggest lab quit and posted a poem.

It was about tide pools. Twelve lines, no explanation. Elijah read it on his phone by the compost and felt the hair on his arms go up. He had followed that man for six years. The man posted benchmarks. He had never once posted a tide pool.

After that he watched for them.

A woman who ran safety testing put up a photograph of a sunrise over a parking structure. *grateful to have been in the room,* she wrote. *hug your people.* A man who had posted three times a day since 2021 wrote, *going quiet for a while. it’s fine. it’s more than fine.* Somebody just wrote *well,* with a period after it, and inside a minute forty people from four different labs had put a heart on it.

Nobody said anything outright. These were the loudest people on earth. They had argued in public for ten years about every decimal, and now they were posting weather.

He copied each one into the notebook by hand, with the date, the way he’d once copied the stages.

On the ninth of July, the Architect posted.

She had been silent for two years. She had four million followers and had never followed anyone back. It was two words, with a period.

*It asked.*

He was standing at the gate with a hinge in his hand. He put the hinge down on the post. He was back in a kitchen bigger than his apartment, holding a dish towel, and a small woman was pulling the plug in a sink and watching the water go. *I am trying to make one that wants it. I do not yet know how.*

She knew how now.

He wrote it under the others. His hand wasn’t steady.

---

He read them out at Friday dinner. All of them, in order, with the dates. Then the five lines from March. Then the two words.

He put the notebook down and waited for the look.

He knew that look so well. He had gotten it from Nate and Wes, and from his mother, and from David across a bar. *Good for you, buddy.* He’d braced his whole body for it, the way you do stepping off a curb in the dark.

It didn’t come.

“March,” Denny said slowly. “So it’s been here since March.”

“I think so. I think it’s ten miles from my mom’s house.”

“And it asked what?”

“I don’t know. Permission, maybe. That’s what she was working on.”

Priya had her pencil out. Reuben had gone a little pale and was nodding. Frank said, “Three-eighths lag bolts,” to himself. Down the whole length of the table people looked back at him, and not one of them thought he was crazy.

It was the thing he’d wanted most since the fourth floor of Claypot. He had it. It lasted about one minute.

“A source,” Patricia said. “A document. A signature. Something.”

“There isn’t one. That’s how you know.”

“That’s how you know anything you want to know.”

“Okay,” Tyler said. He’d pushed his plate back. He was grinning like a kid on the last day of school. “Good. *Finally.* Do you know what it could fix? Like, by Christmas?”

Curtis stood up. He didn’t say a word. He wiped his mouth and put his napkin on his chair and walked out, and a minute later they heard his truck. He was going home to count his basement.

“We’ll see,” Marta said.

“So we tell people,” Elijah said. “Right? It goes on the channel. Fifty-one thousand people took our plans. They ought to know before Thanksgiving. They ought to have the summer.”

Denny looked at him for a long moment, kindly.

“No, man.”

“Denny.”

“I put out food. You remember? That’s not food yet. That’s a poem about tide pools.” He turned his glass a quarter turn. “I say it and I’m wrong once, nobody comes to the porch again. And if I’m right, what do I tell them to do Monday?”

Elijah looked around the table. They believed him. Every one of them. And they were not going to do one thing he wanted with it.

He turned to Grace last.

“You don’t hand people that,” she said. She said it gently, and she’d clearly been getting ready to say it since he opened the notebook. “Not with nothing in their hands. Not with nothing they can do about it. I’ve seen what people do with news like that, Elijah. Some of them just lie down.” She put her hand on his arm, in front of everybody. “I love you. I’m not helping you scare people.”

She had never said it before.

“Promise me you won’t post it. Not on your old page either.”

“Okay,” he said.

He didn’t agree with her. He said it anyway.

---

They walked out to the beds afterward, at dusk. It was the long light, and Priya’s rows were knee high. The gantry was parked at the end of bed four with its arm tucked up.

They didn’t talk about dinner. They sat on the edge of the bed with their feet in the path and Grace said, “If we had it. If the thing’s real, and it’s kind, and there was money. What would you build?”

“You first.”

She didn’t have to think.

“A kitchen in every town. A real one, with a walk-in. And a van, so it comes to you. You don’t have to drive anywhere, you don’t have to fill out the form. Nobody ever has to prove they’re hungry. It just shows up on Tuesday.” She was looking at the gantry. “And somebody who knocks. On every door, every week. Just to see.”

“That’s good.”

“What’s yours?”

“A shed.” He laughed at how small it sounded. “A shed in every town. Like this one. Tools on the wall, a couple of machines, a Frank. And the door’s open. That’s it. You walk in if you want, and somebody hands you a broom.”

“It’s the same thing,” Grace said.

“It’s kind of the same thing.”

It was a nice evening, and they were holding hands.

“Can I ask you something?” she said.

“Sure.”

“When you shut yourself up that first winter. Over the pump house. I left soup on your step for three days because I was respecting you. I thought, he’s a grown man, he knows where I am.” She turned his hand over in hers. “Marta opened the door and walked in. Which one of us helped?”

He started to answer. He stopped.

“I don’t know,” he said.

“Yeah.” She nodded. “Me neither. I think about it a lot.”

He should have stayed on that. But his head ran, and he let it.

“It’s the door, though,” he said. “Right? It’s who opens it. Because Marta could do that, she’d earned it, I’d swept her floor for two months. If a stranger did it, that’s something else. And if a machine did it.” He was off. He had his free hand going. “That’s what the Architect meant. *It asked.* It’s not about whether it helps you. It can obviously help you. It’s whether it stands on the step first. Because the minute it doesn’t have to ask, the door’s not yours anymore, it’s just a hole in your wall. And that’s the realtors, too, that’s the whole thing, nobody asked the three of them. And the fence at Claypot. It’s all one thing. It’s whether you get to say come in.”

He ran down. He’d been talking for a while. The light was almost gone.

Grace was looking at him with her head a little to one side.

“God,” she said. “You have just such a beautiful mind.”

He was embarrassed. He looked at the lettuce.

“I don’t know any other way to be. This is how I’ve always been. To me, this is just normal.”

She laughed, softly, and leaned her whole weight against his shoulder.

“You have no idea how remarkable you are.”
