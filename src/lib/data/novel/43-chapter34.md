# Chapter 34: A Few Thousand Dollars of Robot Arms

The harvest festival was two weeks off, and they were eighteen people short.

Reuben had the number on the whiteboard in the shouse, circled. It was a Tuesday night in the middle of October and everybody who could still stand up was at the meeting. The festival was the one big thing the co-op did all year. The other farms in the network came, and now people from the towns in the inbox were coming too, from as far as Idaho. Reuben figured four hundred, over three days. There would be a cider press and a band and a pie table, and on the last night, if the rain came and the county opened burn season, the bonfire.

And it all landed in the same nine days as everything else. The winter squash had to come in. The apples had to come off. The garlic had to go in the ground. The route still ran on Tuesdays, a hundred and forty boxes, and the route’s money ran out again in December, which nobody was saying out loud.

“Forty bodies for nine days,” Reuben said. “We have twenty-two. One of them is Curtis.”

“Hey,” said Curtis.

Patricia was there, at the end of the table, because the network’s name went on the banner. She had a binder. She had her phone face down next to it.

They went around on it for half an hour. Could the high school send kids. Could Frank close the tool library for a week. Could they skip the garlic. Lou said sure, you could skip the garlic, if you wanted to be the one to explain to Marta in June why there wasn’t any garlic. It was the kind of meeting where everybody is tired and everybody is being nice about it. Elijah had not brought that table an idea since July. But he got comfortable, and he had a beer in him, and he said it the way you’d offer to bring ice.

“I mean, I know a guy with arms.”

A couple of people laughed.

“No, like robot arms. He’s got a whole warehouse of them. I unloaded a bunch for him once. I could give him a call. He’d probably ship a few down, he’s like that.” He was peeling the label off his bottle. He wasn’t even looking up. “They could pack the route boxes and run the press. That frees up, what, eight people? We’d have the squash in by Wednesday.”

Nobody said anything.

The stove ticked. Elijah looked up, finally, and it was like a cold draft had come in under the door. Twenty-some people were looking at him, and then at each other, and nobody wanted to go first.

Wren had her hand at her throat.

“Arms,” Curtis said.

“Just a couple.”

“You want to put the Terminator in the packing shed.”

“It’s not the Terminator, Curtis. It’s an arm. It’s bolted to the floor. It can’t go anywhere.”

“I mean, that just perfectly makes sense, though?” Tyler had his chair tipped back on two legs. “Like, yeah. Let’s automate stuff. We’re eighteen people short. What are we even talking about?”

“We are talking about a machine doing the route,” Wren said. Her voice shook. “People’s food. Some stranger’s machine touching every box.”

“A machine made the box, Wren,” said Frank.

“Whose arms?”

That was Marta. She hadn’t moved.

“A friend’s.”

“What friend?”

“A guy I know. From before. He’s a good guy, Marta. He’s got more of them than he can use.”

He didn’t say who. He didn’t have to. Everybody in that room knew where he had spent 2024, and what kind of people he had met there.

Denny hadn’t said a thing. He was turning his water glass, a quarter turn and back. The arms had come into Denny’s warehouse in 2019. Elijah had heard that story at his first Friday dinner, and he had forgotten it. His face went hot. He felt like an asshole.

Then it broke open. Tyler said this was exactly the kind of fear that kept them broke. Curtis said he’d told everybody the very first night that laptop people show up and bring their friends. A man from the north houses said he’d take a robot over another October like the last one, and his wife told him to hush. Priya wanted to know what an arm actually did to a squash, physically, did it bruise, and nobody answered her. Wren was crying a little. Frank and Irene had gotten off onto a milking machine somebody’s father bought in 1961. It was two arguments, and then it was four.

Then all of it turned on him.

“You said you were done bringing plans,” Wren said.

“It’s not a plan. I said I know a guy.”

“Three years,” Wren said. She was really crying now. “Three years, and it’s still a machine with you. Every time. We’re people, Elijah. You look at us and you see something that needs fixing.”

“That’s not fair.”

“Is this what it was all for?” said the wife of the man from the north houses. “You sweep the floor, you get everybody comfortable, and then here come the friends?”

“Whoa.” He had both hands up. He could hear his own voice going high, like a kid’s. “Whoa! I’m just making a suggestion here. Okay? It is possible for robot arms to do this work. And it doesn’t cost hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars. It costs like a few thousand. Like, I can show you.”

He had the laptop in his bag. He got it out and found the page, a price list, one page, with pictures. He turned it around on the table toward Patricia, because the network’s name was on the banner and she spoke for the network.

“No,” Patricia said.

“It’s one page.”

“I don’t need to see it.”

“You don’t even want to look?”

“No.”

She said it quietly, with her hand flat on her binder. She wasn’t angry. She looked like a woman turning down a pamphlet at her front door.

“If there is a robot arm in that packing shed,” she said, to Reuben and not to him, “the leaf comes off every box that leaves this farm. And the network’s name comes off the festival. I won’t have a choice. I’m sorry.”

“Patricia,” Priya said. “It’s a price list.”

“Hell, I’ll look at it,” said Lou.

But it didn’t matter who else looked. The network bought more than half of what Lou grew, and the little green leaf was on every label, and Patricia was the one who decided who got a leaf. Three or four of them were as shocked as he was. Priya’s mouth was open. Tyler said “Are you serious?” to nobody. But none of them had her say.

He could have taken a no. He had heard no in that room before. But she would not look. It was a price list, and she would not turn her head and look at it. He sat there with the laptop pointed at her shoulder and felt like an idiot.

An arm would have done exactly what she told it, to the ounce, in every box. They could have run that shed better and more precisely than it had ever been run. They just didn’t want to learn a new way of doing things.

Reuben closed the folder on his finger.

“We’ll table it,” Reuben said, which at that table meant it was dead.

Grace had not looked at him once. She was looking at the whiteboard, at the eighteen, and her lips were moving a little, like she was counting something else.

“Item four,” Reuben said. “Toilets.”
