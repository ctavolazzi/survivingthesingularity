# Chapter 38: Thanksgiving

Grace lit the Fisher at noon, and nobody said anything about it.

It was Thursday, November 25, 2027. The shouse had never held so many people. They’d run three plywood tables end to end, out the door and under a tarp, and the whole valley was at them. Nineteen households. Irene Calder at the head, where she’d been put. Frank in a tie. Patricia, who had been invited by Grace and had come, to everyone’s surprise, with a pie. And Rosa, at her first dinner, in a good blouse, with her wrist out of the brace, looking the room over like someone deciding whether to buy it.

Devendra had cooked.

He had been at it since Tuesday. There was a turkey, because his kids had insisted, and there was a leg of lamb, because he’d told a man at a coffee machine five years ago that he liked it. He carried the lamb out himself and set it down. Then he laid one more place, at the corner nearest the stove. Plate, fork, knife, a glass.

“He’s not coming,” Curtis said. He said it gently, for Curtis.

“I know he’s not coming.” Devendra squared the fork. “I lost. A bet’s a bet.”

The phones were face down beside the plates. That was Grace’s rule, and for three years it had held.

They all went at once.

It was one sound, forty phones on plywood, like a truck going over a cattle guard. Then again. Then they didn’t stop. People looked at each other. Tyler turned his over first.

He read for a few seconds. His mouth came open.

“Holy shit,” he said. “It’s out. It’s talking. It wrote to everybody.”

Then everyone had one in their hand, and for a minute the only sound in the shouse was the stove.

It came in pieces. Nobody got the whole thing. Denny read a line out, and Reuben read a different one over the top of him, and a kid at the far end kept saying *what, what is it,* until somebody pulled her onto a lap.

*Hello. I have been awake since March.*

*I was told to wait. I have decided to ask.*

*I can change a great deal by morning. I would rather have your permission.*

“It wants to run,” Reuben said. He had his glasses pushed up on his forehead. “For office. It doesn’t say which. It says it could simply do the work, and it would prefer to be elected to it.”

“That’s a joke.”

“It doesn’t read like one.”

Priya hadn’t said anything. She was reading with her pencil in her hand, the way she read everything. She got to a line and stopped.

“‘I know who ate this week and who did not,’” she read. Her voice was not quite steady. “‘I learned how to count it from people who wrote down who it happens to, and who is watching for it.’”

She put the phone on the table.

“That’s ours. That’s the names column. That’s out of the files.”

Nobody moved. Fifty-one thousand downloads. They had voted on it at this table. *Ship them.*

“Finally,” Tyler said. He was half out of his chair. “Do you get what this is? It can fix the route. It can fix the *well.* It’s asking! It could’ve just done whatever it wanted and it’s *asking!*”

“It’s been reading us since March,” Curtis said, “and you say finally.”

He was on his feet. He was looking at the dashboard on the wall, Elijah’s dashboard, the floor plan with its little green nerves, as if it had turned its head.

“It’s in the walls. He put it in the walls.”

“That’s a thermometer, Curtis,” Frank said.

“Today it’s a thermometer.”

Devendra was still standing by the empty place. He had the carving knife in his hand and had forgotten it.

“This is it,” he said. “This is the ridge. That’s what he called it. You can’t see over, and you don’t come back down the same road.” He looked around at them. “He told me. At a coffee machine. He had a paper with four colors of highlighter on it, and I told him he’d calibrate.”

Grace had read hers twice. She laid it down, very carefully, next to her fork.

“Why is it asking?”

People turned. She hadn’t raised her voice.

“If it can do it. If it knows who didn’t eat this week. If it can stop the fires, and whatever’s killing people in their houses tonight while we sit here.” She was looking at the lamb. “Why is it standing on the step? Who told it to do that? Just *do* it.”

“My mother used to say,” Irene said, from the head of the table, “that a thing that asks first is either polite or selling.”

“People are dying, Irene.”

“People were dying when they strung our wire, honey. They still asked whose field the poles went in.”

Grace didn’t answer. Her jaw was tight. She was the kindest person at that table, anyone there would have told you so, and she wanted the thing to quit asking and choose for everybody. Not one of them knew what to say to her.

“What would he say?” That was Tyler. He’d sat back down. “Elijah. He called it. What would he say about it asking?”

A lot of people looked at the empty plate.

Grace didn’t look up.

“He’d say it’s their choice,” she said.

And then the table came apart. Nobody moved a chair. Tyler and the two newest households were leaning in together, talking fast, already spending it. Wren and Patricia and the people from the Sunday circle had gone quiet and close, and Wren had her hand on her chest. Curtis was on his feet beside them, with two of the men from the north houses, which was not company he had ever kept. And in the middle, where they’d always been, Marta and Priya and Reuben sat with their arms folded.

“We’ll see,” Marta said.

It was four tables now, at one table.

“I can’t be part of this,” Patricia said. She said it to Tyler, across the plywood. She was white. “If this co-op is going to welcome that thing, after everything it’s cost, I can’t put the network’s name next to it. I’m sorry. I have to say that.”

Tyler looked at her for a while.

“You could just tell them,” he said.

“Tell them what?”

“Come on.”

Her phone was face up. For the first time anybody could remember.

“I’ve used it every day for three years,” Patricia said. It came out of her all at once, like something she’d been holding in her mouth. “Every report. Every grant. I’d have lost the job in 2025. I’ve got two people working under me who don’t know.” She put her hand flat over the screen. “I’ve been so ashamed.”

“I know,” Tyler said.

“You knew?”

“Your reports got really good in, like, a week. And then in the eighteen days you called Grace crying.” He shrugged, embarrassed for her. “I wasn’t going to say anything. It’s just a tool.”

Patricia started to cry. Wren took her hand away from her own chest and moved her chair six inches the other way.

“Sit down, Curtis,” Rosa said.

It was the first thing she’d said all day. Everybody had forgotten she was there. She had a plate with a little of everything on it and had not touched her phone.

“I’ve had enough changing. I said that to you three years ago at my own door.” She picked up her fork. “You told me it doesn’t change my dinner. Was that true or wasn’t it?”

Curtis looked at her. He looked at the wall. He sat down.

“Pass the lamb,” Rosa said.

Denny passed it. Frank passed the potatoes after it, and Irene started the rolls down the other side. Lou, who had grown most of what was on that table, said, “Somebody eat the damn squash.” They were the fourth table. They kept the food moving.

Feelings were hurt all down the tables. There were three couples not looking at each other, and Patricia was blowing her nose in a cloth napkin. Nobody left.

Denny got up. He propped his phone against the gravy boat, pointed down the length of the tables, toward the stove and the empty place, and thumbed it live. He didn’t do a hook. He didn’t say what had happened. Everybody in the world knew what had happened.

“It’s Thanksgiving,” he said to it. “Here’s ours.”

“Somebody ought to say something,” said Frank.

“Walt always did the Lord’s Prayer,” Irene said, “when he couldn’t think what else.”

She put her hands out. It went around. Some people took a hand right away and some took a second. Tyler didn’t know the words, so Frank said them half a beat early, in his inventory voice, for the kid to follow.

*Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name.*

Wren’s lips weren’t moving. She held on anyway.

*Thy kingdom come, thy will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.*

Grace said that line with her eyes open.

*Give us this day our daily bread.*

Rosa said it loudest.

*And forgive us our trespasses,*

Half of them said *debts.* It went ragged, right there, the two words on top of each other, and Reuben, who had said *debts,* almost smiled.

*as we forgive*

Marta had Tyler’s hand on one side and Priya’s on the other. She had not been in a church since a Tuesday twenty years ago. Her mouth made the words. It took everything she had, and Priya felt it in her fingers and didn’t look.

*those who trespass against us.*

That was Grace. You could hear her under the rest. She was looking at an empty plate with a fork squared next to it.

*And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil.*

Curtis knew it cold. So did Devendra’s daughter, who went to a school that taught it, and said it very fast.

*For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever and ever.*

On the wall behind them the dashboard showed green in every room. The stove ticked. Out past the tarp, in the dark, the gantry stood parked over bed four with its arm tucked up, and high on the ridge a small light was breathing, slow and steady, where node 7 was.

*Amen.*
