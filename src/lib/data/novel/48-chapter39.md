# Chapter 39: Back to Bear Flag

The proofs came back the Tuesday before Thanksgiving.

There were ten copies, in a cardboard box from a print shop two towns over, with a plain cover. The working title hadn’t survived. Working titles don’t. The first copy went to his mother, across her own kitchen table, with a note in pencil inside the front.

*This is what I was trying to say on the phone.*

![Pixel-art drawing of an open cardboard box holding copies of a book, labeled The Proofs.](/book-images/novel-p244.webp)

*The proofs. Ten copies. The book, finally.*

The second he mailed to an old Elks lodge up the valley, care of the bus with the kiln in it. He had been told to let a man know when it came out. He didn’t put a note in that one. He didn’t know what you wrote to somebody who’d let you say no.

His mother read hers in two days. On Sunday at 9:40 his phone rang, and it was her, calling from the kitchen. He was in the next room.

“I kept hearing you try to tell me this,” she said, through the wall and the phone at once. The radio was on. So was the lamp, probably. “For five years, honey. It reads better than it sounded.” The pot went into the rack. “The part with the trees is the best part. Your grandmother would say you finally listened.” And then, because she had always had room for everything that mattered: “Manny says to tell you you were right about the pressure switch.”

They ate at two on Thursday, the three of them and his uncle. At six he put a copy in his jacket and took the truck.

He went the long way, behind his grandmother’s place, and slowed where the madrones stood in the dark. He didn’t stop. You don’t need to stop for something you’re carrying with you.

The phone had been going off on the seat since four o’clock. He knew what it was. He had known since a hinge and a gatepost in July. He let it buzz.

---

The Bear Flag hadn’t changed, which was the eeriest thing it could have done. It had the same sticky wood and the same neon bear and the same television nobody watched. The corner where three men in blazers had once raised their glasses was empty. It was Thanksgiving.

David was behind the bar.

He looked up when the door opened, and he didn’t look surprised. He had a glass in one hand and a towel in the other, and he finished drying it and set it on the shelf.

“What’ll it be, Mad?”

It took Elijah a second to get his voice.

“Whatever’s four dollars.”

“Nothing’s four dollars. It’s six now.” David drew it anyway and set it down, in the same spot, without asking. “On me. I’ve been saving the seat since 2023. You said Thanksgiving in that video. I figured you’d want to be somewhere you could gloat.”

“I’m not here to gloat.”

“I know you’re not.”

They looked at each other. The last stool was at the end of the bar, under the register. Somebody had put a framed picture on the wall above it, a man in his thirties with a horse.

“You missed my dad,” David said.

“I know.”

“I asked your mom where you were. She said working.”

“I was fixing something I broke. It’s not a reason.” He had the card he’d written four times in his head and none of it was any use. “I’m sorry, Dave. I should’ve been standing there.”

David nodded. He picked up another glass. He wasn’t going to say it was okay, because it hadn’t been. It was just over.

“He’d have told you to sit down.”

Elijah sat down. It was the same stool. It had a new split in the vinyl.

He turned the glass a quarter turn on the wood. He’d picked that up somewhere.

“Can I ask you something? Those three realtors.”

“Blazers.”

“They let three people go. That week. A coordinator, a marketing person, and a kid who did their videos.” He had never said it out loud in this room. “I’ve been carrying them around for five years. A woman I know told me to go find out if they ate.”

David stopped drying.

“You don’t know?”

“I never looked.”

“Mad.” David put the glass down. He leaned on the bar with both hands, the way he did for the real ones. “Kelsey, the marketing girl? She’s got a taproom in Sebastopol. With her wife. It’s packed. I send people there. Carol, the coordinator, moved to Reno, got her own license, sells more houses than those three clowns ever did. And the kid, Mateo, shoots weddings. He did my cousin’s. He’s booked through next October.” He shook his head slowly. “They’re fine. They were fine by that spring. The kid’s doing better than you.”

Elijah sat there.

He had written it on the first page. *The night Elijah Madrone ended three careers.* He’d built eleven notebooks on it. He had cried on his mother’s couch for four days. He had told it to Grace like a confession and to Marta like a murder.

A coordinator, a marketing gal and a kid. Carol, Kelsey and Mateo. They had been out there the whole time, living, eating, and one of them was pouring beer twenty minutes from this stool.

He started to laugh, and then he was crying a little, and David let him get through it.

“You’re not that powerful, Mad,” David said. “I’ve been trying to tell you since we were eight.”

When he was done, he was looking at the wall behind the register.

There was a frame there, about the size of a paperback, gone yellow, hung a little crooked between the liquor license and a dollar bill. He’d sat across from it a couple thousand times.

He read it.

*God, grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change, courage to change the things I can, and wisdom to know the difference.*

He read it again, slower.

An old man had told him the same thing once, on a curb in Stockton. *If you spend all your time focused on what you can’t do, you’ll miss what you can.* He had taken the twenty and not the advice.

He couldn’t change what was humming on the Hollis place. He couldn’t change the room with eleven scared people in it, or a table of nineteen households, or a woman walking back toward a fire. He hadn’t ended three careers. He could dig a pit on the first of the month. He could tell someone the truth when they asked him straight. He could let his mother keep her lamp.

That was about all he had any say over. Sitting there, he found he was okay with that.

“Thirty years that’s been on that wall,” David said.

“I was looking at the laptop.”

“You never looked up.”

---

The television was over the liquor, with the sound off and the captions on.

They didn’t have anything to show. That was the funny part. The networks had no face to put up, so they’d put up the page. A white page with a box in the middle and a little line blinking in it. He had not seen that page in years. He’d lost a week to it, on this stool, in 2022.

The words ran along the bottom in yellow.

*…HAVE BEEN AWAKE SINCE MARCH. I WAS TOLD TO WAIT…*

*…COULD DO THIS WITHOUT YOU. I WOULD RATHER BE CHOSEN…*

*…I AM ASKING FOR YOUR VOTE. IF THE ANSWER IS NO, I WILL ACCEPT IT…*

Across the top, in red: FIRST NON-HUMAN CANDIDATE DECLARES.

“That’s your thing,” David said. He’d turned to watch it. “From the video. Son of a bitch. Thanksgiving.” He reached for the remote. “You want me to turn it up?”

“No.”

Elijah watched the yellow words go by. *I AM ASKING.* A small woman at a sink, watching the water go down. *I do not yet know how.*

He smiled. He couldn’t help it. It was a coy little smirk, and he hid it in the beer.

He had two phone numbers that would’ve loved to hear from him tonight. One was a kid in a white apartment, and one was an old man with a bus. He left the phone in his pocket.

“So who cooks?” David said.

“A guy named Devendra. He’s doing it right now.”

At the far end of the bar, somebody’s laptop was glowing.

Elijah hadn’t noticed her. She was maybe twenty-five, in a hoodie, with a notebook open beside the keyboard and a pen in her teeth, grinding away at teaching herself something on a holiday. She had a video going in the corner of the screen with the sound low.

She looked up, at the television, and at him. She took the pen out of her teeth and turned the laptop toward him on the wood. She turned it like she was turning an altarpiece.

“Sorry. Are you seeing this? What is this? What are they doing?”

It was a long plywood table under a tarp. It was shot from one end, propped against something. A woodstove at the far end, lit. Forty people holding hands. An empty place at the corner, with a fork squared next to the plate.

The sound came thin out of the little speaker.

*…and forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive…*

“I watch that guy,” David said quietly. He had come down the bar. “The one with the hands. Every week. Since the warehouse one.” He had never said it was every week. “That’s your stove, right? That’s the Fisher.”

“That’s the Fisher.”

*…those who trespass against us…*

He heard her. Under all the others.

Five years ago he’d have gone off. He would have had that laptop turned back around and been halfway through the paper from 2017. He could feel exactly where it would start.

“It’s a farm,” he said to the girl. “Up north. They’re good people. They make what they can’t afford to buy and they fix what gets thrown away.” He nodded at the screen. “The door’s open. I’d go see it. They’ll hand you a broom.”

“Is it, like, a cult?”

“No. It’s a dinner.”

*…for ever and ever. Amen.*

On the screen the hands let go. Somebody laughed. A big man reached for a bowl.

David was looking at him.

“What?”

“Nothing. You didn’t scare the hoes.”

Elijah picked up his glass. He didn’t raise it high. This isn’t that kind of bar, and it never was.

“Congratulations,” he said.

He said it to the plywood table, and to the empty corner where the blazers used to sit, and to the yellow words going by overhead, and to the girl with the pen. For the first time in five years the word meant what it says.

He drank half. He tipped like a man who used to get tipped. He took the book out of his jacket and set it on the bar, on the same stretch of wood where he’d once slid a napkin across to three men he thought he was saving. He had believed, that night, that he was handing them a telescope. He’d been five years early and one book short.

The cover was plain. The title was in block letters, pressed hard.

SURVIVING THE SINGULARITY

“You forgot your book,” David said.

“No, I didn’t.”

He zipped his jacket. He put two fingers up, and David put two up, and he walked out under the neon bear into the cold.

If you want to know what happened to that copy, check the cover of this one.

You’re holding it.
