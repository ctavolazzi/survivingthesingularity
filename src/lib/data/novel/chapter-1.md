# Chapter 1: You'll Calibrate

The Monday after the toast, Elijah brought the printout to work.

He'd read "Attention Is All You Need" so many times over the weekend that the pages had gone soft at the corners, furred with highlighter in four colors that had stopped meaning anything. He'd slept maybe five hours across two nights. He carried the thing into Claypot the way you carry a lab result you don't want confirmed. He went looking for Devendra, because Devendra was senior, and calm, and had been doing this since before Elijah could spell gradient descent. Elijah needed someone to tell him he was overreacting. He found him at the good coffee machine on the fourth floor.

![Devendra. Senior and calm.](/images/novel/figure-02.webp)

<em>Devendra. Senior and calm.</em>

"I think it's already over," Elijah said. "I watched three people get replaced last week. Real people. A bar, a Tuesday, three jobs, gone. And the guys who did it didn't even think it was a big deal."

Devendra tapped his cup against the drip tray, unhurried. "Augmentation, not replacement," he said. "It's a very good autocomplete, Elijah. It makes the people who use it faster. Nobody fired those people because of a chatbot. They restructured. Companies restructure."

He smiled the way you smile at someone who's just discovered a thing you made your peace with years ago.

"You'll calibrate. Everybody's first month feels like the apocalypse."

Elijah stood there holding the soft pages and wanted the calm to work on him. But it slid off, because he'd seen the training curves inside this building.

"When, then?" he said. "If it's not now. When does it do your job?"

"Mine?" Devendra laughed. "The surveys say decades. Thirty years. Forty. The people who build these things can't agree within a generation. It's a curve, Elijah. A long, slow curve."

"It took those guys a week."

"Fine." Devendra was enjoying himself now. "Give me a date."

Elijah hadn't known he had one until he said it.

"Five years. Thanksgiving, 2027. A machine that can do any job in this building."

"And if it's just a Thursday?"

"Loser cooks."

Devendra peeled a sticky note off the pad by the sugar. He wrote <em>E.M. Thanksgiving 2027. Loser cooks.</em> in small square letters and pressed it to the side of the machine, under the sign about descaling.

"I like lamb," he said, and went back to his desk.

* * *

He hadn't planned to walk back into the Bear Flag so soon. But it was Monday, the realtors drank on Tuesdays, and there wasn't anywhere else he went.

He put the printout on the bar and tried to explain it to David. He was bad at it. He kept flattening the pages with his hand and starting over. David dried glasses and let him.

"So it guesses the next word," David said.

"Yes. No." Elijah thought about it. "Yes."

"Mad. So does my dad."

At the end of the bar, Ray McGrath raised his club soda an inch without looking over.

"I bet a guy at work it can do all our jobs in five years," Elijah said.

"What'd you bet?"

"Dinner."

"Big spender." David set a beer on the printout, right on the abstract, and it left a ring. "You sleeping?"

"Sure."

David looked at him a second longer than he needed to. Then he went to change a keg.

* * *

Later that night Elijah decided to settle it with his own hands. He'd try to break it.

He had an old tower at home, a space heater of a machine he'd built for cheap when he was learning, two consumer GPUs zip-tied into a case that didn't quite fit them. He pulled a modest open model and started training it further on his own data. He told himself that if he could feel where it topped out, if he could find the ceiling by touch, he'd know it was just software, bounded like all software, and he could sleep.

![The rig. Two GPUs zip-tied into a case that never fit them.](/images/novel/figure-03.webp)

<em>The rig. Two GPUs zip-tied into a case that never fit them.</em>

The room got warm around eleven. Warm like the cab of his delivery car used to get in August, that thick engine heat with a smell to it. The fans spun up to a howl he could hear from the kitchen. He kept feeding it. The error kept sliding down. Not magically, not forever, but steadily. Every step cost him more. More power off the wall, more heat into the room, the meter he couldn't see spinning somewhere out by the carport.

At 1:20 a.m. the machine threw the temperature warning and slowed itself down to protect the chips. He queued another run anyway, because he was stubborn, and it shut off hard. Black screen. The fans coasted to silence. The room ticked as it cooled, exactly like the engine of a car after a long haul.

The limit he found was heat.

The machine that had dissolved three careers at the Bear Flag needed watts. It needed cooling. It ran on the same dumb physics that decided whether a crop lived or died. He had wanted proof it was just software, and what he got was a dead screen and a room too hot to sleep in.

So it had a ceiling. The ceiling was a power bill. Whoever could pay for the power and the cooling could have as much of it as they wanted. He stood at the window in his boxers, looking at the dark apartments across the lot, and he didn't think many of those folks could.

He didn't tell Devendra any of this.

He left the tower off for a week. Then he opened the notebook where he'd written MADRONE IS NOT A DRONE, turned to a clean page, wrote the date at the top, and started keeping track.
