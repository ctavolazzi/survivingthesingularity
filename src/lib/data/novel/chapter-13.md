# Chapter 13: Two in the Afternoon

In the morning the fever was down enough to drive. He did not go in to get sorted out.

He left the lot with the windows down and the heater on, because he couldn't get warm. He had a quarter tank and no money. Home was two hours north. He got as far as a side street downtown and turned the truck off.

He could not have told you why. He wasn't going to walk into her kitchen looking like this. She had told the ladies at church about the senator.

On Sunday at 9:40 the phone rang on the dash. He watched it until it stopped. Then he typed <em>Working late. All good. Love you</em>, and sent it, and turned the phone off.

He turned it back on Tuesday, to look for work.

There was one voicemail, from Sunday, 9:41. He would listen to it later. He would listen to it a great many times.

At two in the afternoon the phone rang in his hand, and it was her number. She never called at two in the afternoon.

"Ma?"

"It's Manny." The radio was on behind him. "I'm at the house, Elijah. I came by about the fence. She's alive. They took her to Santa Rosa."

He didn't remember the drive. He remembered the gauge.

* * *

She was in a bed by a window with the rail up.

Her right hand lay on the blanket where somebody had put it. Her mouth went down on that side. Her eyes found him when he came in, and stayed on him, and he saw her know who he was. That was the last easy thing that happened that week.

"Ma."

She worked at it. He watched her go and get the word, like a woman reaching for something on a high shelf.

"Barry," she said.

"It's Elijah, Ma."

She looked at him as if he had said something a little beside the point.

"She's been saying that since they brought her up." A nurse was hanging a bag. "Is that your dad?"

"My dad was Joe."

"Brother?"

"There isn't any Barry."

The nurse nodded. It was an answer she got a lot.

The doctor was young and stood at the foot of the bed with a tablet. He did not sit down. A clot, a big one, on the left side. She had probably been on the kitchen floor since Monday night. There had been small ones before this, he thought, going back months. You could see them on the picture like old hail damage.

"Had she been forgetting things?"

"Yeah," Elijah said. "Since spring."

"That would be those." He scrolled. "Her pressure's been up a long time. Her sugar. This didn't start Monday. What does she eat?"

Elijah started to say she ate fine. She cooked for everybody.

"Ice cream," he said.

The doctor waited.

"Every night. One of the little tubs."

The doctor wrote it down. He had the next room to get to, and it showed.

He asked what she would get back. The doctor said a good many things, carefully, and the answer was in what he left out.

* * *

The pot was on the stove.

It was not in the rack. It was on the back burner with a wooden spoon in it and something burned to the bottom. Manny had smelled it from the yard. All his life Elijah had heard that pot go into that rack.

He stood in her kitchen and looked. There were three cartons of milk in the refrigerator and two of them were open. Her reading glasses were in the bread drawer. His phone number was on the refrigerator door in her handwriting, on a yellow note, and on a second yellow note right under it.

The freezer was the one thing in order. It was all Ben and Jerry's, the little tubs, a week of them, labels out. When he was small she let him have the lid.

That spring, in his old bedroom, he had heard the same question twice in one evening and let it go. Once she called him Joe, and laughed, and said <em>listen to me</em>. She was sweet about it. She was sweet about everything. He had seen it, and he had gone to Stockton to bolt down robot arms.

She was a woman who wrote the date on a battery before it went in the flashlight, and there was a flashlight in every room. Her mail was in the drawer with the rubber bands. It went back to March. Most of it had never been opened. The bank's name was in the corner window of about every third envelope. One had been opened, and folded up again, and put back in.

The last notice wasn't in the drawer. It was taped to the front door.

Nobody in that house had used the front door since his father's funeral. They used the back. Everybody did. The paper had been out there eighteen days with the sale date on it, facing the road.

He went out to the barn to look at the stove, because it was the one thing on the place that was his.

The barn was full.

There were pallets where the hay had been. White buckets, stacked five high, with the lids taped. Rice. Beans. Wheat she had no way to grind. Four blue water barrels, forty yards from a well. A generator in its box, and next to it the same generator in its box.

He stood there with his hand on a bucket. She had believed him. One person had, and it was her.

There was a card table by the door with a spiral notebook on it, squared to the corner. At the top of the first page she had written herself a note.

<em>Elijah is worried about the computers. Be nice about it.</em>

Under that she had copied out his list, one through five, in her good handwriting, the way she copied a recipe off the radio. Next to number four there was a question mark. <em>What deal? Ask him.</em>

It was on the next page too. And the next. <em>Ask him.</em>

She never had. <em>It's just tools, baby.</em> She had not followed one word of it. She knew her boy was frightened of something, and she knew what you did about a worry. The farm had taught her. You put something by.

The slips were in a coffee can. <em>Thank you for your monthly order.</em> The first was dated the week of the video. The last was three weeks old. Somebody had kept bringing it and she had kept signing, and that was where the payment on the place had gone since the new year.

The Fisher was in the back, under its tarp, behind the wheat.

He went in and sat down at the table. There was nobody to call. His father was ten years gone. There was Melody, in Miami, his father's daughter from before he met Elijah's mother. He had not heard her voice since the funeral, and he did not want to hear it now. He went down it twice, to be sure.

He was the call.

* * *

A woman from the hospital with a lanyard and a folder took him into a room with a box of tissues on the table. She was good at it. She had done it four times that day.

His mother could not go home. She would need two people to lift her, and somebody awake at night. The hospital would keep her until Friday. After that there was a stretch of rehab the insurance would pay for, and after that there was not.

She had two pages for him.

The first was a bed the county would pay for. It was in Stockton. He read the word and thought he had made it up. It was three to a room, two hours from anybody who had ever known her name.

The second was a place on the east side of town with a rose garden nobody sat in. It was ten minutes from the farm. The woman at the front desk knew her from church. It was a room of her own with a window. At the bottom of the page was a number for one month. The first time he read it he took it for a year.

"Does she have assets?" the woman said. "A home?"

"She's got the farm."

"Is there anybody else in the family who should be part of this?"

"No."

"Then it's you," she said, and she was sorry about it.

* * *

He asked her.

He sat by the bed with the two pages and he told her all of it, slowly, the way he had once tried to tell her about the machine. The bank. The door. Stockton. The room with the window. What it would take to pay for the room. He said the word <em>sell</em>, and then he made himself say what he would be selling.

"I need you to tell me, Ma. I need you to tell me what to do."

She looked at him the whole time. She understood that he was upset. He could see her understand it. Her good hand came up off the blanket and patted his.

Then she looked past him, at the door.

"Barry?" she said.

He waited. That was all there was.

* * *

He asked them, too.

He did it in the hospital garage with the phone propped on the wheel. He had been talking to that phone for a year. He had told it what was coming and how it was going, from hay bales and green rooms and the front seat of this truck. A number of people he didn't look at had listened. He had never asked them for one thing.

He did not do it well. He said his mother had had a stroke. He said what a room cost. He said, "I don't know how to ask this. I need help." He put up a link and went back upstairs.

The woman in Tulsa sent twenty dollars inside the minute. <em>For your mom.</em> The elevator man sent ten.

Then the rest of them got there.

<em>And there it is.</em>

<em>Why the fuck do you need help?</em>

<em>Says the world's ending. Wants your Venmo.</em>

<em>Fuck you. You're just asking for handouts.</em>

<em>Get a job. Oh wait.</em>

Somebody cut it down to the three words and ran his list under it, set to music. It went further in a day than the plain videos had gone all spring.

The other kind came in on the side, where strangers couldn't see. A guy he had played ball with. A girl who had booked Second Crop at the grange nine years running. <em>Always knew you thought you were better than us.</em> And Nate, who had stood two feet to his left for eighteen years. <em>Take the band's name off that page.</em>

He read every one. His mother had raised him to answer a letter. He couldn't answer these, and he couldn't stop reading them.

He took the link down inside the hour. He sent Tulsa her twenty back, and the elevator man his ten.

It didn't matter. The clip did what clips do. By morning the other mail was back, as if the spring had never happened. A man with a podcast would fly him out. Somebody's assistant wanted him on a stage in Austin in the fall. A lab he had never heard of asked for his rate. He didn't have a rate. He had been on their stages before, and he knew what it paid.

He stood in a hallway that smelled like hand sanitizer with all of it in one hand. In one window he was a beggar. In the next he was a get. Forty feet away his mother was asking a nurse for Barry. He could not make it fit in one head, and the head was his.

He didn't answer any of it.

* * *

After his father died, and after Melody stood up at the funeral lunch and asked what the place was worth, a lawyer in town had put the place in a trust. Elijah had signed a page at twenty-six and never thought about it again. He found it in the drawer under the bank's envelopes. It said that if she couldn't, he could.

The man from Sacramento came Monday, in a clean pickup, with a folder.

He was very sorry about Elijah's mother. He had the sale date and the amount owing and the taxes, which were also behind. He had a number for what a place like this brought on a courthouse step, and what would be left when the bank and the county were done, and how long that took. That last one was the number he let sit.

Or Elijah could sign this week. His outfit would pay off the bank and close in five days. There was a figure on the last page with a yellow tab beside it.

Elijah turned the hospital's second page over and did the division on the back.

"Who's the outfit?" he said.

The man told him a name with three letters and an LLC on the end. It was the same people who had bought the Hollis place. They liked the valley, he said.

A notary came out in a Camry and they did it on the kitchen table. Elijah signed where the yellow tabs were. On Friday, when it closed, the money would go into an account with her name on it and his name under hers, and the place with the rose garden would take the first of it that afternoon.

He did not keep any. He thought about it for the length of time it took the notary to find her stamp.

The buckets went with the place. He did not bring them up, and neither did the man.

* * *

On Wednesday he drove into town for a letter from her doctor. The title company wanted one before it would close.

He'd parked and was coming down the sidewalk when the door of the music store opened across the street, and Nate and Wes came out of it.

They had a case between them, one on each end, a long black hard case. It was a keyboard. Second Crop had never had a keyboard. They were laughing about something, and Wes was walking backward, and Elijah felt his whole chest lift. It was the first easy thing he'd felt since the fire escape. Eighteen years of load-ins. He knew exactly how Nate would set his end down.

He raised his hand.

Wes saw him first. Then Nate turned and saw him. It was a small street. They were maybe forty feet away, and all three of them were looking right at each other.

Wes looked away.

Nate looked down, at the case, like it needed his attention.

They kept walking. They went up the sidewalk to Nate's van, the same van, and got the back doors open, and slid the case in.

Elijah's hand was still up.

He stood there like that. He didn't know how long. Long enough for a woman with a stroller to go around him. He could not make it make sense. He ran it again and it came out the same: they saw him, and they knew him, and they went. He just stood on Main Street, and his best friends closed the doors of the van and drove off.

He put his hand down.

He got the letter. He walked back to the truck the other way, which took him past the Bear Flag.

There were two men smoking outside the Bear Flag. He knew one of them. Pruitt, who had the tire shop.

"Heard about your mom," Pruitt said. "That's rough. I mean that."

"Thanks."

Pruitt looked at his cigarette. "My wife was up there Sunday with the church. Says your mom had that video of yours on every night. Couldn't follow it and couldn't leave it alone." He flicked it into the gutter. "My sister-in-law quit her job over one of those. She's got two kids. Somebody ought to tell you."

He looked in, because he couldn't not.

David was behind the bar with a rag, wiping it down for the afternoon. He looked up. He saw Elijah through the glass, under the neon bear. He stopped wiping.

It lasted a second or two. Then David looked back down at the wood and went on with the rag, and he stayed where he was, behind the bar.

Elijah walked on to the truck.

* * *

David showed up Thursday morning with a hand truck in the back of his pickup.

Elijah hadn't called him. Nobody had.

"Heard they close Friday," David said.

"Yeah."

"Heard you've got a stove," David said.

"Yeah."

"Heard it's four hundred pounds."

"About."

"Well." David got the hand truck down. "I'm not doing it twice."

They went into the barn. David looked at the buckets, stacked five high, and at the two generators, and did not say one word about them. They pulled the tarp. It took the two of them and Manny and a pair of planks to walk the Fisher up into the bed of the Ranger. <em>Your side. Hold it. Okay, go.</em> David tied it down himself, a trucker's hitch on each corner, and yanked every line.

Then they were standing there.

"Where are you going to put it?" David said.

"I don't know yet."

David wiped his hands on his jeans. He looked at the stove and not at Elijah.

"You don't have to go anywhere, Mad. I've got shifts. Benny quit. Thursday through Sunday, it's decent money, you already know where everything is." He scratched the back of his neck. "You could just come back."

It was a good offer. Elijah knew it. He could see the whole thing, the stool on the other side, the bowling on TV, Ray with his club soda. It would've been so easy.

He could see the other thing too. Pruitt on the sidewalk. Everybody in that room knowing what he had signed.

"I can't, Dave."

"Yeah." David nodded a few times. "Yeah, I figured."

He put the hand truck back in his pickup. He looked at the porch a second. There was nobody on it. He lifted two fingers off the wheel going down the drive, and Elijah lifted his hand.

Manny stayed a minute after. He had not said ten words all morning. He said four through the window of his truck.

"Back room's empty, Elijah."

Elijah thanked him. He could not have slept in sight of that barn.

He took the stove, a bag of clothes, the notebooks, the manuscript and the pot. He did not take one bucket. He could not have said why.

He took the kitchen radio to her.

They had her in a chair by the window when he got there. He set the radio on the sill and found the station, which took no finding. It had been on that station since before he was born. She turned her head toward it.

He told her he had work, and a place, and that he'd be by Sunday. Two of those were not true. She patted his hand.

He left the key to the house on its kitchen table, on top of the mail.
