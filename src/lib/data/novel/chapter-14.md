# Chapter 14: Rain

He drove out the back way, behind his grandmother's place, because the back way didn't go past anybody he knew.

The madrones were on the uphill side, where they'd always been. The bark was coming off in long red curls. He pulled onto the dirt under the biggest one and turned the truck off. He had what was left of a quarter tank and no place on earth he was willing to be.

It got dark. He lay across the bench seat with his knees up and his jacket over him. It was the way he'd lain in the cannery lot, and he noticed that. Behind his head, through the glass, the Fisher sat in the bed with its door wired shut.

He went down the list. It was not the one in the notebook.

He'd been a farm kid, and he had sold the farm. He'd been a guitar player, and the band had bought a keyboard. He'd been a guy with a stool. He'd been a data cowboy, and then a face, and a face was worth exactly a heart from a woman with a cooling company. He had bolted two million dollars of robot arms to a floor and he was not on the list.

He was somebody's son. She had one word left, and it was another man's name.

He played the voicemail.

<em>Hi, baby. I watched your video.</em> The radio was on behind her. <em>You look thin. Is that Mom's stove?</em>

That was all of it. She had said those words to him the first time eleven months ago. They were the last whole sentences she had.

The phone lit up in his hand.

<em>Melody.</em>

It went through him the way it always had, his whole life. A spike, straight up from the stomach. He was six years old and he had taken the bigger piece.

<em>Oh my God. She's calling.</em>

He watched it ring. He thought about not taking it. He could let it go, the way he had let another call go on a Sunday.

He took it.

"Hello?"

"You sold the fucking farm?"

"Hello. Nice to hear from you too."

"Are you fucking insane?"

He closed his eyes. He breathed in, all the way.

"How dare you? How could you fucking do this? What's wrong with you? I always knew you were such a fucking piece of shit, but I can't believe you would do this."

"Melody. My mom had a stroke."

She paused for a second.

"I don't fucking care. You sold the fucking farm. How much money did you keep, you greedy little piece of shit?"

When he was six it had been <em>greedy little grub</em>. She had changed the one word.

"I didn't keep any, Melody."

"I don't believe you. That's a fucking lie."

"I didn't keep any. I don't have any money. I'm living in my fucking truck."

"Oh, are you doing fucking drugs?"

"I'm not doing any drugs. I don't know what I'm supposed to do, but I didn't keep any money. It went into a fund. It's helping her live. She's living in a facility."

"You put her in a fucking home? Oh my God, you are such a fucking piece of shit."

"Well, I don't see you around anywhere taking care of her. I don't see you fucking helping."

"She's not my fucking mother. I don't need to help her. But I was entitled to some of that fucking money from that fucking farm, you fucking asshole. How dare you? What the fuck?"

He could not have said why it was that minute and not some other one in thirty years. It was over. He was done.

"Oh, I'm sure you're having a lot of fucking fun in Miami," he said. "Living it up with all your fucking rich friends."

"Don't you talk about my fucking friends. And how dare you judge my lifestyle? You said you're living in your truck, so you're homeless now?" He could hear her breathing. "Do I need to come home?"

"You can do whatever you want."

He hung up.

It rang again before he had the phone down. He let it ring. It stopped, and it rang again, and he let that one ring too.

Then the texts started. They came in one on top of the other, faster than he could read them.

<em>Hey, stop,</em> he typed. <em>Don't do this. I don't appreciate it.</em>

They kept coming.

He did something then that he had never done in his life. He blocked her.

It took one thumb. It took more nerve than anything he had done in a year, and he did not know that. He thought it was the weakest thing he had ever done. He thought it was what a man does when he has lost.

The phone was quiet.

He laughed. It came out of him like a bark. Then it was not laughing.

"I hate this," he said.

It was just a thing to say. He said it to the dome light.

"I hate this." It was louder. "I hate this. I hate this."

Then something in him let go of the rope.

"This sucks! This is awful!" He hit the dash with the flat of his hand. He hit it again with his fist, and the glove box fell open, and he kicked it. "I didn't do anything! I told them the truth! I told them the truth and I was nice about it!"

He was sitting up now. He had the wheel in both hands and he was shaking it like it owed him money.

"I wish I never saw it! I wish I never typed a goddamn word! I wish this never happened!"

His voice cracked and went up into something he hadn't heard since he was a kid.

"I wish I was never born!"

He had never said that out loud in his life. It hung there in the cab. He didn't take it back.

He had not prayed since his father's funeral, and that time he had only moved his mouth. He didn't know who he was talking to. He put his head back and yelled it at the roof of the truck anyway.

"What do you want from me?"

The roof was gray headliner with a stain on it shaped like Ohio.

"What do you want? You took him! You took her and you left her sitting there! I sold it! I sold her house and she couldn't even tell me no!" He was crying now and it made him furious. "I asked her! I asked her and I'm asking you! Why did you show it to me? Why me? I'm nobody! I play bars! Why did you do this to me? What did I do? Tell me what I did!"

He waited.

He actually waited. He sat there with his chest going and his hands open on his knees, like a man who has asked a fair question and is owed an answer.

Nothing answered.

The engine ticked as it cooled. Up the hill a branch moved. That was all there was. There was no voice and no sign. No better idea came to him. Nobody was listening, or somebody was and had decided not to say.

Then something hit the roof. One tap, like a finger.

Then another one. Then a few on the windshield, fat ones that broke and ran down through the dust.

Then it came down all at once. It was the first rain since April, and it came down on the roof of the Ranger like gravel off a truck. It rang on the stove in the back. It was so loud he couldn't have heard an answer if there'd been one.

He lay back down.

He pulled the jacket up. He cried the way he had yelled, all the way, with his mouth open. Nobody was going to hear it over that. He cried until his ribs hurt, and then for a while after that he just lay there and leaked.

The rain didn't care. It kept on at the same rate. It was not for him and it was not against him. It fell on the truck and on the madrones, and on a roof down the hill that belonged to three letters and an LLC, and on a window on the east side of town where a radio was playing low.

He put his hand up and laid it flat against the headliner. It was cold, and it shook a little with the rain.

He listened. He didn't have anything left to say, to anybody. He listened to it on the roof and in the leaves and running in the ditch, and sometime in the night it put him under.
