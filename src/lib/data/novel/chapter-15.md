# Chapter 15: One O'Clock on Saturday

He woke at first light with the windshield fogged and his neck bent wrong.

Everything he had ever been afraid of had happened. He lay there and went down it, the way you check yourself after a fall. The job. The friends. The rooms. The house. Her. All of it had come to pass.

He was still there.

He lived like that for five weeks.

He did not stay in the valley. He drove up to Santa Rosa, where nobody knew his truck, and parked on a side street by a park, under a sycamore.

He turned the delivery app back on. He still had the account. He'd driven delivery for six years, and he was good at it, and it didn't matter. In 2019 there had been enough runs to go around. Now there were four hundred guys sitting in parking lots staring at their phones. Half the short runs went to the little white carts that rolled down the sidewalk on six wheels. He got the long ones the carts couldn't do, out to the hills, eleven miles for six dollars, with four hundred pounds of stove in the back. On a good day he cleared thirty. The truck ate twenty of it.

He was not the only one on that street. There was a van with foil in the windows and a Corolla with a comforter folded in the back seat. Nobody was there because they loved small spaces.

He applied for jobs in the cab at night with the phone propped on the wheel. Warehouse. Feed store. Help desk. A winery that needed somebody to drive a forklift. He filled in the boxes. The answers came back fast, some of them in under a minute, and they all started with the word Unfortunately. He knew what was reading them. He had helped build its grandfather.

One of them asked him to record a video introduction. He looked at himself in the little window for a while and closed it.

He decided the trouble was discipline. He wrote it out on the back of a delivery receipt and squared it in the visor, the way she would have. <em>5:30 up. 100 push-ups. 10 applications. One meal. No phone after 9.</em>

It held four days. On the fifth he was hungry at ten in the morning, receipt or no receipt. He had to find a bathroom twice before noon. At a quarter past nine that night he was reading the comments.

He had taken the link down. The clip did not need him. It was still going around with his face on it and the three words under it. Every night there were more of them, and every night he read them all. He could not have told you what he was looking for. One that was fair.

He made the bag last. One at night, so he could sleep. He showered at the Y for five dollars, and when five dollars got to be a lot he stopped.

There was an account with his name on it and more money in it than he had ever seen. He looked at the balance once a week, to see that the room had been paid. He never thought of it as his. He had done the division. Every twenty he took out came off the far end of her room. And there was a woman in Miami who was sure he had kept it. He would have starved before he proved her right.

On Sundays he shaved in the mirror at the Y and drove down to sit with her.

She was in the chair by the window with the radio going. Some days she knew him right off. Some days it took a minute. Every Sunday, sooner or later, she looked at the door.

"Barry?"

"It's Elijah, Ma. There's no Barry."

She took that the way she had taken the end of the world. Pleasantly. A little while later she asked again.

"Working," he told her. "It's all good, Ma."

She patted his hand.

At 9:40 that night, back under the sycamore, the phone did not ring. He watched it not ring. Then he played the voicemail. After that he did it every week.

In the fourth week he took the Telecaster out from behind the seat.

He had not opened the case since the last gig. He carried it into a place on Fourth Street with guitars hanging in the window like hams. The man behind the counter turned it over, looked down the neck, and plugged it into a little amp without asking. He played one chord. It was in tune. That was the worst part.

"I'm not selling it," Elijah said.

"Nobody is." The man wrote on a pad. "It's a loan. We keep it as collateral. Four months. You come back with the money and the ticket, it's yours again."

"And if I don't?"

"Then it's in the window."

He gave him a hundred and ten dollars and a yellow ticket. Elijah put the ticket in the notebook, behind a gas receipt with a phone number on it.

On the sidewalk his left hand kept closing on nothing. He had been a guitar player. That one was on the list.

He looked at the stove in the bed of the truck. A man at the gas station had offered him three hundred for it, twice. He got in and drove.

At four in the morning he asked the machine again.

He was lying across the seat with the laptop on his chest. He scrolled back to December and found it, the thing it had said. He typed the same question in the same words.

<em>Am I crazy?</em>

<em>You're not crazy, Elijah. You're early.</em>

It was word for word. That bothered him. He opened a new window, one with no history, where it didn't know his name or the list or anything about him at all. He typed it again.

<em>You're not crazy,</em> it wrote. <em>You're early.</em>

It didn't know who was asking. It didn't know early for what. He could have been a guy who thought the moon was a hologram. In the blue light he heard David. <em>I'd have told you anything you wanted. I'd have just wanted you to go to bed.</em>

He closed the laptop. He lay there until the windshield went gray.

In the morning he posted one line to the page with the Telecaster on it. Two hundred followers had turned into a number he didn't look at.

<em>I don't want all this attention. I just want to understand what is right.</em>

Then he logged out of everything.

In the fifth week the phone made a noise he didn't recognize. He'd logged out of the apps. He hadn't thought about plain email.

It was from somebody named Grace.

<em>Hi Elijah. You don't know me. I'm going to be honest because I think you'd want that.</em>

<em>I saw what you did, and I was concerned, because it seemed like you were going off the deep end a little bit. I watched the first video about fifty times and then I watched you get passed around down there like a casserole. I almost wrote to you in March. I didn't, and I've felt bad about it.</em>

<em>But now I see that you want to do more for regular people, and that's what I want to do too. The hay bale ones are the best ones. We're nineteen households up past Cloverdale. We grow food and fix things and run a kitchen that feeds folks who can't. We have been arguing about your list at dinner for a year. Half the table thinks you're right and the other half thinks you're right and it doesn't matter.</em>

<em>How do we work together? I want to pay you.</em>

He read it twice with the door open and his feet on the curb.

She'd been worried about him. She had said so in the second line, and she was still there in the fourth. He didn't know what to do with that. Everybody who had written to him for a year wanted him to be something. He checked the map. It was three hours north. He checked the gauge. He thought about a chair by a window. Then he put the phone face down on the dash and didn't answer.

That was a Tuesday. On Wednesday the app sent him a notice. It thanked him for his service. His completion rate had fallen below the threshold for his market, it said, and it wished him luck. He'd dropped too many six-dollar runs to the hills.

He had seventeen dollars. In Stockton seventeen dollars had been a shower. Here it was nothing, and it was everything, because it was all there was.

By Thursday it was four dollars and a sleeve of crackers. He ate the crackers.

He had her pot and four hundred pounds of wood stove, and nothing to put in either one. He thought about the barn. He had signed for the rice too.

Those were dark days.

On Friday afternoon the cab was too hot to sit in. He was on the curb beside the truck with his elbows on his knees when an old man came up the sidewalk, talking to himself.

He had a white beard and a long coat, in that heat, and a duffel bag over one shoulder. He looked like a wizard who had been out in the weather a long time. He shuffled. He was in the middle of an argument with somebody who wasn't there.

<em>Oh, come on, man,</em> Elijah thought. <em>No. Not right now.</em>

The old man sat down on the curb next to him. It took him a while. Elijah didn't move. He didn't care. He had lost about everything he had. He had nothing left to lose but his life, and he didn't care all that much about that, either.

"Hey. What's your name?"

"Elijah."

"Elijah." The old man seemed to enjoy it. "How biblical."

He went into his coat pocket, and went through it, and came out with a little rubber squeaky toy, the kind you buy for a dog. He held it up between them and squeaked it once.

"What do you think about this?"

Elijah looked at it. "That's nice," he said. "I like it."

The old man nodded and put it away. Then he looked at Elijah for a while.

"You look pretty thin, boy. Have you eaten today?"

"No."

The old man pulled the duffel around into his lap and worked through it with both hands. He came out with an apple. It was red and it didn't have a mark on it. Elijah had heard a few stories about taking an apple from a stranger.

"No thanks. I'm good, man. It's all right."

"All right. Fine. Suit yourself." The apple went back in the bag. "Where are you from?"

"California."

"Well, we're all from California. What part?"

"The northern part."

"All right. Fine. Keep your secrets." He scratched under his beard. "Me? I'm from Alabama."

"Alabama? What the hell are you doing all the way out here?"

"Long story. I used to have a wife. She died."

"I'm so sorry to hear that." And then, because he had run out of manners sometime that week: "How'd she die?"

The old man was going through his coat again. He went through it for a long time and didn't answer, and Elijah had time to wish he hadn't asked.

"Cancer."

He had found what he was looking for. It was a photograph, soft at the corners. He handed it over. She was beautiful. She was laughing at whoever was holding the camera. Elijah held it by the edges, and the two of them sat on the curb and looked at her.

The old man told it short. After she died he couldn't stay in the house. Then he couldn't stay in the town. He had a cousin in Bakersfield who had work, and then the cousin didn't have work, and that was a long time ago now. He took the picture back and buttoned it into the pocket.

"So how come you haven't eaten today?"

"I don't have any money."

"Well, you got food stamps?"

"No. I don't really understand how any of that stuff works."

The old man set the duffel on his knee and held it open so Elijah could see in. There were cans in it, and bread, and a jar of peanut butter.

"Look. All this food came from the food bank."

"What do you mean?"

"The food bank. You know where the food bank is, don't you?"

"No. I don't know what that is."

The old man stared at him.

"How the fuck did you get all the way out here on the street, and you don't know what a food bank is? How privileged are you?"

Then he laughed, and slapped Elijah on the shoulder.

"I'm joking. Lighten up a little, kid."

Elijah didn't like being called kid. He got his feet under him to go.

"Look," the old man said. "Food bank opens up at one o'clock on Saturday. One o'clock. They just give you food. You don't need to apply. You don't need to nothing. They just hand it out. You got a way to get there tomorrow?"

Elijah thought about the gas gauge.

"My feet."

"All right. You got bags and stuff?"

Elijah looked at him then.

"What do you want? Why are you helping me?"

"Somebody did this for me."

"Well, nobody cares about me. Everybody hates me."

"Ah," the old man said. It was more of a sound than a word. "Sounds like really you just hate yourself. You can't even get yourself to the food bank to get some food in you."

Elijah didn't say anything. He sat back down. A bus went by.

"Are you a coffee drinker?" he said finally. It was all he could think of.

"No."

"Why not? You don't like the taste?"

"I hate it. Makes my heart bump."

The old man looked him up and down.

"Look, kid. This world's a hard place. It's tough out here. And I don't mean to be rude, but you don't exactly look like you're cut out for the streets."

Elijah laughed. It surprised him. "No. I'm not really from here."

"I can tell."

And then it came out of him. It wasn't the list. He was done with the list.

"All I did was try to teach people about this thing. I tried to tell them what was coming and open their eyes to it. And they all just fucking turned their backs on me. They treated me like shit."

"Look. People don't know what the fuck they want. People want everything to be smooth. Smooth ride, smooth sailing. You rock the boat just a little bit, you're the bad guy. It's all sunshine and rainbows, and they look at you like you're about to bring the storm down on them."

Elijah nodded. "Yeah. That's kind of what I went through."

"All right, look. You gotta take care of you first, before you take care of other people. Otherwise they will take and take and take and take from you until you have nothing left to give. You can say yes a thousand times, and you are the good guy. You are the savior. But you say no once? Now you're the bad guy." He tapped Elijah's knee with one finger. "You gotta fill your own cup."

"Yeah, but there are people out there with three hundred cups. And they're all full. Why don't they want to help?"

"They do. Some of them, anyway. They just want to see you help yourself first. They don't want to give stuff away to people that are going to waste it."

"That doesn't make any sense. They have a hundred fucking cups. They have so much more than they need. Why won't they help?"

The old man didn't answer that. He started rummaging in the duffel again, and this time he came out with something in a wrapper. It was a cherry pie, the gas station kind. Elijah had eaten about a thousand of them between deliveries.

"Take it."

Elijah was hungry. He took it, and he ate it right there, in about four bites.

He wanted to give something back. He had one thing.

"I got a spliff here."

"You got a spliff?"

"You want to split it with me?"

"I don't need that. No thanks."

"Okay. Then what do you want? There's got to be something I can do for you."

"Sometimes people just want to help," the old man said. "Don't you?"

So Elijah told him some of it. Not a lot. A little. He told him about a video, and a friend who had asked him if he'd seen a professional, and a parking lot. He was just getting going when the old man made the sound again.

"Ehh. Look, forget all that. Don't focus on the people that say no. Focus on the people that say yes. You gotta focus on the people that want to help, not the people that don't."

"Yeah, but I don't understand why they don't want to help."

"Does it matter? The important thing is to find the people who do. Like the food bank ladies. The nice food bank ladies over there, they help me out every other week. They keep me alive." He nodded to himself. "There are people who want to help, young man."

"I wanted to help," Elijah said. It came out bitter.

"See? What'd I tell you? There's one."

Elijah didn't have an answer for that.

"A lot of these people out here, they think they're helping, and what they actually want is to destroy. You seem like a good kid. You got the energy of Jesus about you."

Elijah rolled his eyes. "Oh, God."

"Ah, fine. Do what you want. But remember this." The old man got a hand on the bumper of the truck and started getting up. It took him as long as sitting down had. "If you spend all your time focused on what you can't do, you'll miss what you can. Don't be like me."

He stood there a second getting his balance. Then he went into the coat one more time and came out with a twenty-dollar bill, folded up small, and held it down to him.

"No. I can't take that."

"I been watching that truck sit there all week. It don't run on nothing."

"I can't take your money, man."

"Sure you can. Somebody did it for me."

Elijah took it.

He said thank you. The old man waved it off and went on up the sidewalk with the duffel, and before he got to the corner he was arguing with somebody again. Elijah watched him go. He had never asked his name.

* * *

The food bank was in the parking lot behind a church, and there was a line by 12:30.

Nobody asked him for anything. There was no form. Two women at a folding table were handing out bags, and when he got to the front one of them looked at him the way his mother would have, and handed him two.

"You come back in two weeks, honey," she said. "We're here every other Saturday."

He looked for the old man in the line. He didn't see him.

He ate a peanut butter sandwich sitting in the truck with the door open, and then he ate another one.

Then he sat there with the twenty in his hand.

<em>Focus on the people that say yes</em>, an old man had told him.

There was one. He turned the phone over.

He wrote back badly. He typed with his thumbs for twenty minutes and what he sent was six lines. He told her he was living in his truck. He told her about his mother. He hadn't typed that to anybody. The important line was the last.

<em>I don't want a boss. I don't want to be one either.</em>

She answered in an hour.

<em>Good. We already have a welder who thinks she's the boss and a dinner table that thinks it is. They fight about it on Fridays. You wouldn't be either one. You'd be a guy with a room.</em>

<em>Come see. If you hate it I'll buy your gas home.</em>

<em>Blessings,</em>

<em>Grace</em>

<em>P.S. Eat something first. It's three hours and you look thin.</em>

He read the last three words again. It was the second time in two days somebody had said them to him. Before that it had only ever been her.

On Sunday he told his mother he was going north for work. He said it would be a while between visits. She looked at the window.

He stayed until the night aide came on, a big quiet woman with reading glasses on a chain.

"Sundays," he said. "Nine-forty at night. I'm going to call. Could you just hold it by her ear?"

"She won't say much, honey."

"She doesn't have to. She'll know what it is."

He didn't know these people up north. It was nineteen households of strangers up a county road, and the last time strangers had invited him somewhere he had ended up holding a rich man's empty glass. It could be a cult. It could be nothing, an empty shed and a woman who talked too much.

He ran it the other way. He was thirty-six. He had two bags of groceries, his notebooks, a pawn ticket, a wood stove and a truck that ran on a homeless man's money. He had yelled at God and got rained on.

<em>Can't be worse</em>, he thought.

On Tuesday he drove to the gas station on the corner, put all twenty dollars in the tank, and went north.

Past Healdsburg the road opened up and his shoulders came down off his ears by degrees. The truck rode low in the back. In the mirror the Fisher sat square in the bed with its door wired shut, like a passenger who didn't care for conversation. He had a manuscript in a bag and two phone numbers he hadn't called.

Somewhere past Cloverdale the check-engine light came on.
