# Chapter 2: Terms of Service

They announced the future on a Thursday morning, with pastries.

Claypot's quarterly all-hands filled the fourth-floor commons: two hundred folding chairs, a projection screen the size of a barn door, catering trays sweating under heat lamps along the back wall.

Elijah came early and took a seat in the last row with his coffee and the notebook. After the Bear Flag, and after the tower that had cooked itself to a hard shutdown in his living room, he had made himself one promise. He was done being surprised. He would write things down before they happened.

The Chief Product Officer walked out to real applause, company fleece over a dress shirt, clicker in hand like a detonator he'd been waiting years to press.

The first slide said COMPANION in the company typeface, warm and rounded, the way you'd letter a children's hospital.

"For twenty years," the CPO said, "our customers have trusted us with the greatest creative corpus ever assembled. Every portfolio. Every layer file. Every draft of every logo you've ever seen on a shelf." He let that hang, smiling. "Starting next quarter, that trust becomes their superpower. Companion is a generative partner trained on the best work humanity has ever made… and it lives inside every subscription tier."

The room applauded. The heat lamps ticked. Elijah uncapped his pen.

<em>Stage 1,</em> he wrote. <em>The Cash Grab.</em>

He'd been sketching the stages for weeks by then, late nights at the kitchen table, trying to map where this went once you stopped pretending it would politely stop. Watching the roadmap unfurl slide by slide was like watching someone else read his notes aloud with a marketing budget. Generative credits, metered by the render. A premium tier. An enterprise tier. A sovereignty tier, for studios that wanted their outputs walled off from everyone else's.

The closest thing to magic his species had ever built, and here it was on a slide with a coin slot bolted to the side, price points in forty-point type.

Then the architecture slide came up, and Elijah stopped writing.

He knew the diagram. He had drawn an uglier version of it fourteen months ago on a whiteboard two floors down. The pipeline that walked through customer libraries and turned two decades of human portfolios into clean, labeled rows a machine could learn from. His first real project at Claypot. His name was in those commits. He'd stayed up nights making it faster.

"The data team did heroic work here," the CPO said, to the room.

And the first thing Elijah felt was pride. A hot flush of it, half a second long, his hand actually starting to rise off his knee before the rest of him caught up. Then it went sour all at once.

The realtors had watched a demonstration. This was the factory, and he had helped build it.

"And a thank-you to Aaron," the CPO said, "who couldn't be with us today, and who believed in this before any of us did."

Polite applause for Aaron. Elijah had never met an Aaron at Claypot. Neither, from the look of the room, had anyone else.

At Q&amp;A, a woman from the illustration tools team stood up. Elijah didn't know her name. Her voice was steady, and you could hear how hard she was working to keep it that way.

"The corpus," she said. "It's our users' portfolios. Working artists. When Companion ships and their clients stop hiring them because the model does their style for nine dollars a month… what do they get?"

"Great question," the CPO said, in the tone that means the opposite. "Value flows back to creators through productivity gains. And of course, everything we've done is covered by terms of service." A beat. "We see this as democratizing creativity."

The applause that followed was thinner, but it came. The woman sat down. The screen changed to the revenue slide, the hockey stick, the line going up and to the right.

The announcement had gone out to the public an hour earlier. Elijah's phone buzzed against his leg. It was Wes.

<em>is my stuff in that thing</em>

Wes had made every Second Crop flyer since 2006 on Claypot software, and for the last four years he'd paid for it. Gig posters, a logo for his cousin's taqueria, the album art for a record that sold ninety copies. All of it sitting in a library with his name on it.

Elijah typed <em>I don't know.</em> He deleted it. He typed <em>Probably.</em> He deleted that. He typed <em>Yes and I built the part that</em> and deleted that one fast.

<em>Let me look into it,</em> he sent.

He never did.

* * *

Afterward he looked for the woman from illustration tools. He wanted to say something to her. He didn't know what. She had already gone back to her desk, or out to her car.

He found Devendra at the good coffee machine instead, watching the drip like it owed him money. The sticky note was still on the side of it.

"Good quarter," Devendra said. "This is what a moat looks like, if you're wondering. This is the part where we win."

"You know what they called it," Elijah said, "when the commons got fenced?"

Devendra's eyebrow went up.

"Enclosure. People used to share that land. Then somebody put up hedges, and Parliament made it legal. They called it improvement."

He nodded at the screen still glowing through the commons doorway.

"The land didn't stop producing. It just stopped being theirs. All you have to build is the fence."

For the first time in two years, Devendra didn't have an answer ready. He looked at the doorway, and at his cup, and said, finally, "The terms of service…"

"That's the hedge," Elijah said. "I helped plant it."

* * *

That night he sat at the kitchen table with the notebook and did what he'd promised. He wrote it down before it happened.

<em>1. The Cash Grab. They put a coin slot on it. (Today.)</em>

<em>2. The Panic and the Plug. It gets loose. Somebody important starts losing money and somebody reaches for the off switch.</em>

<em>3. The Adults Step In. The people with pagers turn it back on.</em>

<em>4. The End of the Deal. You work, you eat. That's been the deal forever. It stops being the deal and nobody has written the next one.</em>

<em>5. The Backlash. People take it out on the machines. Then on each other.</em>

He dated the page and put his initials next to the date, so that later he couldn't pretend he hadn't seen it coming.

Then he wrote one more line.

<em>What happens to people when the work is no longer theirs, but the bills still are?</em>

Under that, small, in the corner: <em>Thanksgiving 2027. Loser cooks.</em>

His phone lay face up next to the notebook. Wes's text was still on the screen, and under it his own.
