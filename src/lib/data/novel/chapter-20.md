# Chapter 20: What a Tomato Costs

Lou put him on drip tape in March, and it just about killed him.

Elijah thought he knew farm work. He'd grown up on a farm. He had said so to Marta on his very first day, like it was a diploma. But what he'd actually done on his mother's place for the last twenty years was fix gate latches and eat, and Lou figured that out in about four minutes, watching him try to unroll a quarter mile of plastic tubing in a crosswind. Lou laughed so hard he had to sit down on a bucket.

"College," he said, wiping his eyes. "You look like a man trying to put pants on an octopus."

Lou was sixty-one years old. He was, as far as Elijah could tell, the only person at the co-op who had ever made a living farming, and then lost it. He'd grown pears outside Ukiah on ground his grandfather cleared. Then the packing house that bought everybody's pears got bought by a bigger one, and after that there was one buyer in the whole valley, and the one buyer named the price, and the price was less than a pear cost to grow. Lou told that story in about three sentences, the way you'd tell somebody your blood type. Now he grew vegetables for a hundred and forty households on forty acres that weren't his, and he seemed pretty happy about it.

Elijah had liked him from the start. He liked him the way you like a big dog that leans on you.

They laid tape down thirty beds that week, on their knees, in the mud. Elijah's back went out on the second day and came back on the fourth as somebody else's back. His hands cracked open at the knuckles. He was thirty-seven years old and he had never been so tired in his life, and he had once bolted two million dollars of robot arms to a floor in Stockton in August.

At noon they sat on the tailgate of Lou's truck and ate Grace's sandwiches, and Lou got out a pencil and turned over a seed box.

"Okay. Pop quiz. What's a tomato cost?"

"At the store? I don't know. A dollar?"

"Not to buy. To make."

Elijah had no idea. He was a little ashamed of that. He had a notebook with the wattage of his cable box in it.

So Lou showed him. He wrote it down the side of the box in a column. The seed, the starts, the water, the tape they'd just laid, the diesel, the boxes, the truck. Then he drew a line under all that and wrote HANDS, and the number next to HANDS was bigger than everything above it added up.

"That's the whole thing right there," Lou said, and tapped it. "That's farming. Everything else is a rounding error. It's hands. It's somebody bent over at six in the morning."

"So what do we sell it for?"

"Less than that!" Lou was delighted. "And we give a third of them away! It's the dumbest business in America. Don't tell Reuben, he thinks we're a going concern."

"Then how does it work?"

"It doesn't work, man. It's never worked. It works because nine people here don't get paid, and Grace is some kind of saint, and my wife taught fourth grade for thirty-one years and left me her pension." He took a bite and talked around it. "Every farm you ever saw is like that. There's a wife with a job in town, or a dead grandpa who paid off the ground, or twelve guys from Michoacán nobody's paying enough. Somebody is always eating the difference. You grew up on one. Who ate it at your place?"

Elijah thought about his mother, and the pot she never let anyone else wash.

"My mom," he said.

"There you go."

It was the best explanation of anything he'd gotten since he came north, and it had taken about ninety seconds and a seed box. Lou was like that. He asked you about three questions first, to find out where you were standing, and then he told you one thing.

Of course Elijah couldn't leave it alone. He was looking at the word HANDS.

"A machine could lay that tape," he said.

"Sure it could. They make a thing you pull behind the tractor. We've got one."

"Then why are we on our knees?"

"Because the tractor's broke and Curtis is in a mood." Lou balled up his sandwich paper. "I'd let a robot do every bit of this. I mean that. I've got one knee that's mine and one that's titanium, and the titanium one's the good one."

"You can't mean that."

That was Wren, who led the Sunday circle and made the candles. She had come down the row with a thermos of something that smelled like a wet lawn, and she had her hand flat on her chest.

"There's a relationship," Wren said. "Between the hand and the plant. The food knows who grew it. You can't give that to a machine, Lou. You can't automate reverence."

"Wren, honey," Lou said, "I have been in a relationship with plants for fifty years, and not one of them has ever called me back."

Elijah laughed and got mustard up his nose. Wren laughed too, a little, in spite of herself. She still looked worried, though. She looked like somebody had proposed paving the creek.

"I just think some things are meant to be done by people," she said.

"I don't think 'meant to' has anything to do with it," Elijah said, and wiped his nose. He could feel himself going, and he went. "It's coming either way. That's what I keep trying to tell everybody. Nobody gets a vote on this. It's like weather."

Lou looked at him. He had very pale eyes in all that sunburn.

"Question," Lou said. "It's coming either way?"

"Yeah."

"So it's already decided. The whole thing."

"Pretty much. I mean, I've got it written down."

"Okay. Then what are you doing on your knees in my tomatoes?"

Elijah opened his mouth, and nothing came out. He actually did not know. He had come north to do something about it. He also believed nobody could do anything about it. He had never put those two facts next to each other.

Lou grinned and slid down off the tailgate. His good knee clicked.

"Think about it," he said. "Get the other end of that roll."

That night Elijah wrote two lines in the notebook, under the names.

<em>What a tomato costs: hands. Somebody's always eating the difference.</em>

<em>Lou: if it's already decided, what are you doing on your knees?</em>
