# Chapter 21: Bait

The co-op needed money the way every real thing needs money.

Not dramatically. Just constantly. In Elijah's first spring there it was Denny, of all people, who said the obvious thing out loud at a Friday dinner: "People pay for what we know. We give it away to anyone who walks into the shed. Why does the walking matter?"

So they decided to put the co-op on the internet. Elijah had been the internet once, for one winter, and a billionaire had explained attention to him on a fire escape. He volunteered to run it.

He did everything correctly. That was the problem. He studied what performed. The pacing, the thumbnails with the circled arrows, the seven-second hook, the open loops ("what we found in the soil SHOCKED us, but first").

He wrote Denny a script for the greenhouse-controller story that was, by every metric he knew how to measure, optimal. Denny read four lines of it on camera, stopped, and looked over the phone at Elijah like a man being asked to wear someone else's dentures.

"Nobody talks like this," Denny said.

"The algorithm," Elijah began.

"I'm not talking to the algorithm. I'm talking to a guy on his couch who just got the letter I got in 2019." He put the script face-down on the workbench. "Point the phone. I'm just going to say what happened."

He told it in one take, hands in frame the whole time because he talked with them, the plasma table ticking as it cooled somewhere behind him. What happened was eleven years in a warehouse, the arms coming in, the county's folder, the second ladder, the second folder.

And then the part Elijah hadn't scripted because Elijah didn't know it yet: what it was like, at fifty-one, to bolt together a machine that feeds people and feel the weight of a tool in your hand instead of a lanyard around your neck. "Everybody's asking what the machines are going to do to us," Denny said to the phone, at the end. "I spent years as the answer to that question, and it nearly killed me. So we built something. Here's ours. Come see it. Bring your kid."

Elijah posted two versions, because he was still a data scientist in his soul and you always run the A/B. One was Denny's take the way he gave it. The other was Elijah's cut of the same take, with the hook up front.

The optimized cut landed in a feed that already felt mostly machine-made to him, fake scripts read by fake voices over fake pictures. It looked like everything around it.

Forty-one views. He watched it between the other videos in his feed and heard the resemblance: the same urgent pauses, the same little promises withheld until after the next cut. He had spent two weeks trying to make a human being sound like a format.

Denny's single take, shot vertical with a thumb over the mic for the first three seconds, went slowly for a day. Then people started sending it to each other.

The comments read like testimony. Forklift drivers, paralegals, a woman who'd trained her own replacement twice. Several wrote that a friend had sent the link. One asked where to get the controller's repair card.

People wrote to the co-op's inbox too, and the inbox was Grace's. She answered every one of them herself, at the table in the kitchen trailer, with a cup of tea going cold at her elbow. He watched her do forty in a night.

"You answer everybody," he said.

"Somebody has to."

He got a second chair and took the ones about the repair card.

The next morning Elijah watched the numbers climb for a while, and then, Sal's rule, closed the laptop and went to find Denny, who was in the shed fitting lids.

"It's working," Elijah said. "The real one. Not mine."

"Course not yours." Denny didn't look up from the bench. "You tried to make bait." He fitted another lid. "I put out food."

They made it official at the next Friday dinner. The channel was Denny's. Elijah ran the pipes: the posting, the backups, the links to the build notes riding under every video like cargo under a truck.

The voice and the face and the say-so were Denny Osei's. The co-op wrote down how the money would be shared and how members could keep their lives out of the footage. Denny could say no to the camera any day he wanted.

That evening Denny recorded a short correction to the repair card. The first version had named the wrong terminal. No hook, no music, no suspense. He pointed to the screw and said which one he meant. Elijah sent the corrected card directly to every grower who'd received the first version.
