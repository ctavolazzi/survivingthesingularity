# Chapter 23: Forty Acres

The first letter was addressed to OWNER/OCCUPANT.

Irene Calder read it on her porch and threw it away, which is also what she did with the second one. The third letter came with a young man attached.

Everything the co-op had built stood on Calder ground. The shed had been Walt Calder's packing shed. The beds ran where his hay had run. The lease was month to month and had been since Walt died. Irene liked Marta and had never raised the rent, and nobody had wanted to disturb an arrangement that got worked out over pie. The young man on the porch offered twenty-three percent over what any forty acres nearby had sold for, all cash, a fourteen-day close, and a flyer with a sunset on it. Irene told him no. Irene's son Dale, in Phoenix, said, "Mom."

Elijah did what he always did when he was frightened. He pulled data. The county recorder's index was public, ancient, and slow, so he was polite to it, scraping at night, and then he did two neighboring counties for context. The pattern assembled itself the way patterns do, all at once, on the third evening.

Eleven different company names, one registered agent, a mail-forwarding address in Sacramento. Fifty-some parcels in two years, always the same shape: an estate or a widow, a cash offer, a fast close. Then the parcel goes quiet inside a shell company for a year or two and reappears stitched into a block big enough to matter, leased to whoever needs flat land, water rights, and no neighbors with opinions.

Reuben, reading over his shoulder, reminded him that a registered agent can represent a hundred unrelated clients. Elijah colored the uncertain links a different shade and kept going.

He knew one of the eleven names already. It took him until the fourth evening to go out to the truck. His own closing papers were in the glove box, under Curtis's hose, where he had put them the day he signed. Three letters and an LLC. Same agent. Three hours apart, the same signature. He sat in the cab with the dome light on. It had not stopped at the Hollis place. He had sold them her kitchen. He circled the link for Reuben to check, and did not tell Reuben how he came to have it.

He built a map. He built, God help him, a dashboard, and it was beautiful. He presented it at Friday dinner with a laser pointer. This time he heard himself doing it and grinned in the middle of a sentence, and the room grinned back.

But the dashboard did its one real job. In the parcels he could trace, the time from first letter to recorded deed ran about nine weeks, and Irene had gotten her first letter five weeks ago. The data couldn't change that. It could only tell them how little time they had.

"It's not a data problem," Reuben said, already dealing folders around the table like cards. "It's a people problem. Fortunately," and here he almost smiled, "I have been waiting my entire adult life for this exact fight. My grandfather lost sixty acres to a signature he couldn't read."

What he dealt out was a community land trust. The trust would own the ground, and it could never sell to a string of companies with one mailing address. Irene would stay in her house for the rest of her life and get a check every month. The co-op would get a lease long enough to plant trees on. Reuben had also brought the less thrilling pages: taxes, insurance, and what happened if they ever missed a payment.

The down payment would take the channel's first real surplus and the greenhouse-controller money. Future payments would have to come from future work. Denny looked from one column to the other. He tapped the second column. "And then we have to keep the promise."

"Every month," Reuben said.

Dale attended the kitchen-table meeting by speakerphone, from Phoenix, and he was not wrong by his own lights. "Their number is higher, Mom. That's just math."

"It's a bigger number," Reuben said to the phone where it lay on the oilcloth, next to the coffee and the folders. "There's a machine that's been running since the fifteen hundreds, Dale. It turns commons into fences. Every century it gets a new name. Enclosure. Consolidation. Urban renewal. This week it's a string of companies with one mailing address and a flyer with a sunset on it. It runs in one direction, and it has never run in your mother's favor. What's on this table is that machine in reverse." He let that sit. "Your mother gets to take the bigger number seriously. She also gets to decide what she wants to keep. It's her porch, Dale. It's her choice. That's why we brought the folders."

Lou had come along and stood by the door with his hat in his hands. He had lost his pears outside Ukiah to one buyer and one price. He hadn't said a word all afternoon.

There was a long quiet on the line, the box fan working, Walt's cap on its peg by the door. Irene had listened to all of it with both hands around her cup, and what she finally asked was not about the resale formula.

"Marta. Walt's tractor, back when. What did you charge him?"

"Sixty," Marta said. "It was the fuel line, not the pump. Dealer wanted nine hundred."

"And it still runs," Irene said, to the phone, to Dale, to the table generally, in the tone of a woman entering evidence. Then she drew the folders toward her. "Leave these. I want my questions written down before anybody signs."

By the door, Lou put his hat back on.

Grace went over the next afternoon and sat on the porch with her for four hours. She didn't bring a folder. She brought a pie she hadn't made and said so. Irene Calder did not want to leave her house. Elijah drove by twice on errands he'd invented. The second time they were both laughing, and Grace had her hand on Irene's arm.

The review took longer than Elijah wanted. Irene asked questions he hadn't thought to put in the dashboard. So did Dale. By the time they agreed, several pages had changed. Reuben carried the completed documents to the county himself, and Doreen at the recorder's window stamped them at 11:04 on a Tuesday morning. The letters from Sacramento kept coming for a month. Irene brought one to dinner and left it unopened beside the salt.

The dashboard had been right about the pattern. It had not closed the deal.

Elijah wrote the number in the notebook anyway, under Sal's rule, where the important numbers lived.
