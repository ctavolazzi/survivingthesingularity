# Chapter 25: Gradually

Every controller the co-op ever shipped had parts from Kowalski's in it. Whenever anybody in the shed said <em>from town,</em> the counter they meant was Frank's. It was the third bay off the square, with wood worn pale where four generations of forearms had rested on it and a bell over the door that Frank's grandfather had hung. Frank could tell you the torque spec for a head bolt from memory, and he would carry a two-dollar part out to your truck like it was an appliance.

Elijah watched the store die for nine months before it died, and said nothing.

He hadn't meant to be watching. He kept the co-op's purchasing ledger, and the ledger noticed before he did. Substitution emails, out-of-stocks on things that had never been out of stock, the fastener aisle developing gaps that stayed gaps.

He pulled the thread, because he could never leave one alone. Frank's regional distributor had been bought by a logistics platform out of Memphis, the kind with a two-letter name and no phone number. The new owner had raised minimum orders past what a single-counter store could float and rerouted the trucks to its own warehouses. It was letting the small end of its customer list wither on schedule.

The chart Elijah built showed the co-op's own orders drifting away from the square, to salvage yards and online, one substitution at a time.

The hours sign got re-taped twice. Tyler's shifts at the register went from five days to three, and then to Saturdays, and then Frank told him in the fastener aisle that he couldn't keep him. Tyler came out to the shed the next morning like it was any other day.

He said nothing, and he had his reasons. He'd been the man with the printout once, at a coffee machine on the fourth floor, and a calm senior colleague had smiled at him the way you smile at a child. He'd spent three years learning what it costs to say the thing early, and the lesson he'd accidentally taken was: don't.

And besides, what was the sentence? <em>Frank, my chart says you're dying?</em> Frank knew his own store. Surely Frank knew.

The paper on the door, when it came, was laminated. Which meant Frank had known long enough to laminate it.

The same week, Elijah got an email with the subject line "checking in," from Devendra. Claypot had cut the whole division. The Companion team and every human who had been augmented, not replaced, gone in one memo.

On the phone Devendra kept starting a sentence, pausing, and starting somewhere else. "You'll appreciate this," Devendra said, with a laugh that wasn't one. "They kept the very good autocomplete." And then, quietly, the real question: "At the coffee machine. You knew. How?"

"I didn't know," Elijah said. He almost followed it with the arithmetic, with you told me I'd calibrate, with all the sentences that would turn another man's lost job into his own vindication. He looked at Frank's closing notice on the screen and let them pass. "I was frightened. I still am sometimes. What do you need this week?"

Devendra didn't answer immediately. Then he asked about the place to stay that Elijah had mentioned once, and whether he could bring the kids.

"Bring them," Elijah said.

"I owe you a dinner."

"Not till Thanksgiving. You've got two years."

Devendra laughed, short, a real one. He said he'd think about it.

He thought about it for a year. He stayed in his house in San Jose with the news on, and for most of that year he was Gary. Elijah called him on Sundays, after his mother. Some Sundays he picked up.

Elijah told Grace that night, at the kitchen trailer. All of it, the ledger and the nine months. She stopped stirring.

"You knew since spring and you didn't knock?"

"I didn't want to be the crackpot again."

"So you let him go through it by himself." She pointed the spoon at him. "You knock, Elijah. He gets to slam it in your face. That part's his. But you knock."

She went back to the pot. He thought about a gate with a strip of orange tape on it, and didn't argue.

In the morning he made the harder visit, the two-mile one. He drove to the square with his chart, parked, sat in the truck long enough for the windows to fog, and walked in through the bell.

Frank was taping boxes. Elijah put the chart on the worn wood and confessed to it. Nine months, the distributor, the platform, the curve, all of it.

Frank looked at the chart for a while. "Fella in Hemingway gets asked how he went bankrupt," he said, taping another box. "Says: two ways. Gradually, then suddenly." He set the tape gun down. "The gradually part, I kept telling myself it would turn around." He tapped the curve's long slow shoulder, the nine visible months of it. "Wouldn't have saved the store, son. The store was dead the day Memphis bought my distributor. But I'd have liked to have had this in March. I'd have chosen some things, instead of hoping them."

At Friday dinner Elijah said it all again, chart and confession both, in front of everyone, because that was how the co-op stayed healthy and he trusted it now. There was one thing he had not said at that table, and it was not about Frank. Denny gave the verdict, and it had edges. "You of all people," he said. "You watched a man get optimized out by a spreadsheet he never saw, and you had the spreadsheet." Then, because Denny's justice always had a second half: "So fix it forward. What's the co-op do about Frank?"

What the co-op did about Frank began with an offer, and Frank sent Reuben's first draft back with questions. They settled on the inventory the tool library could use, bought at honest wholesale, not vulture pennies, and a paid job. The tool library had been running on a clipboard and an honor system. It turned out what it needed was a man who knew where every last thing was.

Then Frank hung his grandfather's bell over the tool library door. The first time it rang for a member checking out equipment, he looked up from the counter before he could help it.

The parts still came from suppliers beyond the valley. Frank still knew which ones.

Elijah put the chart he'd sat on for nine months on the shelf in the shouse, beside the broken weld coupons. He didn't throw any of it away.

* * *

That was the night she knocked.

It was late, after the Friday table had been broken down. One knock. Then the latch, and Grace was standing inside his door with her coat still on and a jar in her hand, looking like she had surprised herself.

"I didn't wait," she said.

"No."

"I brought soup. I don't know why I brought soup. You're fine."

"I'm fine."

"I know you are." She set the jar on the table. "You went and told him. Frank. You walked in there with the chart."

"You told me to."

"I tell everybody to." She sat down in Reuben's chair, on the far side of the room from the stove, and finally unbuttoned the coat. "Nobody does."

He put a split of oak in and shut the door on it. He did not go and sit by her. He sat down on the floor where he was, the way he had the first night, and after a while she picked up the chair and brought it over.

Her truck was still in the gravel in the morning. Curtis walked past it on his way to the shop and found something to look at in the other direction.

* * *

Tyler got the bench by the window. He had lost a register and gained forty hours a week, and he spent them building a computer into a steel ammo can. A seven-inch screen. A keyboard off a dead laptop. The battery from a cordless drill. It ran on nine watts, and it could talk to the Crucible from anywhere on the forty acres without touching the internet.

"What is it?" Elijah said.

"It's a cyberdeck."

Elijah had a page in an old notebook about a machine like that. Something small, something his, that ran on watts he could count on his fingers. He had never had a name for it. Here it was in an ammo can, and a twenty-year-old had the name.

Frank came over from the tool library counter to look, wiping his glasses on his apron.

"When I was his age we put a man on the moon with less computer than that," Frank said. "And every kid on my street could adjust a TV antenna."

"Frank," Denny said, from the next bench, not looking up. "I'm sorry Tyler wasn't around when they put a man on the moon. But right now he's learning the skills to put himself on the moon. He doesn't need a lecture about how kids these days can't adjust a TV antenna. He needs your help and your patience while he builds a goddamn cyberdeck." He blew dust off a lid. "You know how to solder. You know why the fuse blows. Nobody in his phone knows that."

Frank looked at Denny. He looked at the ammo can. He bent down and looked closer, and made a small unhappy noise.

"Son, these joints are cold." He folded his glasses into his apron pocket and held out his hand. "Give me the iron."

Tyler gave him the iron.
