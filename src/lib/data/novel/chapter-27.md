# Chapter 27: Ship Them

The video that did it was the first fire.

Denny had filmed the whole shouse build the summer before, the way he documented everything by then, hands in frame, the county in his vowels. A year after the first fire, he went back through the footage and made four minutes of it. The cut. The columns. Marta's bead going down the header seam like a zipper closing. The old stove with its new brick. And at the end, the draw catching on the first match, and the co-op standing around in the new warm doing nothing historic whatsoever.

He ended it the way he'd ended the first one. "Come see it. Bring your kid."

It posted on a Thursday night. Elijah did the pipes and went to bed.

A week later he was up before light, and he walked to the shed through the first hard frost of the year with a coffee, to check the overnight numbers the way other generations of men had walked out to check stock. The network was green. The greenhouse was green. The channel graph was a wall.

He'd seen spikes. The leak had been a spike. This was the other thing, the curve he'd been drawing in notebooks for four years. This time it was climbing for something he loved.

He wanted to draw a line through it and extend it. He opened the inbox. He read it standing up, with the coffee going cold.

A grange association in Idaho: how do we start. A parish council in Louisiana, two months out from the last flood. A machinist in Ohio with his father's shop and no work in it, asking for the controller plans. A tribal housing authority. A retired county engineer. A woman in Maine who had, she wrote, four acres, a dead barn, and nine neighbors who show up.

Eleven towns by the time the sun was all the way up, in nine states, every one of them asking some version of the same four words.

There was also a text on his phone, from the night before. It was from David.

<em>Saw the stove.</em>

That was all of it. He read it three times. He started to type and didn't know what. He put the phone in his pocket to answer later, when he could think, and went to find Grace.

* * *

She was already in the inbox. She'd been in it since five.

"Eleven towns," she said. She was lit up. He had never seen her like this. "Elijah. That's eleven kitchens. That's maybe a thousand people who eat."

"The route's got no money past June."

"I know."

"That inbox could pay for the route. All of it. Forever."

He heard how it sounded, and he kept going. Course packs. Plans at forty dollars. A paid tier with a call once a month. He had a number for it in about ninety seconds, and it was a good number. He and Grace built the rest of it at the kitchen table in two hours, shoulder to shoulder, her finishing his sentences.

They put it on the big screen at Friday dinner, the inbox and the number both.

Curtis liked it. "That's a product," Curtis said. "People pay real money for half of what we know."

It was Frank Kowalski who took it apart, from the end of the table, in the voice of a man doing inventory.

"I spent thirty years selling parts for two dollars over what the internet charged," Frank said. "Know what I was actually selling? Somebody who knows which part. You can't download me. Him neither." A thumb at Denny. "Fence the files and you're selling paper. Ship them free, and every one of those eleven towns needs a Frank and a Marta and a Priya of their own anyway. And they'll find them down the road, because that part doesn't ship." He folded his glasses. "The store taught me that. Late."

"It feeds people, Frank," Grace said. "That's all it's for. I've got a hundred and forty on the route and a budget that stops in June. Every winter there's somebody on that route we don't get to in time. I can tell you their names."

"I know what it's for, honey. I'm telling you what it turns into."

Elijah tried. He kept it to two sentences, because he had learned.

"Books used to be chained to desks. Then somebody made copying cheap, and I'm just saying the folks who printed them still charged for the book."

Nobody answered. He knew it was wrong as soon as he said it.

Marta had been quiet until then, which was how the co-op knew where it would land.

"I told this one his first day," she said, nodding at Elijah, "that nineteen households who know whose kid is whose don't scale. That's the load-bearing wall. I meant it. It's still true." She looked at the wall of numbers. "The wall doesn't scale. The blueprints of the wall scale fine. Ship them."

"For nothing," Elijah said.

"For nothing."

"Then who pays for June?"

"We'll see," Marta said.

It went to a show of hands. It wasn't close. Grace kept her hand down, and so did he, and so did Curtis, which was not company either of them had expected.

She found his arm under the table. They had lost, together, in front of everybody, and it had been the best week of the two years.

* * *

Getting the files ready took longer than posting them.

Priya sent her soil notes with the part they still had to repeat marked in pencil. Marta wrote on the building drawings that they were a record of one project and not a recipe for anybody's site. Elijah put the leak report in beside the wins. Denny asked everyone in the footage what they wanted left out. The folder ended up smaller than they'd pictured.

Monday morning he stood in the shed at dawn with the coffee and watched the download counter climb through four digits. People he would never meet were carrying off what the co-op had learned from its mistakes.

An email from the machinist in Ohio came in while he watched. One connector label in the controller drawing was wrong. Elijah opened the drawing, found it, fixed it, and thanked the man by name.

At Friday dinner, Denny read that message out loud before he read the view count.

That was the Friday after the midterms. Half the country had just voted for people who promised to slow the machines down, and the other half for people who promised to turn them loose.

It came to the table anyway. Patricia's network had sent every vendor a pledge to sign. No generated code, no generated text, a little green leaf you could put on your label. Tyler read it out in a flat voice. Somebody asked, lightly, who'd voted for whom. Somebody else said it was a fair question. It was the first time anyone at that table had been asked.

"Doesn't matter who's in," Grace said, passing the bowl. She was half joking. "People will vote for the fire if it's warm."

Elijah laughed.

He'd never answered David. He remembered it then, with the bowl in his hands, and it was three weeks old, and he didn't know how to start.
