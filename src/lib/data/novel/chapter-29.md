# Chapter 29: People in It

The co-op held its annual meeting in the shouse.

The shouse had the woodstove and the wall screen and, by its second winter, the unofficial status of parliament. Reuben ran the agenda. The year's numbers. The lease, and the trust's monthly check to Irene Calder. The library's download counter, fifty-one thousand and climbing, from towns in nine countries now. Frank's first full year at the tool library.

Then Elijah stood up for the last item, which was his, and which he had been building for a month, and said: "It's two years from now, and the co-op is dead. I'd like to spend two hours on how it died."

He had come loaded. Of course he had. He had every stage, every watt and every county filing, run through the Crucible against a few hundred possible futures.

Supply shocks: modeled. The Memphis platform buying their steel distributor the way it bought Frank's: modeled, with a second-source list already drafted. Grid failures, fuel spikes, a hostile county supervisor, a change in the property assessment, a landbuyer with better lawyers: modeled, modeled, modeled, modeled.

He walked the room through forty minutes of ways to die, calmly. The room took it seriously. People could follow the math and argue with it.

Then Marta said, "Your machine's got no column for the stuff that actually kills these things," and the second half of the meeting began.

"Factories don't die of the strategy," she said. "I watched mine die for a year before the spreadsheet showed up. The spreadsheet was the coroner. Things die of people, college. Run it again with people in it."

The room ran it again with people in it, and Elijah wrote down what his model had missed.

Burnout, said Priya, looking at Denny. The channel posts three times a week because one man will not stop, and if that man goes down, the lever goes down with him. Denny started to argue and then didn't, which everyone understood, including him. The fix got a name that night. Tyler learns the edit, Frank learns the camera, and the channel becomes a bench and not a pillar.

A divorce, said Reuben, flatly, into the quiet. He didn't name anyone. Nineteen households is a dozen marriages and change, and the trust documents, he confessed, handled a split about as gracefully as a wood chipper. He'd have a fix drafted by March. He had, everyone knew, already started.

Nobody looked at Elijah and Grace. It took some effort, and you could feel it going around the room.

Founder-dependence, said Frank. Every important thing in the valley ran through the same four people, and all four of them were sitting right there.

Then Marta set her cup down and put herself on the list. "Shoulder's done," she said. "Rotator cuff. Tuesdays haven't fixed it and won't. I've got maybe two years of overhead welding left, and this valley has exactly one person certified to make the beads that hold roofs up." She looked at Tyler, twenty-one years old, two years on the roster, holding his coffee like it might be repossessed. "Apprenticeship's four years if you're serious. If you want it, we start Monday."

She picked her cup back up to have something to do. It shook, a little, on the way to her mouth. Elijah saw it. He thought it was the shoulder.

"That leaves two years we haven't covered," Priya said.

Marta nodded. "Then put that on the board too. We need someone qualified to cover the gap. Starting an apprentice doesn't make one finished."

Elijah wrote <em>WELDER FOR THE GAP: UNRESOLVED,</em> and left the box open.

Grace had sat through all of it with a page in her lap. Since the first deliveries, the route had limped along on one short grant after another. Now she put the page beside Elijah's laptop. The route had money through June. After that, nothing was decided. She could show what each delivery cost and which households needed their meals cooked. She couldn't turn a promise of a grant into a paid invoice.

She didn't look at Marta or Frank.

"We could do an appeal on the channel," Denny said.

"We could," Grace said. "But your name is already under burnout."

He looked at the board, then down at his hands.

Rosa had sent a note through the kitchen because she couldn't leave work to attend. Her wrist was better. Her hours at the packing warehouse changed from one schedule to the next. The note asked whether the collection time could stay late enough for people on the afternoon shift. At the bottom she'd added: And can you tell us before the week it stops?

Curtis read that line twice. "They shouldn't have to keep wondering who's generous this month."

Reuben drew a second column. The co-op could put up part of what it had set aside for repairs. The kitchen could bring its books. The people on the route could say what it did for them and where it let them down. Together they could take a real proposal to the county, at the meeting where next year's food money got decided. None of that guaranteed a vote. It gave them something to ask for, with a price on it and people behind it.

They wrote <em>funded through June</em> in ink. The rest went underneath in pencil, with the meeting date beside it. Curtis said he'd be the one to tell the households what was promised and what wasn't. Grace asked for somebody besides Curtis to learn the route. Two hands went up.

Reuben was the last one out. He stacked the chairs, which he never did. Then he took a plastic sleeve out of his folder and laid it on the table by the laptop.

"You circled a link for me once," he said. "The outfit that bought a dairy down by your mother's. You asked me to check it."

It was one page from a county recorder, three hours south. A grant deed, September of 2024. The grantee had three letters and an LLC. The grantor was a family trust, and under it, typed, was the trustee's name.

"I checked it that week," Reuben said. "I've had this a year and a half."

Elijah looked at his own name. The stove ticked.

"I've been reading deeds a long time. I know what a man signs when his mother's in a bed. I'm not asking why you sold." He squared the sleeve to the edge of the table. "I'm asking why I had to go and look."

"Who else knows?"

"I don't carry tales. But it's a public record, and you taught half this valley how to search one." He buttoned his coat. "You modeled forty ways for us to die tonight. Marta told you what you left out."

He went out and pulled the door to behind him, carefully, the way he did everything.

That night, after everyone had gone, Elijah rebuilt the model with the room's amendments. The change that mattered was a names column. Every way to die carried two names now: who it happens to, and who is watching for it. There was one he did not enter.

He was pasting in the last of it when he understood what he was looking at.

The supply models, the watts, the stages, the names, the repair he had misunderstood and the meal they had delivered wrong: it was the notebooks. All of them. MADRONE IS NOT A DRONE, four years of stages and margins and lists, finally in the shape they'd been trying to take since 1:40 in the morning at a kitchen table. He had thought he was writing a warning. It was a manual, with the open boxes left open.

Before he went to bed he put the rebuilt sheet in the library folder, names column and all. In the morning Priya checked what an outsider could see, and it went out with the rest of what they gave away.

That week he opened the old document, the one with the title on it, and started over from the first line.

* * *

The newcomer arrived on the Saturday after the meeting, in a sensible car with two car seats in the back.

It had taken Devendra a year to come. He had spent it in a house in San Jose, reading the news.

He stood in the shed doorway, looking at the plasma table and the shadow-boarded wall. He looked ten years older than he had at Claypot. He said, "I don't know what I expected. It's smaller than the videos."

"It is," Elijah said.

"I still owe you a dinner," Devendra said.

"November."

Devendra looked at the broom leaning by the welding curtain, then back at the car. "I can help. I just need to figure out the kids."

"There's food inside. Bring them in."

"I don't want to take anything before I've…"

Elijah waited for the sentence to finish. Devendra looked away instead.

From the bench, Curtis called, "Dinner's dinner."

Elijah picked up the push broom. It was the same broom, older now, the bristles worn to a slant. He held it out.

"Swarf goes in the red bin." He tipped his head at the kitchen. "After dinner. We'll see."

The children came in holding their father's hands. One stopped to examine a yellow controller box. The other asked where they should put their coats.
