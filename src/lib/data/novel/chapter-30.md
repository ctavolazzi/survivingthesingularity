# Chapter 30: Three Minutes

The county chambers smelled like wet coats and floor wax.

Rain had followed them down the valley, and the co-op came in shaking it off. Curtis in his route jacket. Reuben with an accordion folder under one arm. Lou in a shirt with a collar, which nobody had ever seen before. Grace with a banker's box of receipts, and orange tape on the lid. Devendra with a child asleep against his shoulder and the other one counting ceiling tiles. Irene Calder came last and took the aisle seat as if she'd been assigned it.

Elijah had fourteen slides. He'd built them over three nights. Cost per meal. The ways it could fail, with names beside them. A curve of what the route could carry if the county matched what the co-op had already put up. He'd timed the talk at eleven minutes.

Lou had tried to help. On the drive down he had drawn the whole board on the back of a seed catalog, five boxes with five names, and under each name he'd written what that supervisor was scared of. One was scared of the ranchers. One was scared of a recall. The one in the middle was scared of her own staff. "Nobody up there votes for a thing," Lou said. "They vote so they don't get blamed for a thing. You figure out what each one's scared of getting blamed for. That's all politics is, man. It's five scared people and a clock." Elijah had said that made a lot of sense, and gone back to fixing the font on slide nine.

The clerk read the rules off a laminated card. Public comment, three minutes per speaker. A red light would come on with thirty seconds left.

"Three minutes," Elijah said.

Reuben slid a single page out of the folder. The proposal, costed, one side. He'd filed it with the clerk on Tuesday. Nobody had asked him to.

Item nine came up at 8:40. The supervisors had been sitting since six. One of them was eating almonds out of a paper cup below the dais where she thought nobody could see.

Elijah went first because the sign-up sheet said so. He got through the cost per meal and the funding date, through June. Then the red light came on. He watched himself skip to the last slide, which was the wrong slide, and say "so the model suggests" into the microphone. He sat down with eight minutes of the talk unsaid.

Rosa went second. She hadn't told anyone she was coming. She was still in her warehouse shirt, the wrist brace grey at the edges, and she didn't have a page.

"I pack fruit for a living," she said. "Afternoon shift. The route comes late enough that I can get it. I'm not here to say thank you, though I am thankful. I'm here because every month we don't know if it's the last month. You can't plan a week around maybe." She looked at the supervisor with the almonds. "I asked them to tell us before the week it stops. They said they would. I'm asking you the same thing."

She sat down with a minute left on the clock. Nobody else in the room had managed that.

Grace had not called her, or sent a ride, or put her name on the sheet. She sat with the banker's box on her knees and did not say anything.

The chair thanked her and turned to the staff report. The county's analyst, a tired man in a sweater vest, said the numbers were sound but the program would be an ongoing obligation, and the county didn't take on ongoing obligations for services the private sector and charities already provided. "We simply can't afford to make food free," he said.

Irene was already standing. Her chair scraped the floor loud enough for the microphone to catch it.

"My father-in-law got told the same thing," she said. "Walt's dad. Nineteen thirty-nine. The power company told him wiring out to our place didn't pay. Too far, too few of us. So the farmers along our road made a co-op and borrowed the money from the government, and they strung the wire themselves, on poles they set with a borrowed truck." She leaned on the podium. "They paid it back. Every cent. My husband was born under a bulb that his daddy's neighbors paid for. So when a man in a sweater tells me something can't be afforded, I'd like him to say who it can't be afforded by."

The almonds went down. The chair cleared her throat and said the speaker's time had expired, and Irene said she knew, she'd been watching the light, and sat.

They didn't win. Not the way Elijah had pictured it on the second night of slides. The board voted four to one to extend the route through December and to direct staff to study a permanent food line for the next budget cycle. On the steps outside, Reuben said that was how a county said <em>maybe</em> in writing. The man in the sweater vest got the study. Curtis laughed out loud at that, once, into the rain.

"We could feed every person in this county," Curtis said. "They know it. They've got the number right in front of them. And they're going to study it. You know what that is? That's greed. That's all it ever is."

"That man's not greedy," Irene said. She was doing up the top button of her coat. "He's scared of being the one who signed. The power company wasn't greedy either. They had a rule that said our road didn't pay."

"A rule's just greed somebody wrote down."

"Then it's not a law of physics, is it?" Irene got the button. "It's a choice. Somebody wrote it and somebody can write a different one. Go to the next meeting, Curtis."

Curtis opened his mouth, and shut it.

"December's six more months than June," Grace said. She was already writing it on the lid of the banker's box. "And a study has a due date."

She capped the pen. "We asked nicely," she said, to nobody. "I want that written down somewhere."

Devendra's older child wanted to know if they'd won. Devendra looked at Elijah.

"Some," Elijah said.

Curtis pulled out his phone and started the texts, one household at a time, the way he'd promised. Funded through December. Tell you more in June. He read the first one aloud to Rosa before he sent it. She read it back to him and changed <em>more</em> to <em>the rest</em>.

Elijah stood under the eave with the laptop in its sleeve.

Reuben wrote the study's due date on the tab of his folder in ink.
