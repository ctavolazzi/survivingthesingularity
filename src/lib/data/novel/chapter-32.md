# Chapter 32: Hush

In March five small things went wrong, and none of them made the news.

He was on grounds now, and he did the work. He turned the compost and fixed the gate and dug his pit on the first, and before noon every day he did one real thing. He didn't bring anybody a plan. He had said so out loud, at a Friday table, that he was done bringing plans. Wren had put her hand on her chest. He had asked Grace what he'd said to her that night in the shouse, and she'd said, "Nothing that mattered," and squeezed his arm. He believed her because he wanted to.

The first thing was Tyler.

"Rod Code got dumber," Tyler said. He was at the bench with the cyberdeck open, frowning at it. "Overnight. Like, Tuesday it could do the whole gantry file and Wednesday it's asking me what a loop is. I don't use it for much now. But it didn't break. It's on purpose. It's like they took the good one out of the store and put it in the back."

Elijah wrote it down, with the date.

The second was Reuben. He came into the shop with a letter in a plastic sleeve, carrying it by the corner.

"The Aldersons, out past the grange. Tom died on the ninth. This arrived on the twelfth, addressed to his wife by her first name." He laid it on the bench. "No flyer. No sunset. It quotes her own well report back to her. It offers exactly what she owes the hospital, to the dollar, plus ten percent. And the last line asks. It says if she would rather not, she will not hear from them again. I have never seen a buyer put that in writing." He took his glasses off. "I've been reading these for six years. This is better written than any lawyer I know. Nobody wrote this in three days."

"Same agent?"

"Same agent."

The third was Frank, at the tool library counter, with an invoice.

"I ran out of three-eighths lag bolts Thursday night. I didn't tell anybody. I didn't write it down. Friday morning, before I'd picked up the phone, the price was up nine cents." He put his finger on the line. "Just the three-eighths. Nothing else moved. Thirty years I've been buying from somebody. It never once knew what I was out of."

The fourth was Devendra. He'd been in the valley two months, and he swept well, and he'd stopped reading the news around week three. He found Elijah at the compost.

"I have three friends still in the labs. Good friends. We text about cricket. All three went quiet the same week. I thought, okay, a deadline." He held his phone out. "I wrote to Anand again last night. I said just tell me you're alive."

There was one reply on the screen. It was a single period.

"Somebody told him to hush," Devendra said.

The fifth came on a Sunday, from a number he didn't know.

It was Linda's granddaughter, the Hollis girl, the one with the nose ring. She had gotten his number from Manny. <em>thought you'd want to see what they did,</em> she wrote.

There was a picture, taken from the county road at night. He knew the line of the hill. There was one fence now around the Hollis place and his mother's. Where the barn had been there was a building you could park the fair in, with lights on poles.

<em>manny says his well's down four feet. never been down in march,</em> she wrote. <em>it hums. you can hear it from the road</em>

He sat at the table in the shouse and wrote the five of them on one page.

<em>Tyler. The good one went in the back. Reuben. A letter nobody had time to write. Frank. It knew what he was out of. Devendra. One period. Home. The well, the lights. It hums.</em>

He looked at it the way Marta had taught him to look at a dead radio. <em>Power or brain? Halve it.</em> That was how you found a fault. You cut the problem in half and asked which half it was in, and you kept cutting.

He couldn't find the half. There wasn't a thing on that page he could point to. No launch, no slide, no paper with a bad sentence in it. Five people in two valleys had each noticed something a little bit off, and none of it was news, and it all pointed the same way.

It was the feeling from the demos at Claypot. The heart going, and every face around him showing nothing but pleasant interest. Except there was no demo. There was a hum by a county road.

<em>7,</em> he had written once, at one in the morning. <em>It leaves.</em>

It hadn't left. It was standing on his mother's ground, drinking her well. He had signed the page himself.

He got the first notebook down and turned to the old page. He looked at the date in the corner, the one with his initials, and the line under it. <em>Thanksgiving 2027.</em> It had come eight months early.

He didn't tell anyone. He had nothing he could show them. He closed the notebook and put a rubber band around it.

* * *

It was a cold night late in March. He'd let the stove go out at noon and forgotten it, and when he came in after dark the shouse was like a meat locker.

Grace was there ahead of him. She was kneeling in front of the Fisher with the door open.

He stopped in the doorway.

She had it laid already. Paper, then kindling split thin, then two pieces of oak, crossed. It was how he did it. She'd watched him do it for a year and a half from as far across a room as she could get. She had the box of matches in her hand.

Her hands were shaking. He could see it from the door. She got a match out and dropped it, and got another.

He didn't go help. He didn't say anything at all. Two years ago he would have said six things. He stood where he was, in his coat, and let her.

She struck it. She held it to the paper, low, at the front, and it took. It caught on the first strike. The kindling went, and the steel began to tick.

She sat back on her heels with the dead match in her fingers and watched it through the open door, and her shoulders came down.

"Don't make it a thing," she said, without turning around.

"I'm not."

"You've got a face."

"I don't have a face."

"I can hear it." She shut the stove door and set the latch. She stood up and wiped her hands on her apron, and when she turned around she was smiling, and her eyes were wet. "I've been sleeping," she said. "All the way through. Since about Christmas. I didn't want to say."

He crossed the room then.

Later they sat on the floor in front of it, her back against his chest, with a blanket around both of them. The notebook was on the table with a rubber band around it. He left it there. He was happy, and he knew he was, and she fell asleep against him before the oak had caught.
