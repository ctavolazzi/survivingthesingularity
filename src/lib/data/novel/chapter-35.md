# Chapter 35: The Plants Look Thirsty

He found Lou at six the next morning, at the bottom of the east field.

Lou was where he always was at six, standing at the head of the lower beds with a coffee, looking over the kale. Elijah had not slept. He had lain in the shouse all night having the meeting over again and winning it.

"Hey," he said. "What the fuck was all of that?"

"Morning, college."

"I'm serious. What was that? She wouldn't even look at it. It's a price list."

Lou drank some coffee. He didn't seem surprised to see him, or surprised by the question, and he didn't answer it either. He did what he always did.

"What'd you want to happen?"

"I wanted them to say, huh, okay, how much."

"What'd you think was going to happen?"

"That. I thought that."

"Did it hurt your feelings, or did it piss you off?"

Elijah started to say it pissed him off. He stopped. Lou could always tell anyway.

"It hurt my feelings," he said. "They looked at me like I'd tracked dog shit in. I've been here three years."

"Yeah." Lou nodded slowly. He set his coffee on a fence post. "Okay. Look. There's one thing that people want more than anything else."

"Money."

They both laughed. It felt good.

"No," Lou said. "I mean, yes. Money. But money is really a symbol for something that all human beings want, which is control. People want control more than they want anything else."

Elijah didn't say anything right away. He thought about it. He thought about his notebooks, and every date he had ever written down with his initials next to it.

"You know what? Yeah," he said. "That makes sense. I can understand where you're coming from with that."

"Yeah. That's what this lady's doing. That's what all these people are doing here at the farm. And now extrapolate that out to all of mankind." Lou had started talking with his hands, which he only did when he meant it. "What you're proposing does make sense. It makes sense in the same way that having the internet made sense, or the steam engine made sense. But all the people whose identity was built on being a cowboy in the West, riding on the back of a horse? Not only do they not want to learn about the combustion engine. They don't want to learn how to drive a car either. They would rather have cars not exist. Because they're happy. They're content with the way their life is. They've worked really fucking hard for a really fucking long time, Elijah, to get where they are. To feel as comfortable as they are on the back of the horse."

"But aren't they aware that they're not always going to be able to get up in the saddle and ride?" Elijah said. "Like, their body is eventually going to get old, and they're eventually going to need help. And things change all the time. Like, the technology they use every day didn't exist even ten years ago."

"To be honest with you, man? No. They're not thinking about that. They're just trying to get through to the end of the day. The end of the week. They're trying to plan out what's going to happen next month, next year. And they don't really think much further than that."

"But that doesn't make any sense. Like, they can't look back and then extrapolate what's going to happen in the future?"

"It's not that they can't. It's that they won't." Lou shook his head. "It scares them. They don't want to. They want to imagine that everything's going to be the same, because they want to feel like they're in control of their life. And when you come in and you say, hey, everything's about to be different? Of course they're going to grill you. Of course they're going to feel unnerved and confused and scared and intimidated by that. Their whole identity is built around the illusion of control. And it is one, man. You don't control when you get hungry. You don't control when you have to pee."

"Okay, but…"

"In the same lane, my friend." Lou pointed at him with two fingers. "How much time do you spend thinking about nuclear winter?"

Elijah opened his mouth.

He took a kind of step back inside his own head. There were a hundred and fifty of those things sitting in the ground in Montana right now, with the keys in them. There had been his whole life. He had eleven notebooks about a machine and not one line about that. He never thought about it. He didn't think about it because there was nothing he could do about it, and because he wanted to sleep at night, and because he had squash to get in.

He was doing the exact same thing they were.

"I guess I see what you mean, huh," he said.

"Look."

Lou put a hand on his shoulder. It was a heavy hand, warm, with dirt in every line of it, and Elijah's eyes stung, which was embarrassing. Nobody had put a hand on his shoulder like that since his dad.

"I get what you're trying to do. I really do. Your heart is in the right place. You want people to have what they need to survive." Lou waited until Elijah looked at him. "But you don't understand your impact quite yet."

Then he put his other hand on the other shoulder. He had him square, eyes to eyes, the way you'd hold a kid who was about to run out into the road.

"And furthermore. You can't change anyone's mind."

He gave him a little shake when he said it. It wasn't rough. It went through Elijah like being rocked.

"People have to change their own minds. By accepting or rejecting the information they are given."

Elijah stood there in the man's hands and felt about nine years old. It was a relief.

"What do I do?"

Lou took his hands off. He stepped back and gave a big slow shrug, palms up.

"That's up to you, big boy."

He let his hands drop.

"Well, what are you going to do?"

Lou looked over one shoulder, at the kale. Then he looked over the other, at the long rows of squash still sitting out in the field with nobody to pick them.

"Right now? I'll probably turn on the water," he said. "The plants look thirsty."

And he walked over and turned on the water.

* * *

That night Lou had the barrel stove going behind the packing shed, with the door swung open so he could see the fire.

Elijah came down the path a little after nine. He hadn't decided to. His feet had. Lou pointed at the other lawn chair with a walnut.

They sat a while. Elijah rolled one and lit it off a stick from the stove. The fire popped. Up the field the irrigation was still ticking over.

"You've heard of mutually assured destruction, Elijah."

"Of course."

"An unbeatable threat we have all but forgotten." Lou cracked two walnuts in one fist and looked at the fire, not at him. "Our parents did drills in school. Got under their desks for a bomb that never came. Or at least, not yet." He picked the meat out with his thumbnail. "And that's always how it is, isn't it? Not yet. Not yet."

Elijah thought about Montana, and the keys.

"Politicians with their finger on the trigger," Lou said, "ready to end all life at a moment's notice." He tossed the shells in the stove. "I believe in something even more powerful."

"What could be more powerful than the nuclear bomb?"

Lou turned in his chair and looked him right in the eyes. He had very pale eyes, and the fire was in both of them.

"The man who refuses to launch it."

Elijah didn't say anything.

"Choice, my friend. We always have a choice."

"Yeah, but they'd court-martial him. They'd throw him under the prison."

"And he can choose," Lou said, "to suffer those consequences."

Then neither of them said anything for a while.

Elijah sat with it. He had filled eleven notebooks with the people who had their fingers on things. He had never once written down the name of somebody who didn't push. He didn't know any of their names. Nobody did. That was sort of the point of them.

He'd never really thought about that before.

"I believe we are living under a much more powerful force," Lou said. "A much more benevolent one. The force of mutually assured survival." He leaned forward and put another stick in. "You see it every day. If you look for them, the helpers are everywhere. All over. All of the time."

Elijah knew some things about work. He'd driven deliveries. He'd seen the word HANDS on the side of a seed box, and the number next to it.

"Yeah," he said. "Because they're getting paid to."

"Some of them, yes." Lou nodded like that was fair. "But no one pays a mother to protect her son. No one pays a father to look out for his daughter. They just do."

The fire settled. Lou watched it.

"I believe in the force of mutually assured survival," he said, "particularly because of women. Sure, most of the people in government and corporations are men. But the non-profits are dominated by women. Those who give, and ask for nothing in return." He smiled at the stove. "Who do you think feeds forty people at the grange hall every Sunday? I believe they're the ones who will hold the line when the day comes. I believe women will save us."

He tossed the last of the shells in.

"There is no more powerful force in all the cosmos than a mother protecting her young."

Elijah watched the firelight crackle across Lou's face and felt the certainty in his words. He took another drag, and, for the first time in a long time, felt a sense of peace wash over him.
