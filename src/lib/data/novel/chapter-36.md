# Chapter 36: The Fire

He told her on the Wednesday of festival week, in the packing shed, a little before midnight.

They were building route boxes by hand. A hundred and forty of them, squash and apples and a jar of something, the two of them and a roll of tape. He had been carrying Lou's two sentences around for a week. <em>You can't change anyone's mind. People have to change their own.</em> Somewhere in the orchard it had quit feeling like losing. He thought she would be glad.

"I'm letting it go," he said. "The arms. I'm not going to bring it up again."

Grace kept packing.

"Why would you do that?"

"Because they said no."

"Patricia said no. Patricia wouldn't look at it."

"That's hers to do. Lou said something to me, and I think he's right. You can't change anybody's mind, Grace. They have to change their own. All I can do is show them, and then it's theirs to pick up or not."

Her hands stopped for a second. Then she taped the box.

"The route is out of money in December."

"I know."

"Okay," she said.

That was all she said. She finished the row. When she left she did not put her hand on his arm, and he stood in the shed for a while afterward with the tape gun.

It sat between them the rest of the week. They were both too tired to pick it up.

* * *

The festival ended the way it always did, with a fire.

They had gotten it done, and he still wasn't sure how. Twenty-two people did the work of forty for nine days. The squash came in and the apples came off, and four hundred strangers ate pie in the yard. Lou's bad knee, the one that was still his, swelled up like a grapefruit. Wren fainted at the cider press on Saturday and went right back to it. Nobody at the table had said the word <em>arm</em> out loud since the meeting.

The rain came Thursday and the county opened burn season Friday. On Sunday night, when the last car from Idaho had pulled out, they lit the pile at the bottom of the east field. It had been growing there all summer, prunings and pallet wood and the old fence.

Grace built the fire.

She had asked to. She laid it the way she had laid the Fisher in March, small in the middle, and she lit it herself in front of everybody. When it went up she stepped back with her hands on her hips and grinned like a kid. Two years before, she couldn't sit within eight feet of a wood stove. Elijah watched her from the far side, through the heat. He loved her. He had not said it to her yet, and he thought he might tonight.

There was Denny's cider, which was stronger than Denny admitted. There was a guitar. By ten they were both a little drunk.

A woman found him at the edge of the light. She was from one of the towns, the one up near Redding. She had driven four hours to see the shed, and she had two kids asleep in the back of a Subaru at the top of the field, and she kept looking up there.

"You're him," she said. "From the video. The one with the list."

"That was a while ago."

"You said Thanksgiving. This year. That's a month from now." She had her arms crossed tight. "I do billing for a hospital. I sit down all day. I need you to tell me straight. Were you right?"

He looked across the fire. Grace was laughing at something Curtis had said.

He had promised her he wouldn't post it, and he hadn't. This was a person standing in front of him, asking. He thought about Lou. <em>The information they are given.</em>

"I think it's already here," he said. "I think it's been here since March. I can't prove it. If I were you I'd start learning to fix something."

She didn't say anything for a few seconds. The color went out of her face.

"Okay," she said. "Thank you for telling me."

She walked up the field. A minute later the Subaru's lights came on.

"What did you say to her?"

Grace had come around the fire. She had seen the car.

"She asked."

"She has two kids, Elijah."

"She asked me straight out. What was I supposed to do, lie to her?"

"You promised me."

"I promised I wouldn't post it. She was standing right there."

Grace took his sleeve and pulled him away from the others, out toward the dark, where the grass was wet. Behind them somebody started a song, and people laughed at how it started.

"So you'll tell a stranger that," she said. "A mother. But you won't make one phone call for a hundred and forty families. I waited all week. I thought, he'll go around them. I kept looking up the driveway for a truck."

"You wanted me to bring the arms in anyway? After they said no?"

"Yes. Once they're packing boxes, nobody sends them back. People get used to things."

"I'm not going to do that to them. It's their farm."

"People say no to all kinds of things that would save them. And they ask for all kinds of things that hurt them."

"That's not ours to decide."

"You don't fucking get it."

She said it loud. It went out over the field. Nobody at the fire turned around.

"Millions of people die every single year. We have to save them." She pressed her fist against her own chest, hard. "I have to save them."

"But if they want to do what they're doing, they should be able to do that. It's their choice, not yours."

"I wish it was their choice. I wish it wasn't mine. I wish I had more control. I wish I could make a choice." There was venom in it now. He had never heard that in her, in either voice. "I wish I could choose not to care like you."

It landed in his stomach.

"I do care. I care deeply."

"No, you fucking don't. You don't care, because otherwise you'd be doing more. How can you sit back and let this happen? We have to do something, Elijah."

"No, we don't."

He heard how it sounded the second it was out. He meant that he wished they would choose better, every one of them, and that it was still theirs to choose. What she heard was a man saying <em>let them die.</em> He could see her hear it.

"We can give them information," he said. "We can try to educate them. We can show them how we live here."

"How we live, Elijah? You mean like how I showed you how to do this?"

"Yes."

"You chose to go with us. You chose to do this. I thought you were one of us." Her voice cracked. "Turns out you're just like the rest of them."

"Grace. Please. Come on, listen to me."

"No. I don't want to hear another fucking word."

"Come on. That's not what I mean. I don't mean I want them to die."

"But you're okay allowing them to kill themselves? You're okay allowing them to continue to poison the planet, destroy the resources, kill every living being? You're okay with all of that?"

"That's not what I'm saying."

"Well, then what are you saying?"

She wasn't shouting anymore. She was pleading. She wanted him to have an answer. He could see that she would have taken almost anything.

He had only the one.

"I'm saying that it's not our responsibility to choose for them."

"Yes, it is."

He looked at her. Her fist was still against her chest. He thought he saw it then, the whole shape of it.

"Grace," he said. He said it quietly. "This is the gate."

She went still.

"The man at the end of the road. He told you. <em>My house, my choice.</em> You couldn't have made him. That wasn't yours either."

It was the kindest thing he had.

She took a step back from it.

"Don't." Her hand came up between them, flat. "Don't you bring him out here. I told you that at your stove."

"I'm not…"

"You are. You kept it, and now you get it out." She was looking at his hands. "You do that. You called me the lamp. Did you know that? On your floor, with your head in my hands. <em>You're just the lamp.</em> You don't even remember saying it."

He didn't.

"You signed your mother's house away and she couldn't tell you yes or no, and you've been sick over it for three years. So now nobody gets to choose for anybody. And now you're taking out your mother trauma on me!"

She growled, low in her throat, and shook both hands up by her chin.

Elijah was speechless. His heart sank and fell out the bottom of his gut.

Here was the woman he trusted most. In one instant all of that trust was utterly shattered.

They looked at each other. She was crying, and there was disgust in her face, and rage, and he just stood there. He loved her so much.

She didn't wait for him.

"Look. I thought you were one of us."

"I am."

"No, you're not."

She shook her head.

"No, you're not."

She looked away, at the dark field, at nothing.

"How could I be so stupid?"

"Wait. Listen."

"I can't believe I did this again. I can't believe this happened again."

He knew what <em>again</em> was. A gate with a strip of tape on it. A good man in a kitchen saying <em>you can't make them leave.</em> She had found him in a video and written to him and offered him gas money home, and he was the captain. He had been the captain all along.

He grabbed her arm.

"Grace."

She recoiled. She pulled away so hard she stumbled. In three years she had put her hand on his arm a thousand times, and on everybody's, and he had never once reached for hers.

"Don't touch me."

They stood there. Each of them was waiting for the other one to cross it.

His hands had come up by themselves, open, his elbows at his sides, palms toward her. <em>I don't mean you any harm.</em> He held them there. He waited for her to say something. Anything.

Slowly, he let them drop.

"I thought you were one of us…" she said, her head slowly descending with her heart.

Everything inside him wanted to reach out his hand to her, but he didn't. He couldn't.

"I…did too." Elijah's voice was barely a whisper.

She turned her body away from him and took a step toward the fire. Then another. His heart broke with every pace.

She looked over her shoulder, but only halfway, as she continued walking back toward the fire. Her eyes were wet, and beautiful.

"Maybe you should go home," she said.

<em>This is my home,</em> he thought, watching her silhouette grow smaller and smaller.
