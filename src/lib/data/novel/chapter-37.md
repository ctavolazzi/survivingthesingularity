# Chapter 37: Go Home

Nobody asked him to leave. He kept waiting for somebody to.

He gave it a week. He turned the compost. He fixed a drip line. On the second day he saw Grace across the yard, loading the trailer, and she saw him, and neither of them lifted a hand. At Friday dinner there was a place for him and people passed him things. He could have stayed. He understood that completely. They'd have fed him every week until he died, across a plywood table from her, and been kind about it.

On Tuesday he packed.

It took a morning. A bag of clothes. The laptop. A milk crate of notebooks, eleven of them now, and the manuscript, which was three hundred pages and had a title. The broken coupons, in a coffee can. His mother's pot. Everything he owned fit in the bed of the Ranger with room left over. There was room for the Fisher, too. It had come north in that bed.

He stood in front of it for a long time. It was his grandmother's, and it was the last thing of hers he had. He didn't want to leave it. But it heated a room where other people ate now, and held their meetings, and in a few weeks there would be a Thanksgiving in front of it.

He put two pieces of oak in and shut the door and set the damper low, so it would last.

Devendra was on the step when he came out. He had heard. Everybody had heard.

"There's a lease in the folder on the table," Elijah said. "Reuben can change the name. It's a roof. Put the kids in it."

"I can't take your house."

"It's not a house. It's a shop set up to keep the person running it alive." He handed him the key. "Keep the stove going."

Devendra turned the key over a couple of times.

"I owe you a dinner this month."

"Cook it anyway."

* * *

He didn't say goodbye to Grace. She was on the route. It was Tuesday.

Marta was in the shop. She had his newest coupon in the big bench vise already, with the pipe slipped over the end. He had dropped it on her bench the week before the fire and forgotten it.

"You going to make me do this by myself?" she said.

She couldn't pull it anymore. The shoulder was done. She stepped back and nodded at Tyler, and Tyler set his feet and took the pipe in both hands and hauled.

The coupon bent. It folded over on itself like a hairpin, slowly, and the steel on either side of the weld stretched and went dull, and the bead held. It didn't crack. It didn't sparkle. There wasn't a line where his metal stopped and the other began.

Tyler let go. Nobody said anything.

"Huh," Marta said.

She loosened the vise and took the piece out and looked at it close, on both sides, through the bottom of her glasses.

"It became the steel."

She held it out to him. He took it. It was still a little warm from bending.

"I'll come back up," he said. "For the pit. First of the month."

"We'll see."

She pulled her glove off, finger by finger, and put her bare hand out, and he shook it. It was the first time she had.

"Go on, Elijah," she said.

She had never called him that, not once in three years. He got out of the shop fast.

* * *

Curtis was in the gravel with the hood of the Ranger up. Nobody had asked him to. He had the dipstick out and was squinting at it.

"You're a quart low. I put it in." He dropped the hood and wiped his hands. "You've still got my hose."

"It's in the glove box."

"I know where it is. Keep it."

Denny was on his porch with a coffee. His phone was in his shirt pocket and it stayed there. He came down the steps and hugged him, hard, the kind that knocks your wind out.

"You know where the porch is," Denny said.

Priya was at the beds. She didn't stop what she was doing. She reached into her apron and handed him a paper sample bag and a pencil, a new one, sharpened.

"Dig one at home. Tell me what you find at thirty."

Frank rang the bell over the tool library door, once, with his finger. He didn't come out.

Lou was down at the valve at the bottom of the east field. He didn't come up either. He lifted the hose over his head with one hand, like a man holding up a trophy, and Elijah lifted his hand back. He had wanted to ask him one more question. He couldn't think what it was.

Tyler walked him to the truck, with the cyberdeck under his arm, trying to think of what you say.

"You were right, you know," he said finally. "About all of it. The whole list. I looked at the old video."

Elijah opened the door.

"I'd rather have been wrong," Elijah said.

Tyler stood there, twenty-one years old, in a welding jacket that was still too big for him. He didn't know what to say to that.

At the gate Elijah stopped the truck.

The old strip of flagging tape was on the post, where it had been the first night, faded almost white. Next to it there was a new one. It was bright orange, tied in the same knot, and the ends were still stiff. It hadn't been there yesterday.

She had come by. Before the route, in the dark. She hadn't knocked.

<em>Somebody checked.</em>

He sat there with the engine running and cried. When he could see the road, he drove.

* * *

He drove south with the windows down. The truck rode high and light, with nothing in the back to hold it.

He took the back way in. He slowed at the madrones and didn't stop. The bark had finished peeling for the year. They stood there on the uphill side, smooth and red in the last of the sun.

Below the road the place had a new fence around it and lights on poles. He kept his eyes on the road for that part.

The rose garden was on the east side of town. Nobody was sitting in it.

There was a book on the front desk for visitors, with a pen on a chain. He wrote his name and the time. Then he turned back a page, and another. He had not meant to.

There was a name on every Sunday. <em>D. McGrath, 10:15.</em> It went back like that two and a half years, in the same blue pen. Before that the hand changed. <em>R. McGrath.</em> It was an old man's writing, and it leaned. That one went back to the first November.

He looked for his own. It was in there a few times a year.

He let the pen go on its chain.

She was in the chair by the window. The radio was going on the sill. Next to it there was a lamp. A little one, with a linen shade.

He knew it before it spoke.

"Welcome back," the lamp said, in a pleasant voice. "You must be Barry. She asks for you."

His mother turned her head. She looked at him, and then at the door behind him.

"The church ladies brought that." It was the night aide, the big quiet one with the glasses on a chain. She was doing the bed. "For her birthday. It talks to her at night. She likes it."

He knew what was on the other end of that cord. He had smoked a cigarette with the kid who made it.

He looked at his mother. She had worried for seventy years. The well, the bank, the Hendersons' chimney. Nobody else could wash the pot. Now two girls lifted her in the morning and somebody else did the dishes for a hundred people, and she sat in the sun with the radio going. Three years, and he was still not used to it: his mother sitting down, and not getting up again for something.

It was her room.

He left the lamp alone.

He pulled the other chair over.

"Barry?" she said.

For three years he had told her there wasn't one.

"Not yet, Ma."

She patted his hand.

"It's chicken tonight," the aide said. "I can have them send two."

He said two would be fine.

He slept that night at Manny's, in the back room, with his boots by the door. Manny had offered it once, three years back, through a truck window, in four words. It turned out the offer was still good. His was the one fence left on that road without a light on it.

In the morning he fried two eggs in his mother's pot, because it was the only pan he owned. He washed it himself, and set it in Manny's rack.
