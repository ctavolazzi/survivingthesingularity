# Chapter 5: The First Watt

The envelope was thin, which fooled him.

Elijah's power bills had always been the boring kind of mail: forty-something dollars, autopay, straight into the recycling. This one he opened over the kitchen sink out of pure Monday inertia, and then he stood there with the tap running, because the number at the bottom said $211.46.

His first thought was that it was a mistake. His second, arriving with the slow heat of a blush, was the truth. The meter hadn't lied.

One month. One month of training runs on the tower, of tests left running overnight, of two GPUs breathing like sled dogs under his desk. A hundred and seventy dollars of difference between a man who thinks about machine intelligence and a man who runs it, printed on paper and mailed to his door like a grade.

He sat down at the kitchen table with the bill and the notebook.

After the tower cooked itself, he'd written down the wall he'd hit: heat. He'd understood it then the way you understand a headline. Now he was holding the invoice.

Intelligence wasn't magic. Intelligence was energy, arranged. Every word the machine produced was a little puff of waste heat over a server rack somewhere. Every clever thing it said had a wattage. And every clever thing he said did too: about a hundred watts, day and night, the output of one warm light bulb, fueled by groceries.

He wrote the two numbers side by side. The data center, in gigawatts. Him, a hundred watts.

That week he became insufferable in a brand-new way. He bought a plug-in power meter, a little gray box, fourteen dollars, and went through the apartment like an auditor with a grudge.

The television, off, sipping eleven watts around the clock for the privilege of turning on two seconds faster. The cable box, twenty-three watts, doing nothing, a tenant who paid no rent. The microwave clock. The laser printer he used four times a year.

He filled two notebook pages with it. He had been leaking energy and money in a fine mist, everywhere, all the time.

Then he did the other math. The tower drew 600 watts flat out to run a model that could think alongside him. His phone drew five to make him dumber. Somewhere between those two numbers there was a machine worth owning. Something small, something his, something that ran on watts he could count on his fingers and served nobody's meter but his own.

* * *

He was on his second notebook by then. He took both of them to the Bear Flag on a Wednesday and put them on the bar in front of David.

"Read it," he said. "All of it. Then tell me I'm wrong."

David looked at the notebooks, and at the two guys waiting on pitchers, and at Elijah. He poured the pitchers. Then he came back, put on the drugstore glasses he kept by the register, and read. He read the stages. He read the thistle and the satellite and the ledger of the cable box. It took him forty minutes, between customers, and he didn't skip. Nobody else had read every page. Devendra had read three and said "interesting."

David took the glasses off.

"It's a lot of work, Mad."

"That's not an answer."

"I know."

Ray McGrath had come down from his stool for a refill. He stood with his club soda and looked at the open page for a moment, at the tight columns, the underlines, the dates with initials next to them.

"I had one of those," Ray said. "Mine was horses."

David winced. Ray went back to his stool. Elijah knew about the horses. Everybody in that bar did. He was annoyed that the comparison had been made at all. He was sleeping about four hours a night. There were dishes in his sink from Sunday. He would have told you he was being thorough.

* * *

On Sunday he drove out to his mother's and went into the barn, where his grandmother's things had been sitting under tarps since the funeral, and he came back with the woodstove.

It was a squat black Fisher, older than he was, four hundred pounds of plate steel with a crack in one firebrick. Manny had the next place over and had been fixing things on theirs since Elijah's father got sick. The two of them got it up one flight of stairs with an appliance dolly from the rental place. He had no fireplace and no flue and no permission from his landlord, so it sat in the corner of the living room as a monument, which was the point.

His grandmother had heated a whole farmhouse with it. She cooked on it, dried socks over it, kept lambs alive behind it in bad Februaries, all on wood she'd bucked herself from her own downed oaks. Total utility bill, as far as he'd ever known: a chainsaw's gas and her own hundred watts.

For a minute he wanted to count the cost as nothing but fuel and muscle. Then he remembered the borrowed trailer, the neighbor who helped when her shoulder went, the business of keeping a chimney clear. There had been a whole small arrangement around that stove. His grandmother knew what it needed and who she could call.

He put his hand flat on the cold steel the way she used to put his hand on the madrones, and left it there a while.

He unplugged the cable box on his way to bed. Twenty-three watts. They were the first ones he ever took back.
