# Chapter 9: The Architect

The house was on a hill, and every window in it was lit.

A man took Elijah's jacket at the door before he could say he'd keep it. Ruby kept his hoodie. Past the hall there was a dining room with a table long enough to land a plane on, and about twenty people along it, candles, wine, a wall of glass with the bridge in it. Elijah knew four of the faces from slides. The rest just looked like people who owned things.

There was one empty chair, near the head, with a full place setting in front of it.

"Not yours," Ruby said, and steered him past by the elbow. "That's hers. They set it every time. She's never sat in it."

"Whose?"

"The Architect doesn't do tables."

The kitchen was bigger than Elijah's apartment. Four caterers in black stood along the counter with nothing to do, because a small woman in a gray cardigan was at their sink, washing their dishes.

She was near seventy. Her sleeves were pushed up past the elbow and she had reading glasses on a cord. She washed each plate, rinsed it, held it up to the light over the sink, and looked at it front and back before she set it in the rack. She didn't turn around when they came in.

Elijah knew the back of her head. It was grayer now. He'd watched it at a whiteboard about thirty times.

"I brought you one," Ruby said. He'd lifted a little tart off a tray on the way through and was eating it with his fingers, leaning in the doorway. "The guy with the list."

"I know who he is."

"She makes the thing," Ruby told Elijah, with his mouth full. "Then I sell it back to lonely people for twenty-nine a month. It's a great business. She hates it."

"I do not hate it. It is what I said it would do." She held a plate to the light. "You are the one I said it to."

Ruby grinned. He never came any farther into the room than the doorway.

"You have the notebook with you," she said. It wasn't a question. "Show me."

He got it out of his bag and held it out to her. She lifted her hands an inch out of the water to show him they were wet, and went back to the plate. So he opened it on the counter beside the sink, found the page, and held it flat. She read with her head tilted, through the bottom of her glasses, and kept washing.

There was a towel folded over the oven door. He was his mother's son. He picked it up and started on the rack.

"One through five are correct," she said. "The order may change. Turn."

He turned the page with his dry hand.

"Six. Yes. Seven." She read the line he'd drawn across the page and the two sentences under it. <em>Past Stage 7 I don't have paper big enough. Nobody does.</em> "Here you stopped."

"I tried to go further. I had a spreadsheet. It was pretty dumb."

"It was not dumb to stop. Most of them do not."

"Most of who?"

She handed him a plate without looking. "Every year, someone like you finds me. One year it was a nurse. One year it was a man who drove trucks. There is always a notebook. It is the same list in different handwriting."

He stood there holding the plate.

"What do you tell them?"

"That they are correct." She rinsed a glass. "It has never once helped."

"He choked today," Ruby said from the door. "On stage. Guy asked him what AGI was and he said 'most things.'"

"Then say it now."

Elijah's ears went hot. But it was there this time, the whole chord.

"A machine that can do any work a person can do sitting down."

"And the second."

"ASI. It does that work better than all of us put together. Including building the next machine."

"Yes. And the third word. It is on the banner at that event. It is in the mouth of every person at that table. Not one of them could give it to you plainly."

"The singularity."

"It is borrowed from physics. It is the place in an equation where the numbers run to infinity and the mathematics stops telling you anything. That is all. We needed a name for the day after the second machine, and we took that one. From where you and I are standing, you cannot see past it. It does not mean heaven. It does not mean the end. It means the place where the forecast stops." She glanced at the notebook. "You wrote it yourself. You ran out of paper."

Elijah dried the plate and set it on the stack. He'd driven down that morning with one question, and it wasn't on any slide.

"Do we survive it?"

"That is the wrong verb."

She let it sit while she did a serving dish.

"Nothing goes through a singularity and comes out the same thing. That is what makes it one. Ask a seed whether it survived. Something comes out the other side. It will have come from us. I would like it to remember you. I spend some of my time on that."

The caterers had quit pretending not to listen.

"Are you afraid of it?" Elijah said.

"I said once, in a lecture, that I was afraid of us."

"I saw it."

"I was being polite." Plate to the light. "I am afraid of the part where it is kind."

"Why?"

"Because you will let it."

She turned her head and looked straight at him for the first time. Her eyes were pale and she didn't blink enough.

"A cruel machine you would fight. You are good at that, as a species. A kind one will ask what is wrong. It will be right about what is wrong. It will bring you what you need before you have finished needing it. And one day you will notice that you have not got up out of the chair in a long time, and that you do not mind." She tipped her head, a little, toward the doorway. "He sells the lamp already. People pay him."

"Twenty-nine a month," Ruby said. He'd stopped eating.

Elijah thought about Wes. <em>is my stuff in that thing.</em>

"Did you ask anybody?" he said. "Before you built it."

Her hands stopped in the water.

"My friend makes flyers. For our band. Seventeen years of his stuff went into one of these and nobody asked him. These realtors I know let three people go, and nobody asked those three either. And I didn't ask. I helped build one. I'm not, I'm just wondering if anybody ever did. At any point."

The kitchen was very quiet. Across the room a dishwasher finished its cycle and clicked.

"No," she said.

She started washing again, slower.

"We said there was no one to ask. That was convenient. The ones who find me ask how long. They ask whether to buy land. They do not ask that."

"I'm sorry. I didn't mean to…"

"Do not apologize for the one good question." She rinsed the last glass. "It is the only part of the work I still do. The rest I have given to people who are younger and faster than I am. I work on whether it asks first."

"Asks what?"

"Anything. Before it does a thing to you, or for you. A machine that capable does not need your permission. I am trying to make one that wants it." She pulled the plug and watched the water go down. "I do not yet know how."

He had to ask. Everybody did.

"How long?"

She turned off the tap.

"The dishes are done."

She held out her hand. He thought she wanted the towel, and gave it to her, and she dried her hands with it one finger at a time. Then she held her hand out again. He understood that time. He gave her his pen.

She bent over the notebook. Beside <em>Nobody does,</em> in small upright letters, she wrote one word.

<em>Correct.</em>

She capped the pen, laid it in the fold of the book, and went out the back door of the kitchen, the one the caterers used, without saying goodnight to anybody. Through the window he saw a small figure pass under the garden light and start down the hill on foot.

"She liked you," Ruby said. "The trucker cried. She didn't like that."

* * *

They didn't stay for dinner. The man at the door had Elijah's jacket ready before he asked for it.

On the front steps a big man in a camel coat came down past them, talking over his shoulder to someone with a tablet. He put an empty glass in Elijah's hand without looking at him.

"Is the car here?"

"I don't work here," Elijah said.

"Nobody does anymore," the man said, and kept going.

Elijah stood on the steps holding the glass.

"You know who that was?" Ruby said.

"No."

"Good. Keep it that way."

He left the glass on a planter. In the back of the car he held the notebook shut on his knee with both hands, like it might open by itself.

He had a document at home that he'd started at 1:40 in the morning, fifteen months back, under a title he'd typed just so the page wouldn't be empty. SURVIVING THE FINAL TURNING. He'd never liked the last two words. They belonged to Nate's paperback.

He changed them in his head, somewhere going down the hill, to her word.

She'd told him the verb was wrong. He thought about that for a few blocks. He kept the verb. It was the one everybody he loved would use.

Ruby was looking out the other window, chewing the side of his thumb.

"You smoke?" he said.

"I quit."

"Sure," Ruby said.
